\section{Starting with motion}

Rub two balloons with a wool cloth and
bring them near each other. They can
repel: one moves away from the other.
This visible motion is our starting
observation. Its microscopic explanation
comes later.

Imagine an idealized experiment with
balloons A and B. Hold A still and
release B from rest. Film B beside a
ruler, recording its position at
successive times. Suppose B can move
horizontally without friction. Ignore
air resistance, and treat the balloons
as small compared with their separation.
Other forces balance vertically.

These are simplifying assumptions,
not instructions for a precision
experiment with real balloons.

\section{From motion to force}

Suppose B has mass $1$ gram. During
the first $0.1$ seconds, it moves
$5$ millimetres away from A.
For a sufficiently short interval,
approximate its acceleration as constant.
If $s$ is the distance travelled and
$t$ is elapsed time, starting from rest
gives
$$
s=\frac12 at^2.
$$
Acceleration measures how quickly
velocity changes. Our recorded motion
therefore gives
$$
a=\frac{2s}{t^2}
=\frac{2(0.005\,\mathrm m)}
{(0.1\,\mathrm s)^2}
=1\,\mathrm{m/s^2}.
$$
Newton's law converts this acceleration
and the measured mass into a net force:
$$
F=ma
=(0.001\,\mathrm{kg})
(1\,\mathrm{m/s^2})
=0.001\,\mathrm N.
$$

The newton, written $\mathrm N$, is
a unit of force. One newton gives a
one-kilogram mass an acceleration of one
metre per second squared. Under our
assumptions, the horizontal force we
measured is electrical repulsion.

Motion alone is insufficient: we also
need the mass. The same force gives a
larger acceleration to a smaller mass.

\section{Choosing a unit of charge}

Electric charge is a measurable property
of a body that characterizes its
participation in electrical attraction
and repulsion. Its magnitude measures
the strength of that interaction when
compared using the same reference body
at the same distance. We write this
quantity as $q$.

A unit is a chosen reference amount.
Measuring a quantity means asking how
many copies of that reference amount
it contains.

For our toy experiment, declare that
the charge B currently carries counts
as one balloon-unit of charge.
Choose its sign to be positive.
This is an invented unit, not a
standard scientific unit. It refers
to B's present state, not to every
balloon or to B at all later times.

Keep A unchanged. Replace B with C
at precisely the same starting distance.
Measure C's mass and initial acceleration
and compute the force as before.
If the repulsive force is twice as
large, assign C two balloon-units of
charge. Three times the force means
three balloon-units.

This comparison uses the experimentally
established proportionality between
force and charge in our idealized
setting. It is not a consequence of
Newton's law alone.

The standard unit, the coulomb, written
$\mathrm C$, supplies a shared reference
amount so different laboratories can
report comparable charges. It does not
mean the charge of one balloon or one
charged particle. Converting our
balloon-unit into coulombs would require
a separate calibration; the toy
experiment alone has not supplied it.

\section{Enter charge}

Using our balloon-unit, B has numerical
charge $q_B=+1$. If C experiences twice
B's repulsive force, assign $q_C=+2$.
If it experiences twice the force in
the opposite direction, attracting A,
assign $q_C=-2$.

We are still using an idealized model
of small charged bodies. Polarization,
which can complicate real balloon
experiments, is left for later.

Experiments reveal two kinds of charge,
called positive and negative. Like
signs repel and opposite signs attract
in this model. Choosing which kind
to call positive is a convention.

Charge belongs to the body's electrical
state; force describes its interaction
with another body. Move C farther
from A: the force becomes weaker,
even though C's charge can remain
unchanged.

For two stationary point charges in
vacuum, Coulomb's law gives the force
magnitude:
$$
F=k\frac{|q_Aq_C|}{r^2},
\qquad
k=\frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0}.
$$
Here $r$ is their separation; the
expression for $k$ uses SI units.
The signs determine whether the force
attracts or repels.

\begin{itemize}
\item Double either charge, keeping
everything else fixed: double the force.
\item Double both charges: quadruple
the force.
\item Double their separation: divide
the force by four.
\end{itemize}

Our force comparisons kept A and the
initial distance fixed. Consequently,
$$
\frac{F_C}{F_B}
=\frac{|q_C|}{|q_B|}.
$$
We have reached $q$ without introducing
electrons: observe motion, infer force,
compare interactions, assign charge.
The inverse-square dependence is an
experimental law, beyond the choice
of units.

See \cite[Vol. II, Section 1-1]
{feynman-lectures} for electrical forces
and Coulomb's law.

\section{The route to data}

We begin with charged bodies and their
observable interactions. The route then
passes through fields, voltage, current,
circuits, transistors, logic, memory,
and encoding.

Electrons enter when we ask what carries
charge inside a metal. For this journey,
their microscopic nature and origin
remain a black box. We use the empirical
fact that each electron carries the same
negative charge, written $q=-e$, with
$e>0$.

A bit represents a choice between two
distinguishable physical states. Later
sections explain how a circuit makes
those states and how another circuit
reads them.

\section{Electric fields and voltage}

A small stationary test charge $q$ feels
a force $\mathbf{F}$. Keeping the other
charges fixed, define the electric field
by
$$
\mathbf{E}=\frac{\mathbf{F}}{q}.
$$
The electric potential difference
$\Delta V$ measures the change in
potential energy per unit charge in an
electrostatic field,
$$
\Delta U=q\Delta V.
$$
The cell, switch and bulb experiment in
\href{https://www.heap-project.org/%
tag/0HHL}{Electromagnetism}
gives these quantities a circuit to
describe.

\section{Current and elementary circuits}

Current measures charge flow:
$$
I=\frac{dQ}{dt}.
$$
In metals, mobile electrons carry current.
In other materials, ions or other
carriers can transport charge. Current
conventionally points in the direction
positive charges would flow.

A resistor limits current. An ideal
ohmic resistor obeys
$$
V=IR.
$$
A capacitor stores separated charge
and energy in an electric field:
$$
Q=CV,\qquad U=\frac12 CV^2.
$$

Unlike a resistor, a capacitor can
temporarily retain a charge state.

\section{Semiconductors and transistors}

A metal conducts because it has mobile
electronic states. Semiconductors have
electrical behaviour that can be
controlled by doping and electric fields.

A field-effect transistor uses the
voltage on its gate to control conduction
between two other terminals. Networks
of transistors can be designed to
produce robust high or low output
voltages.

\section{From voltage to Boolean logic}
\label{section-inverter}

Digital electronics interprets one
voltage range as $0$ and another as $1$.
The exact ranges depend on the circuit.
These symbols are a convention for
physical states.

Consider a CMOS inverter. Its output is
connected to the positive supply through
a p-channel transistor and to ground
through an n-channel transistor. Both
gates receive the input voltage. With
the input at ground the upper transistor
conducts and charges the output toward
the supply voltage. With the input at
the supply voltage the lower transistor
conducts and discharges the output toward
ground. The next gate reads the resulting
voltage range. Calling low zero and high
one makes this circuit implement negation.

Transistor networks implement Boolean
operations. An AND gate outputs $1$
exactly when both inputs are $1$:
$$
0\land0=0,\quad 0\land1=0,
\quad 1\land0=0,\quad 1\land1=1.
$$

Gates compose into arithmetic circuits,
control circuits, and processors.
The important bridge is not that
electricity somehow turns into
mathematics: engineered circuits
physically realize logical operations.

\section{Where a bit is stored}

In dynamic RAM, a bit is represented
by charge stored in a tiny capacitor.
The charge leaks and must be refreshed.

In static RAM, connected transistors
maintain one of two stable states
while powered.

Flash memory in an SSD retains
information through charge stored in
special transistor structures. That
charge changes the electrical response
when the cell is read and can persist
without electrical power.

Bits encode letters, numbers, images,
and instructions only through agreed
representations and interpretation.
A pattern has no unique meaning by
itself.

For one worked example, 64 bits read as
the number $\sin 60^\circ$ and a whole
moving surface stored as about 1.36
million bits, see
\href{https://www.heap-project.org/%
tag/0HM9}{How wires carry one moving
surface} in Computing Systems.

\section{From bits to light}

Display electronics interpret pixel
values and drive physical subpixels.
LCDs regulate transmitted backlight;
OLEDs emit light when electrically
driven. Thus visible images arise
through another engineered conversion
of electrical signals.

\section{Questions guiding our study}

\begin{itemize}
\item Why do metals conduct electricity?
\item How does voltage arise in a battery?
\item What happens inside a transistor?
\item How does a NAND gate implement logic?
\item How does DRAM preserve and refresh
a bit, and why does RAM need power?
\item How does flash memory keep data
when the laptop is turned off?
\item How do stored bits represent a
character or an image?
\item How does the physics of information
relate to entropy?
\end{itemize}
