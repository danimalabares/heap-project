\section{Four model equations}
\label{section-introduction}

\noindent
The first four equations on Salsa and
Verzini's list answer four questions.
What happens to a
profile carried by a current? To a hot spot?
To a plucked string? What temperature
profile persists when the heat entering
each small region balances the heat
leaving it? Here are the
four equations, in their order in
\cite[Section 1.2, (1.2)--(1.5)]%
{salsa-verzini}.

Write $x$ for position and $t$ for time.
The unknown $u$ is a function of these
variables. In Euclidean coordinates set
$$
\Delta u=\sum_{j=1}^n
\frac{\partial^2 u}{\partial x_j^2}.
$$
Consider first a ring with angular
coordinate $x$, so $\Delta u=u_{xx}$.
We can try the same initial profile
$u(x,0)=\cos x$ in three different laws.

\begin{enumerate}
\item \emph{Transport.} Material moving at
constant velocity $b$ carries its profile
with it. A particle starting at $a$ is
at $a+bt$ at time $t$. The derivative
of $u(a+bt,t)$ is $u_t+bu_x$, so
$$
u_t+bu_x=0,\qquad u(x,t)=\cos(x-bt).
$$
The maximum travels around the ring.

\item \emph{Heat.} Take the diffusivity
to be one. At a peak of the cosine the
second derivative is negative, so the
temperature there starts to drop. In fact
$$
u_t=\Delta u,\qquad u(x,t)=e^{-t}\cos x.
$$
The peak stays put and loses amplitude.
This is the model parabolic equation.

\item \emph{Waves.} Take wave speed one
and release the string from rest. Now the
curvature of the profile determines its
acceleration. Since the time derivative
is initially zero, the solution is
$$
u_{tt}=\Delta u,\qquad u(x,t)=\cos t\cos x.
$$
At $t=\pi$ the initial peak has become a
trough of the same depth. This is the
model second-order hyperbolic equation.
We had to specify an initial velocity as
well as an initial displacement.

\item \emph{Laplace and Poisson.} A
time-independent temperature in a region
with no internal heat source satisfies
$$
\Delta u=0.
$$
On an interval this says $u''=0$.
Temperatures prescribed at the two ends
select the straight line joining them.
In higher dimensions this is the model
elliptic boundary value problem. With a
source $f$ we write Poisson's equation
as $-\Delta u=f$.
\end{enumerate}

All four operators are linear. If $P$ is
one of them and $Pu=f$, $Pv=g$, then
$$
P(au+bv)=af+bg
$$
for constants $a,b$. Other linear examples
in the same list are Black--Scholes for an
option price, the vibrating plate, and
Schr\"odinger's equation for a complex
wave function. In Burgers' equation the
velocity is the solution itself. The term
$uu_x$ breaks this superposition rule.
Fisher's equation adds population growth
to diffusion. In the porous medium equation
the density controls its own diffusivity.
The minimal surface equation governs the
height of a stationary area graph, and the
eikonal equation governs arrival times.
The list ends with systems for elastic
displacements, electromagnetic fields,
and incompressible fluid motion (Navier,
Maxwell, and Navier--Stokes).

For each model one must specify the data
and ask whether a solution exists, whether
it is unique, and how much it changes when
the data change. These are the questions
of well-posedness
\cite[Section 1.3]{salsa-verzini}.
The \href{https://www.heap-project.org/%
download/slides/kodaira-lectures/%
lecture-24.pdf}{Kodaira lectures} posed
just such a problem. The datum was a
closed form $f$.
We wanted a primitive $u$ with
$\bar\partial u=f$ and a bound on its
size. First consider how a differential
equation already arose in geometry.

\section{From a geodesic to a field}
\label{section-geodesic-field}

A free particle moves on a Riemannian
manifold $(M,g)$. Its position is a curve
$\gamma(t)$. The metric measures the
squared speed, so its action for unit mass
is the integral of the kinetic energy,
$$
E(\gamma)=\frac12\int_a^b
g_{\gamma(t)}(\dot\gamma,\dot\gamma)\,dt.
$$
The fixed-endpoint variation comes from
Lagrangian mechanics in the Physics part
of the Heap Project. See
\emph{Classical mechanics}, Section
\ref{classical-mechanics-section-action}.

In coordinates write $q^i=\gamma^i(t)$
and sum over repeated indices. The
Lagrangian and its two derivatives are
$$
\begin{aligned}
L(q,v)&=\tfrac12 g_{ij}(q)v^iv^j,\\
L_{v^k}&=g_{kj}v^j,\\
L_{q^k}&=\tfrac12(\partial_k g_{ij})v^iv^j.
\end{aligned}
$$
Insert $v=\dot q$ in
$\frac{d}{dt}L_{v^k}=L_{q^k}$. The time
derivative hits both the metric and the
velocity. Raising the index $k$ with the
inverse metric and collecting the terms
quadratic in velocity gives
$$
\ddot q^\ell+
\Gamma^\ell_{ij}(q)\dot q^i\dot q^j=0,
$$
where
$$
\Gamma^\ell_{ij}=\tfrac12 g^{\ell k}
(\partial_i g_{jk}+\partial_j g_{ik}
-\partial_k g_{ij}).
$$
This is $\nabla_{\dot\gamma}\dot\gamma=0$.
It is an ODE, even when $M$ has many
dimensions. All the coordinates of the
unknown depend on one parameter $t$.
In the Euclidean plane the Christoffel
symbols in Cartesian coordinates vanish,
and $q(t)=p+tv$ is the familiar solution.
See \cite[Section 19]{arn} and the
\href{https://www.heap-project.org/tag/05Q2}
{Heap Project section on geodesics}.

Now let the unknown depend on two
coordinates. Stretch a membrane over a
plane region $\Omega$, prescribe its height
at the boundary, and write $u(x,y)$ for
the height in the interior. Its quadratic
energy for small slopes is
$$
E(u)=\frac12\int_\Omega|\nabla u|^2\,dx\,dy.
$$
Vary $u$ to $u+s\eta$, where $\eta$ is
smooth and supported in the interior.
The first variation at $s=0$ is
$$
\int_\Omega\nabla u\cdot\nabla\eta\,dx\,dy.
$$
If $u$ is smooth, integration by parts
puts both derivatives on $u$. Stationarity
for every $\eta$ gives $-\Delta u=0$.
This is the fourth equation on the list.
In one space dimension the same energy is
the kinetic action of a curve in the real
line, and its equation is $u''=0$.

There is a reason to keep the equation in
its integrated form. Add a load by
subtracting $\int_\Omega fu\,dx\,dy$ from
the energy. Stationarity now means
$$
\int_\Omega\nabla u\cdot\nabla\eta\,dx\,dy
=\int_\Omega f\eta\,dx\,dy
$$
for every smooth interior test function.
This identity makes sense as soon as $u$
has square-integrable first weak
derivatives and $f$ is square-integrable.
It is the weak form of $-\Delta u=f$ in
the interior. Boundary values are imposed
separately. We will use the same idea of
testing an equation in complex geometry.
See \cite[Chapter 8]{salsa-verzini}.

\section{What does a primitive solve?}
\label{section-dbar-primitive}

In one complex coordinate $z=x+iy$, a
function $u$ has
$$
\bar\partial u=
\tfrac12(u_x+i u_y)\,d\bar z.
$$
Thus $\bar\partial u=f$ is a first-order
linear PDE. For $f=d\bar z$ on a disc
one solution is $u=\bar z$. Adding any
holomorphic function gives another one.
Already here the kernel explains the
failure of uniqueness.

On the complex torus
$\mathbb{C}/(\mathbb{Z}+i\mathbb{Z})$ the
same $d\bar z$ is a global form. Could
it have a global smooth primitive? Its
lift would be a periodic function with
$u_x+i u_y=2$. Integrate over the unit
square. The integral of each derivative
vanishes because the opposite edge values
agree, whereas the integral of $2$ is $2$.
Local primitives exist on every chart and
still fail to fit together globally.

In higher degree $\bar\partial^2=0$
gives a necessary condition on the data,
$\bar\partial f=0$. The quotient of
closed forms by forms with a global
primitive is Dolbeault cohomology.
In the Kodaira problem $X$ is a compact
K\"ahler manifold of dimension $n$ and
$L$ a holomorphic line bundle. The datum
$f$ is an $L$-valued $(n,q)$-form with
$1\le q\le n$. We seek an $L$-valued
$(n,q-1)$-form $u$. In a holomorphic
frame $\bar\partial$ acts on the form
coefficients. For $q=1$ take a local
holomorphic frame $\tau$ of $K_X\otimes L$.
Writing
$$
u=a\tau,\qquad
f=\sum_j f_j\,d\bar z^j\wedge\tau,
$$
we are asking one coefficient $a$ to
satisfy all $n$ equations
$$
\frac{\partial a}{\partial\bar z^j}=f_j.
$$
For smooth data the condition
$\bar\partial f=0$ says that the mixed
derivatives required of $a$ agree,
$$
\frac{\partial f_j}{\partial\bar z^k}
=\frac{\partial f_k}{\partial\bar z^j}.
$$
Local solvability for smooth closed forms
is the
\href{https://www.heap-project.org/tag/08QG}
{Dolbeault lemma}. What can rule out a
global obstruction like the one on the
torus?

\section{What curvature estimates}
\label{section-dbar-curvature}

Give $X$ a K\"ahler form $\omega$ and $L$
a smooth Hermitian metric $h$. They give
forms a pointwise length and an integrated
squared norm
$$
\|v\|^2=\int_X |v|^2\,\frac{\omega^n}{n!}.
$$
The adjoint $\bar\partial^*$ moves a
derivative from one factor of the inner
product to the other. On smooth forms,
$$
(\bar\partial a,v)=(a,\bar\partial^*v).
$$
In the middle degree $(n,q)$ there are
two ways to differentiate a test form $v$.
The form $\bar\partial v$ goes up one
degree and $\bar\partial^*v$ goes down
one degree. Why should their two norms
together control the size of $v$?

Write $D'$ for the $(1,0)$ part of the
Chern connection on $L$. Its curvature
$\Theta_h(L)$ measures the failure of
covariant derivatives to commute.
Let $\Lambda$ be the adjoint of wedging
with $\omega$, and let $A$ be the
commutator of wedging with $i\Theta_h(L)$
and $\Lambda$. The integrated
Bochner--Kodaira--Nakano identity on a
compact K\"ahler manifold without boundary
gives, in bidegree $(n,q)$,
$$
\begin{aligned}
\|\bar\partial v\|^2+\|\bar\partial^*v\|^2
&=\|(D')^*v\|^2+(Av,v).
\end{aligned}
$$
The term $\|D'v\|^2$ vanishes because
its holomorphic degree would be $n+1$.
See \cite[Section 4, (4.7) and (4.10)]%
{demailly-cime}.

At a point diagonalize $i\Theta_h(L)$
relative to $\omega$, with eigenvalues
$\lambda_1,\ldots,\lambda_n$. Each
component of $v$ already contains all $n$
holomorphic indices. If its antiholomorphic
indices form the set $J$, then $A$ acts
on this component by
$$
\sum_{j\in J}\lambda_j.
$$
For $q=1$ a component ending in
$d\bar z^j$ therefore sees precisely
$\lambda_j$. If the curvature satisfies
$i\Theta_h(L)\ge\varepsilon\omega$ for
some $\varepsilon>0$, each eigenvalue
is at least $\varepsilon$. There are $q$
indices in $J$. Integrating and dropping
the squared norm on the right gives
$$
q\varepsilon\|v\|^2\le
\|\bar\partial^*v\|^2+\|\bar\partial v\|^2.
$$
The smooth Kodaira lecture chose
$\omega=i\Theta_h(L)$ for a positive
line bundle. All eigenvalues are then
one. With a fixed $\omega$ the lower
bound records how much positivity we have.
The flat trivial bundle on the torus has
zero curvature, so this argument supplies
no positive constant there.

\section{How the estimate gives a solution}
\label{section-estimate-solution}

Fix a closed datum $f$ of degree $(n,q)$.
The inequality we just obtained is about a
test form $v$. The unknown $u$ does not
occur in it. To find $u$, ask what its
inner products would have to be if it
solved the equation.

Write $H_j$ for the Hilbert space of
square-integrable $L$-valued $(n,j)$-forms,
using $H_{n+1}=0$. The two successive
derivatives are
$$
H_{q-1}\xrightarrow{T}H_q
\xrightarrow{S}H_{q+1}.
$$
Both are $\bar\partial$, on different
degrees. Take their weak domains. For
example $a\in H_{q-1}$ belongs to the
domain of $T$ when its distributional
derivative is represented by a form in
$H_q$. Testing against smooth forms shows
that if $a_j\to a$ and $Ta_j\to b$ in
these norms, then $Ta=b$. This says $T$
is closed. It is densely defined since it
contains all smooth forms. The same holds
for $S$, and $\bar\partial^2=0$ gives
$\operatorname{im}T\subset\ker S$.

Let $T^*$ be the Hilbert space adjoint.
On this compact manifold smooth forms are
dense in the common domain of $T^*$ and
$S$ for the norm controlling $v,T^*v,Sv$.
Thus the smooth estimate passes to that
domain. Write $C=q\varepsilon$. We know
$$
C\|v\|^2\le\|T^*v\|^2+\|Sv\|^2.
$$
Here is the existence argument of
\cite[Theorem 5.1 and its proof]%
{demailly-cime}, written with inner
products linear in the first variable.

\emph{Which part of $v$ can pair with
$f$?} Put $K=\ker S$. Since $S$ is
closed, $K$ is a closed subspace. For
any $v$ in the domain of $T^*$, take
its orthogonal decomposition
$$
v=v_0+v_1,\qquad v_0\in K,\quad v_1\perp K.
$$
For every $a$ in the domain of $T$,
$Ta\in K$, so $(Ta,v_1)=0$. By the
definition of the adjoint, $v_1$ belongs
to its domain and $T^*v_1=0$. Hence
$T^*v_0=T^*v$ and $Sv_0=0$. Apply the
estimate to $v_0$. It gives
$$
\sqrt C\,\|v_0\|\le\|T^*v\|.
$$
Since $f\in K$, $(v,f)=(v_0,f)$. By
Cauchy--Schwarz we now have precisely the
bound on the pairing that we need,
$$
|(v,f)|\le
\frac{\|f\|}{\sqrt C}\|T^*v\|.
$$

\emph{How do we recover $u$?} If $Tu=f$,
then testing against $v$ would give
$(T^*v,u)=(v,f)$. The right side is known.
Define a functional on the range of $T^*$
by prescribing
$$
\ell(T^*v)=(v,f).
$$
If two test forms have the same $T^*v$,
apply the pairing bound to their
difference. They give the same value of
$\ell$. The same bound gives continuity,
so $\ell$ extends to the closure of the
range. Set it equal to zero on the
orthogonal complement. By the
\href{https://www.heap-project.org/tag/0562}
{Riesz representation theorem}, this
functional on $H_{q-1}$ is given by a
vector $u$ with
$$
(T^*v,u)=(v,f),\qquad
\|u\|\le\frac{\|f\|}{\sqrt C}.
$$
The identity for every $v$ in the domain
of $T^*$ is exactly the definition of
$u$ belonging to the domain of $T^{**}$
with $T^{**}u=f$. A closed densely
defined operator equals its double
adjoint. We have proved $Tu=f$.

All other solutions differ from $u$ by a
vector in $\ker T$. Our extension by zero
makes $u$ orthogonal to $\ker T$, since
$(\operatorname{im}T^*)^\perp=\ker T$.
Pythagoras therefore makes $u$ the unique
solution of smallest norm. The difference
of two such solutions is the minimal
solution for the difference of their data.
Thus the very same bound gives continuous
dependence on $f$. Existence, uniqueness
after normalization, and stability now
have concrete meanings for this PDE.

What if $f$ is smooth? For $q>1$ the
minimal solution is perpendicular to the
image of the preceding $\bar\partial$,
since that image lies in $\ker T$.
Thus $\bar\partial^*u=0$ weakly. For $q=1$
this holds by degree. Applying the
Dolbeault Laplacian gives
$$
\Delta_{\bar\partial}u
=\bar\partial^*f.
$$
This second-order operator is elliptic.
With smooth metrics its elliptic regularity
implies that $u$ is smooth. Every smooth
closed form of the indicated type now has
a smooth primitive. By Dolbeault's theorem
this is precisely the vanishing
$$
H^q(X,K_X\otimes L)=0,\qquad 1\le q\le n.
$$
Compare the harmonic-form proof in
\href{https://www.heap-project.org/tag/066P}
{Complex geometry, Kodaira vanishing}.

\section{Correcting a local section}
\label{section-cutoff-section}

In degree $q=1$, a primitive $u$ is an
$L$-valued $(n,0)$-form, or equivalently
a section of $K_X\otimes L$, where $K_X$
is the canonical bundle. Suppose we know
a holomorphic section $t$ on a chart and
want a global holomorphic section taking
the same value at a chosen point $p$.
Choose a smooth cutoff $\chi$ supported
in the chart and equal to one near $p$.
Extend $\chi t$ by zero. It is global
and has the right value. Its error is
$$
f=\bar\partial(\chi t)=\bar\partial\chi
\wedge t.
$$
The error is supported where $\chi$
changes and is closed. If we can solve
$\bar\partial u=f$, then
$$
s=\chi t-u,\qquad\bar\partial s=0.
$$
There is still a problem. The choice
$u=\chi t$ solves the PDE and gives
$s=0$. What forces a correction to
preserve the value at $p$?

This is where the singular weight in the
Kodaira embedding lectures enters. In
coordinates centered at $p$, use a
squared metric weight comparable to
$|z|^{-2n}$ near $0$. The error $f$
vanishes there, so it pays no cost at the
pole. The weighted estimate gives a
primitive $u$ of finite weighted norm.
Near $p$ it solves $\bar\partial u=0$
weakly, so its local coefficient is
holomorphic. If $u(p)\ne0$, that
coefficient is bounded away from zero on
a smaller ball. In radial coordinates its
squared norm would contain a positive
multiple of
$$
\int_0^\delta r^{2n-1}r^{-2n}\,dr
=\int_0^\delta\frac{dr}{r}.
$$
This diverges. Thus $u(p)=0$, and
$s(p)=t(p)$ as required.

The weighted existence theorem requires
a global metric whose curvature is still
bounded below by $\varepsilon\omega$ in
the sense of currents. Its proof extends
the smooth theory by regularization. In
the embedding argument one starts with a
positive bundle $P$ and takes
$L=P^m\otimes K_X^{-1}$. For $m$ large
the positive curvature from $P^m$ absorbs
the curvature cost of introducing the
local poles. Now $s$ is a section of
$P^m$. Poles at two points allow sections
to distinguish their values. A stronger
pole forces the correction to vanish to
second order and preserves a prescribed
first derivative. These are the sections
that give projective coordinates in the
\href{https://www.heap-project.org/tag/0679}
{Kodaira embedding theorem}. See
\cite[Corollaries 5.3, 5.12--5.13 and
Exercise 5.14]{demailly-cime}.

There is a useful point of comparison
with a holomorphic Lagrangian fibration
$\pi:X\to B$. If $\omega_B$ is a
K\"ahler form on the smooth base, then
$\pi^*\omega_B$ vanishes on vectors
tangent to a smooth fibre. It cannot give
the strictly positive bound used above.
This locates the extra difficulty in
trying to construct a fibration from a
line bundle by the same argument.

\section{Further topics}
\label{section-further-pde}

The \href{https://impa.br/disciplina/%
equacoes-diferenciais-parciais-%
teoria-linear/%
}{IMPA course on linear PDEs}, taught by
Jo\~ao Pedro Ramos in 2026, goes from weak
derivatives and Sobolev spaces to elliptic
equations and evolution equations. The
following are topics for further study.

\begin{enumerate}
\item The model elliptic, parabolic, and
hyperbolic equations: the Laplace, heat,
and wave equations.

\item How characteristic curves explain
first-order equations, transport, and the
formation of singularities.

\item The fundamental solutions and Green
functions of the model equations, together
with the maximum principle and mean-value
property.

\item Fourier-series and Fourier-transform
methods, separation of variables, and the
role of distributions.

\item Sobolev spaces, weak derivatives,
embeddings, compactness, and trace
theorems.

\item Weak formulations and the main
existence mechanisms: energy estimates,
the Lax--Milgram theorem, compactness, and
the calculus of variations.

\item Elliptic regularity: when a weak
solution becomes smooth, and how the
regularity of the data controls that of
the solution.

\item Energy and semigroup methods for
evolution equations, including existence,
uniqueness, stability, and long-time
behavior.

\item The distinction between classical,
weak, distributional, and viscosity
solutions, and why different equations
require different notions of solution.

\item How differential operators fit the
geometric language already developed in
the project: symbols, ellipticity, the
Laplace--Beltrami operator, and de Rham
and Hodge Laplacians.

\item The first nonlinear phenomena:
conservation laws, shocks, blow-up,
scaling, and critical exponents.
\end{enumerate}
