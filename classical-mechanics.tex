\section{What determines a trajectory?}
To predict where a cart will be a second from
now, record its position and velocity and
specify the forces acting on it. In an
inertial frame a cart of constant mass $m$
obeys
$$
m\ddot x=F.
$$
A force determines acceleration. Two carts
subject to the same force can pass the same
point at different speeds.

\section{Releasing a spring}
Attach the cart to a spring on a frictionless
track. Measure $x$ from equilibrium. A
displacement $x$ produces a restoring force
$-kx$, where $k>0$. Pull the cart to $a$ and
release it from rest. Its motion is
$$
x(t)=a\cos(\omega t),\qquad
\omega=\sqrt{k/m}.
$$
Differentiating twice gives the force law.
The initial position fixes the amplitude and
the initial velocity fixes the phase. At
equilibrium the spring exerts zero force, yet
the cart passes through at its greatest
speed. Its initial spring energy $ka^2/2$ has
become kinetic energy.

See \cite[Vol. I, Sections 21-2 and
21-4]{feynman-lectures}.

\section{The Lagrangian and the action}
\label{section-action}

The same spring has a second description.
At position $x$ and velocity $v$, its
kinetic energy is $mv^2/2$ and its
potential energy is $kx^2/2$. Their
difference is the \emph{Lagrangian}
$$
L(x,v)=\frac{m}{2}v^2-\frac{k}{2}x^2.
$$
To a whole path it assigns a number, the
\emph{action},
$$
A[x]=\int_a^b L(x(t),\dot x(t))\,dt.
$$
The law of motion says that the first
derivative of the action vanishes when
we vary the path with its endpoints fixed.
What equation does this give?

Take a smooth displacement $\eta(t)$ with
$\eta(a)=\eta(b)=0$. Along the nearby
path $x+s\eta$, the velocity also changes,
by $s\dot\eta$. By the chain rule,
$$
\left.\frac{d}{ds}A[x+s\eta]\right|_{s=0}
=\int_a^b(L_x\eta+L_v\dot\eta)\,dt.
$$
Here both derivatives of $L$ are taken at
$(x(t),\dot x(t))$. Integrate the second
term by parts. The endpoint term vanishes,
leaving
$$
\int_a^b
\left(L_x-\frac{d}{dt}L_v\right)\eta\,dt=0.
$$
Since we can support $\eta$ in any small
time interval, the continuous coefficient
of $\eta$ vanishes everywhere. This is the
Euler--Lagrange equation. For the spring,
$L_v=m\dot x$ and $L_x=-kx$, so we recover
$m\ddot x=-kx$.

In coordinates $q^1,\ldots,q^d$ on a
configuration manifold, we can vary each
coordinate separately. Writing $v^i$ for
the velocity coordinates, we get
$$
\frac{d}{dt}
\frac{\partial L}{\partial v^i}
=\frac{\partial L}{\partial q^i}.
$$
For a free particle of unit mass the
metric gives $L(q,v)=g_q(v,v)/2$.
Its trajectories are geodesics. The PDE
chapter, Section
\ref{pde-section-geodesic-field}, takes up
the same variation when the unknown is a
field. See also
\cite[Sections 12, 13 and 19]{arn}.

\section{Hamiltonian mechanics}
\label{section-hamiltonian-mechanics}

How does the energy of a particle tell us
where it goes? Consider a particle of mass
$m>0$ moving on a line in a smooth
potential $V$. Its state at time $t$ is
the point $(q(t),p(t))$ in phase space,
where $q$ is position and $p=m\dot q$ is
momentum. Its energy as a function of
these two variables is its Hamiltonian,
$$
H(q,p)=\frac{p^2}{2m}+V(q).
$$
Holding $q$ fixed and differentiating
with respect to $p$ gives $p/m$, the
velocity. Holding $p$ fixed and taking
the negative derivative with respect to
$q$ gives $-V'(q)$, the force. Newton's
law says that the force is $\dot p$.
These two identities give
\emph{Hamilton's equations}.
$$
\dot q=\frac{\partial H}{\partial p},
\qquad
\dot p=-\frac{\partial H}{\partial q}.
$$
The derivatives are evaluated at the
current state $(q(t),p(t))$. Given an
initial position and momentum, the two
equations determine a trajectory for as
long as the solution exists. For the
spring, $V(q)=kq^2/2$. Hamilton's equations
give $\dot q=p/m$ and $\dot p=-kq$.
Eliminating $p$ gives $m\ddot q=-kq$.

Why do the same equations work for other
Lagrangians? Start with $L(q,v)$ as in
Section~\ref{section-action}. Set
$p=\partial L/\partial v$ and suppose
we can solve this equation smoothly for
$v$ in terms of $(q,p)$. The Hamiltonian
is then $H(q,p)=pv-L(q,v)$, with that
value of $v$. Its differential is
$$
\begin{aligned}
dH &= v\,dp+p\,dv
      -\frac{\partial L}{\partial q}\,dq
      -\frac{\partial L}{\partial v}\,dv\\
   &= v\,dp
      -\frac{\partial L}{\partial q}\,dq.
\end{aligned}
$$
The two terms in $dv$ cancel by the
choice of $p$. The coefficient of $dp$
is $v=\dot q$ along a trajectory. The
coefficient of $dq$ is $-\dot p$ by the
Euler--Lagrange equation. Reading off
the two partial derivatives of $H$ gives
Hamilton's equations again.

The geometric meaning is that the velocity
of the phase point is the Hamiltonian
vector field $X_H$ at that point. For the
derivation of the resulting equation for
an observable along the trajectory, see
\emph{Symplectic Geometry}, Section
\ref{symplectic-geometry-%
section-hamil-vecto-field-poiss}.
See also \cite[Section 2.5, Eq.~(2.25)
and Proposition~2.25]{hallq}.

For the corresponding evolution of a
quantum state, continue with the
Schr\"odinger equation in
\emph{Quantum Mechanics}, Section
\ref{quantum-mechanics-section-dirac-rule}.
