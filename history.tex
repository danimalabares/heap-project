\section{Apartheid in South Africa,
1948--1994}
\label{section-apartheid}

Apartheid, meaning separation in
Afrikaans, was a system of legally
enforced racial hierarchy and political
exclusion. The National Party came to
power in 1948 and expanded longstanding
colonial segregation and land dispossession,
including the 1913 Natives Land Act.

\medskip\noindent
{\bf Laws and social institutions.}
The Population Registration Act (1950)
assigned official racial categories.
The Group Areas Act (1950) imposed
segregated residential zones and
forced removals. Pass laws restricted
movement and employment; the Bantu
Education Act (1953) entrenched
educational inequalities. The
government created so-called
\emph{Bantustans} or homelands,
using them to exclude Black South
Africans from national citizenship
and political participation.

\medskip\noindent
{\bf Resistance and repression.}
The African National Congress (ANC),
founded in 1912, became a major
liberation movement, alongside the
Pan Africanist Congress, labor
unions, student movements, churches,
and the Black Consciousness movement.
The Freedom Charter (1955) articulated
demands for a nonracial democracy.
Police killed 69 demonstrators
at Sharpeville in 1960. The ANC
and PAC were banned; Nelson Mandela
was sentenced to life imprisonment
after the Rivonia Trial (1964).
The 1976 Soweto student uprising
against discriminatory education,
the death in police custody of
Steve Biko (1977), and escalating
labor and civic resistance intensified
domestic and international pressure.

\medskip\noindent
{\bf Negotiated transition.}
F. W. de Klerk unbanned liberation
movements and released Mandela
in 1990. Negotiations, violence,
and the repeal of apartheid laws
preceded the first election with
universal adult suffrage on
27 April 1994. The ANC won;
Mandela became president.
The Truth and Reconciliation
Commission investigated abuses.
Democratization did not immediately
undo inequalities in land, wealth,
education, and urban settlement.

\medskip\noindent
{\bf A connection to present politics.}
The ANC's role in the liberation
struggle helps explain its historic
legitimacy, but its governing record
must be assessed separately.
Apartheid's institutional legacy
remains central to debates about
land, inequality, public services,
and political representation.
Municipal electoral losses for
the ANC do not mean that the
post-apartheid democratic order
has itself been overturned.
