\section{Neutrinos}
\label{section-neutrinos}

\noindent
Hiroshi Nunokawas, PUC-Rio.
Friday Seminar, Seminar Name.
June 27, 2025.

\medskip\noindent
{\bf Abstract.} Hiroshi will come and tell us
everything we (not) wanted to know
about these mysterious particles, and are not
going to be afraid to ask. In
particular, about the neutrino oscillation,
and the great matrices.

\bigskip\noindent

Protons and neutrons have very similar mass
of $m_p\approx 940$ MeV, while
electrons have mass of $m_e \approx 0.5$ MeV.
MeV is $10^{6}$ electronvolts,
where one eV is approximately $1.6\times
10^{-19}$ J. This is standard in high
energy physics, they use electronvolts
instead of Joules. Recall that
2J=1N$\times$1m.

Most of the things we see are protons since
they are so much larger than
electrons. But protons nor neutrons are
elementary particles.

Here's the standard model:

\medskip

\begin{tabular}{c c c c}
Quarks & $\begin{bmatrix} u\\d
\end{bmatrix}_L $ & $\begin{bmatrix} c\\s
\end{bmatrix}_L$ & $\begin{bmatrix} t\\b
\end{bmatrix}_L$\\
Leptons & $\begin{bmatrix} \nu_e\\e^-
\end{bmatrix}_L $ & $\begin{bmatrix}
\nu_\mu\\ \mu^-
\end{bmatrix}_L$ & $\begin{bmatrix}
\nu_\tau\\ \tau^- \end{bmatrix}_L$\\
Generation & 1st & 2nd & 3rd\\
Bosons & $g$, $\gamma$ , $\omega^\pm$, $z$ \\
Higgs Bosson & $H$
\end{tabular}

\medskip

It is very particular that nature repeats
itself three times. The $L$ in those
matrix actually means left-handed, and
accounts for chirality. Only left-handed
fermions have weak interaction. Right-handed
have electromagnetic interaction,
gravitational interaction, but not weak
interaction.

And then there's neutrinos. They have
negative helicity (chirality). Being
left-handed, mathematically, means to have
helicity $-1$. I think this means
that the spin is left-handed. But chirality
and helicity are not the same:
helicity is observer-dependent, and chirality
is not. Almost all neutrinos we
can see (\% 99.99999…) have negative
helicity, but not all of them.

Consider the following:
$$
n+\nu_e\leftrightarrow p+e^-
$$
But it's not completely correct: we'd better
put $d$ instead of $n$, and $u$
instead of $p$: the $d$ and $u$ quarks,
instead of the neutrons and protons.

Now consider the following reaction: a
neutron decays into a proton, an electron
and an antineutrino:
$$
n\to p+e^+\overline{\nu}_e
$$
Protons is very stable, that's why we are
here. But neutron decays in only 15
minutes.

By experimental data, we can conclude that
neutrinos' mass is consistent with
zero. But if they have mass, it should be
much smaller than the electron's $m_v
\leq 0.5$ eV. And the electron is already the
lightest fermion!

If the mass of the neutrino was zero, i.e.
$m_\nu=0$, then $v_\nu=c$ in vacuum,
which would imply that
$$
\xymatrix{
\nu_e\ar[r]^{L}&\nu_e\ar[r]&\nu_e\\
0:00&0:00&0:00
}
$$
meaning: time doesn't pass! And this means
the state of the particle cannot
change.

\section{Vertex Algebras and the Monster}
\label{section-vertex-algebras-monster}

\noindent
David Melo. September 18, 2026.
Students' Colloquium. Room 228.
Friday, 15:20--16:20.

\medskip\noindent
{\bf Abstract.}
The classification of finite simple groups is
one of the most remarkable achievements in
algebra. Its proof spans about 500 articles,
involves about 100 mathematicians, and fills
more than 10,000 pages. The work surrounding
the classification also uncovered exceptional
structures connecting several areas of
mathematics.

A particularly striking example is the
relationship among the Monster group, sphere
packings, modular forms, and conformal field
theory through vertex algebras. The Leech
lattice gives the optimal sphere packing in
dimension $24$. A conformal field theory is
constructed from this lattice. Borcherds's
proof of monstrous moonshine explains
remarkable coincidences between the
Monster and Ramanujan's $j$-function. This
talk will introduce the exceptional objects
and outline their relationship.

\medskip\noindent
{\bf Introduction.}
The Monster is the largest sporadic finite
simple group. Its order is
$$
\begin{aligned}
|\mathbb{M}|
&=2^{46}3^{20}5^9 7^6 11^2 13^3 17\\
&\phantom{={}}\cdot19\cdot23\cdot29\cdot31
\cdot41\cdot47\cdot59\cdot71.
\end{aligned}
$$
Some of the coefficients in the Fourier
expansion of Ramanujan's $j$-function have
unexpected connections with dimensions of
representations of the Monster. Monstrous
moonshine explains these numerical
coincidences.

The Monster is the automorphism group of the
moonshine vertex algebra.

\medskip\noindent
{\bf Applications of the modular group.}
The modular group has applications in the
following areas.

\begin{enumerate}
\item
It parametrizes complex structures on a
torus.

\item
It occurs in Diophantine equations, including
the proof of Fermat's last theorem.

\item
It is used in cryptography.

\item
It helps compute elliptic integrals.

\item
It governs Fourier expansions of modular
functions and modular forms.
\end{enumerate}

\medskip\noindent
{\bf Modular invariants.}
The modular group is
$$
\operatorname{SL}_2(\mathbb{Z}).
$$
Its action on the upper half-plane comes from
translation and inversion.
$$
\tau\longmapsto\tau+1,
\qquad
\tau\longmapsto-\frac{1}{\tau}.
$$

A modular function is a meromorphic invariant
function for this action. A modular form of
weight $k$ transforms by
$$
f\left(\frac{a\tau+b}{c\tau+d}\right)
=(c\tau+d)^k f(\tau).
$$
The matrix with entries $a,b,c,d$ belongs
to the modular group.

Examples include Eisenstein series, theta
functions, and the Dedekind eta function.
Their coefficients often count solutions of
arithmetic equations. They were a key
ingredient in the proof of Fermat's last
theorem.

The $j$-function is the simplest nonconstant
modular function. Its Fourier expansion is
$$
j(\tau)=q^{-1}+744+196884q
+21493760q^2+\cdots.
$$

\medskip\noindent
{\bf Classification of simple groups.}
Let $G$ be a finite simple group. Then $G$ is
one of the following.

\begin{enumerate}
\item
A cyclic group of prime order.

\item
An alternating group.

\item
A group of Lie type.

\item
One of the $26$ sporadic groups.
\end{enumerate}

The periodic table of finite simple groups
displays the sporadic groups and their
relationships.

$$
\xymatrix{
& \mathbb{M}\ar[dl]\ar[d]\ar[dr] & \\
\mathbb{B} &
\mathrm{Fi}_{24}' &
\mathrm{Co}_1 \\
& \mathrm{Fi}_{23}\ar[ur] &
\mathrm{Co}_2\ar[u]
}
$$

\medskip\noindent
{\bf Lattices.}
An even lattice is a discrete subgroup
$L\subset\mathbb{R}^n$ such that
$$
\left\langle{} x,x \right\rangle{}
\in2\mathbb{Z}
$$
for every $x\in L$. Its dual lattice consists
of vectors in $\mathbb{R}^n$ whose pairings
with every element of $L$ are integral.

The roots of an even lattice are the vectors
of norm $2$. They have the smallest possible
positive norm.

\medskip\noindent
{\bf The Leech lattice.}
The Leech lattice comes from coding theory.
It gives the optimal sphere packing in
dimension $24$, as was proved in 2016. It is
the unique even unimodular lattice of rank
$24$ without roots.

The identity
$$
1^2+2^2+\cdots+24^2=70^2
$$
is another striking occurrence of the number
$24$.

Show a slide with a matrix whose rows form a
basis of the Leech lattice.

\medskip\noindent
{\bf Conway's group.}
The Conway group is
$$
\mathrm{Co}_1=
\operatorname{Aut}(\Lambda)/\{1,-1\}.
$$
It is a sporadic simple group. The Monster is
not obtained from $\mathrm{Co}_1$ by
adjoining one element. Rather,
$\mathrm{Co}_1$ occurs as a quotient of a
subgroup of the Monster.

\medskip\noindent
{\bf Group representations.}
{\it Definition.} A representation of $G$ on
$V$ is a homomorphism
$$
\rho:G\longrightarrow\operatorname{GL}(V).
$$
A morphism of representations is a linear map
$f:V\longrightarrow W$ such that
$$
f\rho(g)=\sigma(g)f
$$
for every $g\in G$.

Examples include the following.

\begin{enumerate}
\item
The group $\operatorname{Aut}(\Lambda)$ acts
on $\mathbb{R}^{24}$. The quotient
$\mathrm{Co}_1$ acts projectively.

\item
The Monster has an irreducible representation
of dimension $196883$.

\item
The symmetric group $S_n$ acts on
$\mathbb{R}^n$ by permuting coordinates.

\item
The circle has one-dimensional
representations indexed by $n\in\mathbb{Z}$.
$$
e^{i\theta}\longmapsto e^{in\theta}.
$$

\item
The groups $A_4$ and $A_5$ act on
$\mathbb{R}^3$ as rotation groups.
\end{enumerate}

\medskip\noindent
{\bf Putting a ring on it.}
For a group $G$, the group algebra
$\mathbb{k}[G]$ is the free vector space on
$G$, with multiplication extending the group
operation. Representations of $G$ are
equivalent to $\mathbb{k}[G]$-modules.

\medskip\noindent
{\bf Reducibility.}
{\it Definition.} A representation is
reducible if it has a nonzero proper
subrepresentation. In a semisimple category,
it is then a direct sum of two nonzero
representations. Otherwise, it is
irreducible.

\begin{theorem}
\label{theorem-finite-group-representations}
Let $G$ be finite and let $\mathbb{k}$ have
characteristic zero. There are finitely many
irreducible representations $V_j$, and every
finite-dimensional representation is a direct
sum of such representations.
\end{theorem}

\begin{theorem}[Peter--Weyl]
\label{theorem-peter-weyl}
Let $G$ be a compact topological group. Then
$$
L^2(G)=\bigoplus_{j\in\Sigma}
V_j\otimes V_j^*
$$
as an orthogonal decomposition of Hilbert
spaces.
\end{theorem}

For $G=S^1$, this gives
$$
L^2(S^1)=\bigoplus_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}
\mathbb{C}e^{in\theta}.
$$
The number of irreducible complex
representations of a finite group equals its
number of conjugacy classes.

\medskip\noindent
{\bf The Virasoro Lie algebra.}
{\it Definition.} A Lie algebra is a vector
space with a bilinear bracket satisfying
antisymmetry and the Jacobi identity.

On $\mathbb{C}^{\times}$, put
$$
L_n=-z^{n+1}\frac{d}{dz}.
$$
These vector fields satisfy
$$
[L_m,L_n]=(m-n)L_{m+n}.
$$
Their unique nontrivial central extension has
relations
$$
[L_m,L_n]=(m-n)L_{m+n}
+\frac{m^3-m}{12}\delta_{m+n,0}C.
$$
This is the Virasoro algebra. It is the
quantum analogue of local conformal
transformations and is central in string
theory. The central extension accounts for
normal-ordering anomalies.

\medskip\noindent
{\bf Positive-energy representations.}
{\it Definition.} A positive-energy
representation of the Virasoro algebra is a
vector space $V$. The element $C$ acts by a
scalar $c$, $L_0$ acts diagonally with
spectrum bounded below, and every eigenspace
of $L_0$ has
finite dimension.

Its renormalized character is
$$
\chi_V(q)=\sum_{h\in\mathbb{R}}
\dim(V^{L_0=h})q^{h-c/24}.
$$

\medskip\noindent
{\bf Vertex algebras.}
{\it Definition.} A conformal vertex algebra
consists of a vertex algebra $V$, a vacuum
vector $|0\rangle$, and a Virasoro vector $L$
with
$$
T=L_{-1}.
$$
The vector $L$ is the energy-momentum tensor.

\medskip\noindent
{\bf Borcherds's identity.}
For $a,b,c\in V$ and $m,n,k\in\mathbb{Z}$,
the identity is
$$
\begin{aligned}
&\sum_{j\geq0}\binom{m}{j}
(a_{(n+j)}b)_{(m+k-j)}c\\
&=\sum_{j\geq0}(-1)^j\binom{n}{j}
\left(a_{(m+n-j)}b_{(k+j)}c\right.\\
&\qquad\left.{}-(-1)^n
b_{(n+k-j)}a_{(m+j)}c\right).
\end{aligned}
$$

\medskip\noindent
{\bf Examples and applications.}
For a simple Lie algebra $\mathfrak{g}$ and a
complex number $k$, the universal affine
vertex algebra is $V^k(\mathfrak{g})$. Its
weight-one subspace is naturally
$\mathfrak{g}$. If $f\in\mathfrak{g}$ is
nilpotent, quantum Hamiltonian reduction
gives the vertex algebra
$W^k(\mathfrak{g},f)$. An even lattice $L$
gives a lattice vertex algebra
$V_L$.

Vertex algebras have applications to knot
theory and braid relations, integrable
partial differential equations in
symplectic geometry,
$D$-modules on Riemann surfaces,
combinatorial product-sum identities, and
theoretical physics.

\medskip\noindent
{\bf Bosonic strings.}
Let
$$
\mathcal{H}=\mathbb{C}[x_{-1},x_{-2},\ldots]
$$
and let $|0\rangle=1$. The Heisenberg modes
act by
$$
\begin{aligned}
(x_{-m})_{(n)}P&=x_{-m}P\qquad(n=-m),\\
(x_{-m})_{(n)}P&=n
\frac{\partial P}{\partial x_{-n}}
\qquad(n>0).
\end{aligned}
$$
For the standard conformal vector, the
central charge is $1$ and
$$
L_0x_{-k}=kx_{-k}.
$$
The character is
$$
\chi_{\mathcal{H}}(q)=
\prod_{m\geq1}\frac{1}{1-q^m}.
$$

\medskip\noindent
{\bf Lattice vertex algebras.}
For an even lattice $L$, the lattice vertex
algebra $V_L$ combines the Heisenberg algebra
with a copy of the group algebra of $L$.
Show a picture of the lattice, with a
Heisenberg cone at each lattice point.

\medskip\noindent
{\bf Moonshine.}
Starting with the Leech lattice vertex
algebra $V_\Lambda$, orbifolding by the
involution
induced by $-1$ gives the moonshine module
$$
V^{\natural}=V_\Lambda^+
\oplus V_\Lambda^{T,+}.
$$
The second summand comes from a twisted
module.
The automorphism group of $V^{\natural}$ is
the Monster.

\medskip\noindent
{\bf Extra ingredients.}
The no-ghost theorem states that critical
bosonic string theory in dimension $26$ has
no negative-norm physical states. Borcherds's
proof constructs a Lie algebra from a
semi-infinite cohomology group for
$$
V^{\natural}\otimes V_{II_{1,1}}.
$$
This construction is closely related to
current research on semi-infinite cohomology.



\section{Comparing spheres in high
dimensions}
\label{section-comparing-spheres}

\noindent
Bruno de Mendon\c{c}a Braga, IMPA.
XXIII Oktobermat, PUC-Rio.

Qui 29 out 2026, 13:00--14:00.

12\textsuperscript{o} andar do Edif\'icio
Leme, Sala de Reuni\~oes do Decanato do CTC.

\medskip\noindent
{\bf Title.} Comparando esferas em
dimens\~oes altas.

\medskip\noindent
{\bf Abstract.} The Oktobermat poster gives
only the title; no abstract was available.

\medskip\noindent
{\bf Sources of these notes.} Photographs of
the slides and of the board, and an
automatic transcript (whisper.cpp, in
Portuguese) of the audio. The talk was in
Portuguese; these notes are in English.
Statements follow the slides; remarks marked
as spoken come from the transcript, which is
poor in places. References were checked
against the arXiv papers listed at the end;
unchecked attributions are marked.

\medskip\noindent
{\bf Spheres of $\ell_p^k$.} Recall that a
norm on a real vector space $X$ is a function
$\|\cdot\| : X \to [0,\infty)$ such that
$\|x\| = 0$ only for $x = 0$,
$\|\lambda x\| = |\lambda|\,\|x\|$, and
$\|x+y\| \le \|x\| + \|y\|$. Different norms
give different geometries. For
$p \in [1,\infty)$ and $k \in \mathbb{N}$,
$$
\|(x_i)_{i=1}^k\|_p
= \Big(\sum_{i=1}^k |x_i|^p\Big)^{1/p},
$$
and for $p = \infty$,
$$
\|(x_i)_{i=1}^k\|_\infty
= \max\{|x_i| : 1 \le i \le k\}.
$$
We write $\ell_p^k$ for $\mathbb{R}^k$ with
the norm $\|\cdot\|_p$, and
$$
S_{\ell_p^k}
= \{x \in \mathbb{R}^k : \|x\|_p = 1\}
$$
for its unit sphere. For $k = 2$ the sphere
$S_{\ell_1^2}$ is a square standing on a
vertex, $S_{\ell_\infty^2}$ is an axis
parallel square, and as $p$ goes from $1$ to
$\infty$ the spheres $S_{\ell_p^2}$ pass
through the circle $S_{\ell_2^2}$ from one to
the other. For $k = 3$ they go from the
octahedron to the cube.

For fixed $k$ all these spheres are
homeomorphic: the radial projection
$$
x \in S_{\ell_p^k}
\mapsto \frac{x}{\|x\|_1}
\in S_{\ell_1^k}
$$
is a homeomorphism. The question of the talk
is how to compare all the spheres at once,
uniformly in the dimension $k$.

\begin{definition}
\label{definition-equi-uniformly-continuous}
A sequence of maps
$f_k : S_{\ell_p^k} \to S_{\ell_1^k}$,
$k \in \mathbb{N}$, is
\emph{equi-uniformly continuous} if for every
$\varepsilon > 0$ there is $\delta > 0$ such
that, for all $k$ and all
$x,y \in S_{\ell_p^k}$,
$$
\|x - y\|_p \le \delta
\implies
\|f_k(x) - f_k(y)\|_1 \le \varepsilon.
$$
\end{definition}

\begin{exercise}
\label{exercise-radial-projection-not-equi}
Let $p \in (1,\infty)$ and let $f_k$ be the
radial projection above. For
$\varepsilon \in (0,1)$ put
$x^k = (1,0,\ldots,0)$ and
$$
y^k_\varepsilon
= \Big((1-\varepsilon)^{1/p},
\big(\tfrac{\varepsilon}{k-1}\big)^{1/p},
\ldots,
\big(\tfrac{\varepsilon}{k-1}\big)^{1/p}
\Big).
$$
Then
$\sup_k \|x^k - y^k_\varepsilon\|_p \to 0$ as
$\varepsilon \to 0$, but
$$
\sup_k \|f_k(x^k) - f_k(y^k_\varepsilon)\|_1
= 2
$$
for every $\varepsilon > 0$. So the radial
projections are not equi-uniformly
continuous. (The second display was partly
hidden in the photograph; the supremum over
$k$ is my reading. Indeed
$\|y^k_\varepsilon\|_1 \to \infty$ as
$k \to \infty$, so $f_k(y^k_\varepsilon)$
puts almost all its mass off the first
coordinate.)
\end{exercise}

\medskip\noindent
{\bf Mazur maps.} For $p \in [1,\infty)$ and
$k \in \mathbb{N}$ define
$$
M_p^k : S_{\ell_p^k} \to S_{\ell_1^k},
\quad
(x_i)_{i=1}^k \mapsto
\big(\operatorname{sign}(x_i)\,
|x_i|^p\big)_{i=1}^k.
$$

\begin{theorem}[Mazur, 1929]
\label{theorem-mazur-maps}
Let $p \in [1,\infty)$. For every
$k \in \mathbb{N}$ and all
$x,y \in S_{\ell_p^k}$,
$$
\frac{1}{2^p}\|x - y\|_p^p
\le \|M_p^k(x) - M_p^k(y)\|_1
\le p\,\|x - y\|_p.
$$
\end{theorem}

\noindent
The constants do not depend on $k$, so the
spheres $S_{\ell_p^k}$ and $S_{\ell_1^k}$ are
equi-uniformly homeomorphic: the maps
$M_p^k$ are equi-Lipschitz and their inverses
are equi-H\"older. Spoken: the same formula
works for infinite sequences, giving a
uniform homeomorphism between the spheres of
$\ell_p$ and $\ell_1$. On the board, with
exponent $p/2$ instead of $p$, the same
formula gives
$M_{p,2} : S_{\ell_p} \to S_{\ell_2}$.

\begin{remark}
\label{remark-mazur-slide-typo}
The slide writes $M_p^k(x) - M_p^k(x)$ in the
middle term; it should be
$M_p^k(x) - M_p^k(y)$. I did not check the
constants against Mazur's paper. The Mazur
map from $S_{\ell_p}$ to $S_{\ell_2}$ is the
uniform homeomorphism of Benyamini and
Lindenstrauss, \emph{Geometric nonlinear
functional analysis}, Theorem 9.1, as cited
in Braga et al., arXiv:2512.14966.
\end{remark}

\medskip\noindent
{\bf The case $p = \infty$.} The formula for
$M_\infty^k$ makes no sense, and the
continuity estimates get worse as
$p \to \infty$. Are $S_{\ell_\infty^k}$ and
$S_{\ell_1^k}$ equi-uniformly homeomorphic?
The slide answers: no (Enflo, 1969). I have
not checked this attribution.

\medskip\noindent
{\bf Problem.} Is there a sequence of
homeomorphisms
$f_k : S_{\ell_\infty^k} \to S_{\ell_1^k}$
which is equi-uniformly continuous? Nothing
is asked of the inverses $f_k^{-1}$.

Spoken: the identity from $\ell_1^k$ to
$\ell_\infty^k$ is $1$-Lipschitz, but it does
not send the sphere to the sphere.

\medskip\noindent
{\bf Why the problem matters.} First, W. B.
Johnson asked it on MathOverflow
(``Lipschitz homeomorphisms between spheres
of $N$-dimensional spaces'', question 60426,
April 2011). He asks, with balls and
$\ell_2^N$ in place of spheres and
$\ell_1^k$, whether the infimum $C_N$ of
Lipschitz constants of homeomorphisms
$B_{\ell_\infty^N} \to B_{\ell_2^N}$ is
bounded; he notes that the $\ell_\infty^N$
norm is $2$-equivalent to the $\ell_p^N$ norm
for $p = \log_2 N$, so Mazur maps give
$C_N$ of order at most $\log N$. Spoken: this
is morally the same question.

Second, the reason Johnson asked: the Novikov
conjecture, through the work of Kasparov and
Yu.

\begin{definition}
\label{definition-property-h}
A real Banach space $X$ has
\emph{Property (H)} if there are increasing
sequences of finite dimensional subspaces
$(X_n)_n$ of $X$ and $(H_n)_n$ of $\ell_2$,
with $\bigcup_n X_n$ dense in $X$ and
$\bigcup_n H_n$ dense in $\ell_2$, and a
uniformly continuous map
$f : S_X \to S_{\ell_2}$ such that each
restriction
$$
f|_{S_{X_n}} : S_{X_n} \to S_{H_n}
$$
is a homeomorphism.
\end{definition}

\begin{remark}
\label{remark-property-h-degree-one}
This is the definition written on the board.
In Braga et al., arXiv:2609.22416, the
restrictions are required only to have
degree one; rational Property (H) asks for
nonzero degree. Spoken, in a question from
the audience: homotopy theory is already
present in the definition, through these
degree conditions.
\end{remark}

\noindent
Only $f$ is asked to be uniformly continuous,
not its inverses on the $S_{X_n}$, which may
not exist globally. Examples: Hilbert space,
and more generally $\ell_p$ for
$p \in [1,\infty)$ using the Mazur maps
$M_{p,2}$, whose inverses are even uniformly
continuous. Spoken: generalized Mazur maps,
used by Odell and Schlumprecht in the
solution of the distortion problem
(\emph{Acta Math.} 173, 1994), give
Property (H) for spaces with an unconditional
basis that do not contain the
$\ell_\infty^k$ uniformly.

Spoken: until July of this year, no separable
Banach space without Property (H) was known,
and the main open case was $c_0$, asked as
Problem 1 by Kasparov and Yu.

\begin{theorem}[Kasparov--Yu]
\label{theorem-kasparov-yu}
The strong Novikov conjecture holds for
groups that coarsely embed into a Banach
space with Property (H).
\end{theorem}

\noindent
Reference: G. Kasparov and G. Yu, \emph{The
Novikov conjecture and geometry of Banach
spaces}, Geom. Topol. 16 (2012), 1859--1880.

\medskip\noindent
{\bf The Novikov conjecture, 1965.} From the
slide: it is one of the most important open
problems of topology. It concerns groups, and
it is about the homotopy invariance of
certain polynomials in the Pontryagin classes
of a manifold that come from its fundamental
group. It is known for finitely generated
abelian groups, and not known for all groups.

\medskip\noindent
{\bf Back to the problem.} A positive answer
to the Problem would imply the Novikov
conjecture for every group. Indeed, the
$\ell_2$-sum
$$
\Big(\bigoplus_k \ell_\infty^k\Big)_{\ell_2}
$$
would have Property (H), and it contains
coarse copies of all countable groups
(Baudier and Lancien, 2008). Spoken: since
$c_0$ also contains copies of all separable
metric spaces, Property (H) for $c_0$ would
give the same conclusion; this is why $c_0$
is the main case. Conclusion on the slide:
the answer is probably negative. Spoken: it
would be too good to be true, since there is
no nontrivial statement about all groups at
once.

\begin{remark}
\label{remark-c0-universal-references}
Checked references: F. Baudier and
G. Lancien, \emph{Embeddings of locally
finite metric spaces into Banach spaces},
Proc. Amer. Math. Soc. 136 (2008),
1029--1033, Theorem 2.1, gives that the
$\ell_2$-sum of the $\ell_\infty^k$ is
Lipschitz universal for locally finite metric
spaces. I. Aharoni, \emph{Every separable
metric space is Lipschitz equivalent to a
subset of $c_0$}, Israel J. Math. 19
(1974), 284--291, gives the universality of
$c_0$. Both as cited in arXiv:2512.14966 and
arXiv:2609.15817.
\end{remark}

\medskip\noindent
{\bf A first attempt.} The group first tried
to prove that the answer is positive. The
radial projection
$$
(x_i)_{i=1}^k \in S_{\ell_\infty^k}
\mapsto
\Big(\frac{x_i}{|x_1| + \cdots + |x_k|}
\Big)_{i=1}^k
\in S_{\ell_1^k}
$$
is not equi-uniformly continuous. A modified
formula followed on the slide (illegible in
the photograph). Spoken: it looks at the
coordinates close to the maximum, counts how
many there are, and integrates. It passed
many numerical tests until a counterexample
appeared.

The counterexample uses staircase vectors.
For $m_1 < m_2 < \cdots < m_d \le k$ let
$x(\bar m) \in S_{\ell_\infty^k}$ be the
decreasing staircase with first coordinate
$1$, with $d$ steps of height $1/d$, the
$j$-th step ending at coordinate $m_j$, and
with zero coordinates after $m_d$. If
$m_1 < n_1 < m_2 < n_2 < \cdots < m_d < n_d$,
the difference $x(\bar n) - x(\bar m)$ takes
only the values $0$ and $1/d$, so
$$
\|x(\bar n) - x(\bar m)\|_\infty \le 1/d.
$$
But if the indices are ``spread out
enough'', then
$$
\|f_k(x(\bar n)) - f_k(x(\bar m))\|_1
> 1/2,
$$
so the modified maps are not equi-uniformly
continuous either. Spoken: as the gaps grow
one gets a lower bound independent of $k$.

\begin{remark}
\label{remark-first-paper-staircase}
These staircases appear in B. M. Braga,
C. Gartland, G. Lancien, P. Motakis,
E. Perneck\'a and Th. Schlumprecht, \emph{On
the uniform continuity of homeomorphisms
between the spheres of $\ell_\infty^k$ and
$\ell_1^k$}, arXiv:2512.14966 (December
2025). There they prove that no sequence of
equi-uniformly continuous homeomorphisms
$S_{\ell_\infty^k} \to S_{\ell_1^k}$ exists
among maps that do not increase support size,
or among step preserving maps. The radial
projection is their map (2.1). This paper was
not named in the talk.
\end{remark}

\medskip\noindent
{\bf Results.} Joint with Gartland, Lancien,
Motakis, Pernecká and Schlumprecht.

\begin{theorem}
\label{theorem-braga-et-al-null-homotopic}
Let
$f_k : S_{\ell_\infty^k} \to S_{\ell_1^k}$
be equi-uniformly continuous. Then $f_k$ is
homotopic to a constant map for $k$ large
enough.
\end{theorem}

\noindent
In particular no such sequence of
homeomorphisms exists, which answers the
Problem negatively. Spoken: the reason is
that the $\ell_\infty^k$ contain all finite
metric spaces, and the obstruction is
expander graphs.

\begin{theorem}
\label{theorem-expanders-prevent-h}
Let $X$ be a Banach space such that a
sequence of expander graphs coarsely embeds
into $X$. Then $X$ does not have
Property (H).
\end{theorem}

\begin{corollary}
\label{corollary-c0-not-h}
The space $c_0$ does not have Property (H).
\end{corollary}

\begin{remark}
\label{remark-expanders-prevent-h-paper}
Reference: B. M. Braga, C. Gartland,
G. Lancien, P. Motakis, E. Pernecká and
Th. Schlumprecht, \emph{Expanders prevent
Property (H)}, arXiv:2609.22416 (dated
September 22, 2026). Their Theorem 1 is more
general: $X_n$ and $Y_n$ finite dimensional
normed spaces, $Y_n \subset L_1$, expanders
equi-coarsely embedded in the $X_n$, and the
conclusion that equi-uniformly continuous
maps $S_{X_n} \to S_{Y_n}$ have degree zero
for large $n$. Their Corollary 2 excludes
rational Property (H) too, and Corollary 3
gives, through Osajda's groups containing
expanders, a finitely generated group that
coarsely embeds into no Banach space with
Property (H). The paper reports a Lean
verification of the results.

Not mentioned in the talk: L. Cheng,
Q. Cheng and Y. Wang, \emph{On Property (H)
of $c_0$ and Lipschitz problems of Gromov and
Johnson}, arXiv:2609.15817, also prove that
$c_0$ has neither Property (H) nor rational
Property (H), and that Johnson's constants
satisfy $C_N / \log N \to 1/2$. I have not
compared the two papers.
\end{remark}

\medskip\noindent
{\bf Expander graphs.} Let $(V,E)$ be a
finite graph, $k \in \mathbb{N}$ and $h > 0$.
For $A \subset V$ put
$$
\partial A
= \{v \in V \setminus A :
\exists u \in A,\ (v,u) \in E\}.
$$

\begin{definition}
\label{definition-expander}
A finite graph $(V,E)$ is a
\emph{$(k,h)$-expander} if every vertex has
at most $k$ neighbours and
$|\partial A| \ge h|A|$ for every
$A \subset V$ with $|A| \le |V|/2$. A
sequence of finite graphs $(V_n,E_n)_n$ is a
\emph{sequence of expander graphs} if all of
them are $(k,h)$-expanders for the same $k$
and $h$, and $\lim_n |V_n| = \infty$.
\end{definition}

\noindent
Spoken: $k$ is tiny compared with $|V|$, so
the graphs are sparse, but the boundary
condition says they are still very well
connected.

A connected graph is a metric space with the
\emph{shortest path distance}: $d(u,v)$ is
the least $n \in \mathbb{N}$ for which there
are vertices $v_0, \ldots, v_n$ with
$v_0 = u$, $v_n = v$ and
$(v_i,v_{i+1}) \in E$ for all
$i \in \{0,\ldots,n-1\}$.

\begin{definition}
\label{definition-coarse-embedding-sequence}
A sequence of metric spaces $(Y_n,d_n)_n$
\emph{coarsely embeds} into a metric space
$(X,d)$ if there are maps
$f_n : Y_n \to X$ and functions
$\omega,\rho : [0,\infty) \to [0,\infty)$
with $\lim_{t\to\infty} \rho(t) = \infty$
such that
$$
\rho(d_n(y,z))
\le d(f_n(y),f_n(z))
\le \omega(d_n(y,z))
$$
for all $n \in \mathbb{N}$ and all
$y,z \in Y_n$.
\end{definition}

\noindent
In other words: for every $r > 0$ there is
$s > 0$ such that $d_n(y,z) \le r$ implies
$d(f_n(y),f_n(z)) \le s$, and for every
$s > 0$ there is $r > 0$ such that
$d_n(y,z) \ge r$ implies
$d(f_n(y),f_n(z)) \ge s$. Spoken: with
$\omega(t) = Ct$ the maps would be
equi-Lipschitz; here $\omega$ is only finite
and $\rho$ only tends to infinity, so coarse
embeddings ignore topology and cardinality,
for instance the integer part
$\mathbb{R} \to \mathbb{Z}$ is a coarse
equivalence. The word ``grosseiro'' is a free
translation of ``coarse''.

\begin{remark}
\label{remark-coarse-slide-typo}
The slide writes
$\lim_{t\to\infty} \rho(t) = 0$; it should be
$\lim_{t\to\infty} \rho(t) = \infty$, as in
the ``in other words'' reformulation on the
same slide.
\end{remark}

\noindent
Examples, from the slide (attributions not
given in the talk and not checked by me):
\begin{enumerate}
\item A Banach space $X$ is infinite
dimensional if and only if the countably
branching trees $(T_k^\omega)_k$ of height
$k$ coarsely embed into $X$.
\item If expander graphs coarsely embed into
$X$, then $X$ does not coarsely embed into
$\ell_2$.
\item Every sequence $(Y_n,d_n)_n$ of
separable metric spaces coarsely embeds into
$c_0$.
\end{enumerate}
Item 3 together with
Theorem~\ref{theorem-expanders-prevent-h}
gives Corollary~\ref{corollary-c0-not-h}.

\medskip\noindent
{\bf Poincar\'e inequality for expanders.} If
$(V,E)$ is a $(k,h)$-expander and
$f : V \to L_1$, then
$$
\frac{1}{|V|}\sum_{v \in V}
\Big\|f(v) - \frac{1}{|V|}
\sum_{u \in V} f(u)\Big\|
\le \frac{2k}{h}\operatorname{Lip}(f),
$$
where $V$ has the shortest path distance, so
the Lipschitz constant is defined. The mean
deviation of $f$ from its average is
controlled by its local variation. Reference:
M. Ostrovskii, \emph{Metric embeddings}, De
Gruyter, 2013, Theorem 4.7, as cited in
arXiv:2609.22416.

\medskip\noindent
{\bf Idea of the proof.} Let
$f_k : S_{\ell_\infty^k} \to S_{\ell_1^k}$ be
equi-uniformly continuous. The Poincaré
inequality is used to show that, for $k$
large, $f_k$ is homotopic to a map
$$
x \in S_{\ell_\infty^k}
\mapsto \frac{F(x)}{\|F(x)\|_1}
\in S_{\ell_1^k},
$$
where $F$ is continuous and nowhere zero on
the whole ball $B_{\ell_\infty^k}$ (the
target of $F$ was hidden in the photograph).
Since the ball is contractible and $F$ never
vanishes, contracting the ball gives a
homotopy to a constant map. Spoken: once the
map has this form the conclusion is
immediate, but proving that it has this form
needs computations.

\medskip\noindent
{\bf Questions (spoken).}
\begin{enumerate}
\item How do the expanders enter the proof?
Assume the expanders equi-coarsely embed
into the spaces $\ell_\infty^{k}$; this holds
because $\ell_\infty^k$ contains finite
metric spaces isometrically once $k$ is
large enough. Start from equi-uniformly
continuous maps between the spheres. The
first lemma uses the Poincaré inequality to
modify the maps: recentre the images of the
graph so that their mean is $0$, keep the
average of their norms close to $1$ so that
they fall back near the sphere, and keep
control of the Lipschitz constant.
\item A remark from the audience: geometric
group theory needs extra conditions to have
interesting theorems, such as Gromov
hyperbolicity, and the expansion condition
looks like a weak form of hyperbolicity,
since it compares the boundary of a set with
its interior. The speaker did not know.
\item By analogy with the nonexistence of
isometric embeddings of the hyperbolic plane
into $\mathbb{R}^3$ (Hilbert's theorem),
where exponential growth forces a
singularity: is there a lower bound, in
terms of $k$ and $h$, for the dimension of a
Euclidean space into which a
$(k,h)$-expander embeds with given
distortion? The speaker did not know. The
questioner added that Gromov brought many
ideas of classical geometry to discrete
geometry, and that the Poincaré inequality
for expanders is the discrete analogue of an
isoperimetric inequality.
\end{enumerate}

\medskip\noindent
{\bf Sources of these notes.} Photographs of
the slides and of the board, and Daniel's

\medskip\noindent
{\bf Ramsey's theorem.} The story begins in
Cambridge in 1928, with a lemma of Frank
Plumpton Ramsey: if $n = n(k)$ is large
enough, then every colouring of the edges of
the complete graph $K_n$ with two colours
contains a monochromatic copy of $K_k$. More
generally, if $n = n(r,s,k)$ is large enough,
then every colouring of the $s$-element
subsets of $\{1,\ldots,n\}$ with $r$ colours
contains a monochromatic clique of size $k$:
a set of $k$ elements all of whose
$s$-subsets
have the same colour.

\medskip\noindent
{\bf The happy ending problem.} A few years
later, in Budapest, Esther Klein observed
that among any $5$ points in the plane, no
$3$ on a line, there are always $4$ points in
convex position. She asked whether, for every
$k$, any $n$ points in the plane with no $3$
on a line contain $k$ points in convex
position, once $n$ is large enough.
Spoken, her proof: if the convex hull of the
$5$ points has $4$ or $5$ vertices we are
done; otherwise it is a triangle with $2$
points inside, and the line through those
$2$ points leaves $2$ vertices of the
triangle on one side, which together with
the $2$ inner points are in convex
position.

\begin{theorem}[Erd\H{o}s--Szekeres, 1935]
\label{theorem-happy-ending}
If $n = n(k)$ is sufficiently large, then
every set of $n$ points in the plane, with no
$3$ on a line, contains $k$ points in convex
position.
\end{theorem}

\medskip\noindent
{\bf Ramsey numbers.} The Ramsey number
$R(k)$
is the smallest $n \in \mathbb{N}$ such that
every colouring of the edges of $K_n$ with
two colours, red and blue, contains a
monochromatic copy of $K_k$.
Ramsey's theorem says $R(k) < \infty$
(the slide dates it 1930).

\begin{theorem}[Erd\H{o}s--Szekeres, 1935]
\label{theorem-erdos-szekeres-upper}
$$
R(k) < 4^k.
$$
\end{theorem}

\noindent
The proof on the slides is the neighbourhood
argument. Pick a vertex $x$ and split the
other vertices into its red neighbourhood
$A$ and its blue neighbourhood $B$. A red
$K_{k-1}$ in $A$, or a blue $K_k$ in $A$,
gives what we want, and similarly for $B$;
iterating (the picture shows vertices
$v_1, v_2, v_3, \ldots$ and nested sets
$X_1 \supset X_2 \supset \cdots$) gives the
bound. In the off-diagonal notation below it
reads
$$
R(\ell,k)
\le \binom{k+\ell-2}{\ell-1}.
$$

Spoken: pick a vertex, keep the larger of
its red and blue neighbourhoods, which has at
least half of the remaining vertices, and
repeat inside it. After $2k$ steps one colour
was chosen at least $k$ times, and those
vertices form a monochromatic clique. Each
step halves the set, so $2^{2k} = 4^k$
vertices suffice.

\medskip\noindent
{\bf Lower bounds.} The first explicit
construction gives
$$
(k-1)^2 < R(k) < 4^k.
$$
Take $k-1$ disjoint copies of $K_{k-1}$ and
colour their edges red, and all the other
edges blue. A red clique lies in one copy, so
has at most $k-1$ vertices; a blue clique
meets each copy at most once, so also has at
most $k-1$ vertices. The slide ends with
``Challenge: find a better explicit
colouring!'' Spoken: it is not easy to beat
this ``ugly square'', but Erd\H{o}s already
proved an exponential lower bound.

\begin{theorem}[Erd\H{o}s, 1947]
\label{theorem-erdos-1947}
$$
2^{k/2} < R(k) < 4^k.
$$
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Colour randomly: each edge of $K_n$ is red or
blue independently with probability $1/2$.
If the expected number of monochromatic
copies of $K_k$ is less than $1$, then it is
zero for some colouring. Let $X$ be the
number of monochromatic copies of $K_k$. Then
$$
\mathbb{E}[X]
= \binom{n}{k} 2^{1 - \binom{k}{2}}
< 1
$$
if $n \le 2^{k/2}$.
\end{proof}

\begin{theorem}[Campos--Griffiths--Morris--Sahasrabudhe]
\label{theorem-cgms}
There is a constant $\varepsilon > 0$ such
that
$$
R(k) < (4 - \varepsilon)^k
$$
for all sufficiently large $k \in
\mathbb{N}$.
\end{theorem}

\noindent
Checked: M. Campos, S. Griffiths, R. Morris
and J. Sahasrabudhe, \emph{An exponential
improvement for diagonal Ramsey}, Annals of
Math. 203 (2026), no.~3; arXiv:2303.09521.
This was the first exponential improvement
on
Theorem~\ref{theorem-erdos-szekeres-upper}.

\medskip\noindent
{\bf Off-diagonal Ramsey numbers.} The
Ramsey number $R(\ell,k)$ is the smallest
$n \in \mathbb{N}$ such that every red--blue
colouring of $E(K_n)$ contains either a red
$K_\ell$ or a blue $K_k$. For $\ell = 3$:
either a red triangle or a blue $K_k$. The
slides go through the history of $R(3,k)$
(dates as on the slides, not checked).
\begin{enumerate}
\item Erd\H{o}s, 1957:
$k^{1+\varepsilon} < R(3,k) < k^2$, by a
geometric construction: place $n$ vertices
randomly on a high dimensional sphere and
colour a pair red if the points are ``far
apart''. On the board: a sphere $S^d$ with
points and an angle between two of them, and
a spherical cap. Spoken: colour a pair red
when the angle between the points exceeds
$2\pi/3$. There is no red triangle, since
three unit vectors cannot have pairwise inner
products below $-1/2$ (the square of the norm
of their sum would be negative). A blue
clique has all angles below $2\pi/3$, and the
concentration of measure on high dimensional
spheres keeps such sets small. Asked about
the constant $\varepsilon$, the speaker did
not know it.
\item Erd\H{o}s, 1959:
$(k/\log k)^{3/2} < R(3,k)$, by colouring
randomly and destroying the triangles.
Spoken: with fewer than $n/2$ triangles,
remove one vertex from each; deleting
vertices creates no blue cliques.
\item Erd\H{o}s, 1961:
$(k/\log k)^2 < R(3,k)$, the same idea, now
re-colouring edges. A slide shows a random
red--blue colouring of $K_5$. Spoken:
recolouring an edge may create larger blue
cliques, and controlling this needed new
techniques, now standard.
\item Ajtai--Koml\'os--Szemer\'edi, 1980:
$R(3,k) < C k^2/\log k$, by the
``semi-random'' method. Spoken: in a
colouring with no red triangle, the red
neighbourhood of a vertex is a blue clique.
So either some vertex has red degree at
least $k$, or one can pick a vertex, discard
it with its neighbourhood, and repeat; this
gives $k^2$. Choosing the vertices at random
among those left, depending on earlier
choices, saves the $\log k$.
\item Kim, 1995:
$R(3,k) = \Theta(k^2/\log k)$, also
semi-random: choose the red edges randomly,
one by one (in batches), depending on
earlier choices. Spoken, the simpler
\emph{triangle-free process}: start with no
red edges, and at each step make red an edge
chosen uniformly among those that create no
red triangle.
\end{enumerate}

\begin{theorem}[Shearer; Bohman--Keevash; Fiz
Pontiveros--Griffiths--Morris]
\label{theorem-r3k-quarter}
$$
\Big(\frac{1}{4} + o(1)\Big)
\frac{k^2}{\log k}
\le R(3,k) \le
(1 + o(1))\frac{k^2}{\log k}.
$$
\end{theorem}

\noindent
The upper bound (Shearer) is an optimised
version of the Ajtai--Koml\'os--Szemer\'edi
approach. The lower bound (Bohman--Keevash,
Fiz Pontiveros--Griffiths--Morris) tracks
the \emph{triangle-free process}, which adds
random edges one at a time subject to
creating no triangle, all the way to its
asymptotic endpoint. Checked references, as
cited in arXiv:2510.19718: J. Shearer,
\emph{A note on the independence number of
triangle-free graphs}, Discrete Math. 46
(1983), 83--87; T. Bohman and P. Keevash,
\emph{Dynamic concentration of the
triangle-free process}, Random Structures
Algorithms 58 (2021), 221--293; G. Fiz
Pontiveros, S. Griffiths and R. Morris,
\emph{The triangle-free process and the
Ramsey number $R(3,k)$}, Mem. Amer. Math.
Soc. 263 (2020), no.~1274.

\medskip\noindent
{\bf More off-diagonal numbers.}
\begin{theorem}[Erd\H{o}s--Szekeres, 1935;
Spencer, 1977]
\label{theorem-spencer}
$$
\Big(\frac{k}{\log k}\Big)^{(\ell+1)/2}
\lesssim R(\ell,k) \le k^{\ell-1}.
$$
\end{theorem}

\noindent
Erd\H{o}s conjectured in 1947 (date as on the
slide) that
$$
R(\ell,k) = k^{\ell - 1 + o(1)}
$$
for fixed $\ell$ and $k \to \infty$. For
$\ell = 4$ the slides give
$$
\frac{k^{5/2}}{(\log k)^2}
\le R(4,k) \le
\frac{k^3}{(\log k)^2}
$$
(Ajtai--Koml\'os--Szemer\'edi, 1980; Bohman,
2009), and the conjecture
$R(4,k) = k^{3+o(1)}$.

\noindent
Spoken: for $\ell = 3$ both exponents equal
$2$, but for $\ell \ge 4$ they differ, and
the methods for $R(3,k)$ did not close the
gap.

\medskip\noindent
{\bf Multicolour off-diagonal numbers.} The
Ramsey number $R(3,3,k)$ is the smallest
$n \in \mathbb{N}$ such that every colouring
of $E(K_n)$ with red, blue and green contains
a red triangle, a blue triangle, or a green
$K_k$. The Erd\H{o}s--Szekeres argument gives
$k \le R(3,3,k) \le k^3$.

\begin{theorem}[Alon--R\"odl, 2005]
\label{theorem-alon-rodl}
$$
\frac{k^3}{(\log k)^4}
\le R(3,3,k) \le
\frac{k^3}{(\log k)^2}.
$$
\end{theorem}

\noindent
Spoken: the upper bound improves $k^3$ by
$(\log k)^2$ with the
Ajtai--Koml\'os--Szemer\'edi method; lower
bounds beyond $k^2$ were out of reach until
Alon and R\"odl. ``I want to show you the key
lemma of this proof, which is very beautiful,
a very simple proof \ldots{} an idea that was
very important in the area in recent years.
So perhaps this idea was the key of the
story in Ramsey theory in recent years.''
(Translated from the transcript.)

\begin{lemma}[Alon--R\"odl, 2005]
\label{lemma-alon-rodl}
If there is a triangle-free graph $G$ with
$n$ vertices and fewer than
$\sqrt{\binom{n}{k}}$ independent sets of
size $k$, then $R(3,3,k) > n$.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
Let the red and the blue edges be random
copies of $G$: put $G$ on the vertex set
$\{1,\ldots,n\}$ by two independent uniformly
random bijections. If the expected number of
green copies of $K_k$ is less than $1$, it is
zero for some choice of the two bijections.
Let $X$ be the number of green copies of
$K_k$, and for a $k$-set $S$ let $p_S$ be
the probability that $S$ is independent in
one random copy of $G$. Then
$$
\mathbb{E}[X]
= \sum_{|S| = k} p_S^2.
$$
\end{proof}

\noindent
The slides stop there. To finish (my
completion, not on the slides): a set $S$
spans a green $K_k$ exactly when it is
independent in both copies; the two events
are independent and each has probability
$i_k(G)/\binom{n}{k}$, where $i_k(G)$ is the
number of independent $k$-sets of $G$. So
$$
\mathbb{E}[X]
= \binom{n}{k}
\Big(\frac{i_k(G)}{\binom{n}{k}}\Big)^2
= \frac{i_k(G)^2}{\binom{n}{k}},
$$
which is less than $1$ by hypothesis. An
edge lying in both copies can be coloured
red: the red and blue graphs are then
subgraphs of copies of $G$, hence
triangle-free. For
Theorem~\ref{theorem-alon-rodl} one takes for
$G$ a triangle-free graph with few
independent $k$-sets, for example a famous
algebraic construction of Alon.

Spoken, in the discussion: the two copies of
$G$ are placed on the same vertex set by a
random bijection, and an edge lying in both
can take either colour. The randomness is
only there to avoid green copies of $K_k$.
The speaker said this idea is subtle, and
audiences often have trouble with it.

\begin{remark}
\label{remark-alon-rodl-reference}
Checked: N. Alon and V. R\"odl, \emph{Sharp
bounds for some multicolour Ramsey numbers},
Combinatorica 25 (2005), 125--141, as cited
in arXiv:2609.28455. That paper describes
the method as: show that there is a
triangle-free graph with few independent
$k$-sets, then take random copies of it as
two of the colours. Its footnote says that
Alon and R\"odl stated slightly weaker
bounds ([Theorem 3.2] there), and that He
and Wigderson show how to get the bounds of
Theorem~\ref{theorem-alon-rodl}. I did not
check the lemma's statement against Alon and
R\"odl's paper itself.
\end{remark}

\begin{theorem}[Andrades--Campos--Morris,
2026+]
\label{theorem-acm-r33k}
$$
R(3,3,k)
= \Theta\Big(\frac{k^3}{(\log k)^2}\Big).
$$
\end{theorem}

\noindent
Proof idea on the slide: let the red and
blue edges be random copies of a
triangle-free graph with $2^{O(k)}$ times
the minimal possible number of independent
$k$-sets. Checked: B. Andrades, M. Campos and
R. Morris, \emph{On the multicolour Ramsey
numbers $R(3,3,k)$}, arXiv:2609.28455. Their
Theorem 1.2 gives, for fixed $r \ge 2$,
$$
R_r(3,\ldots,3,k)
= \Theta\Big(\frac{k^r}{(\log k)^{r-1}}\Big),
$$
and the abstract says the lower bound
combines the Hefty--Horn--King--Pfender
construction with the method of Alon and
R\"odl.

{\bf Back to $R(4,k)$.}

\begin{theorem}[Mattheus--Verstra\"ete, 2024]
\label{theorem-mattheus-verstraete}
$$
\frac{k^3}{(\log k)^4}
\le R(4,k) \le
\frac{k^3}{(\log k)^2}.
$$
\end{theorem}

\noindent
Proof: an algebraic and geometric
construction due to O'Nan (1972), together
with probabilistic and combinatorial methods
to count independent sets. Spoken: an
algebraic object, with random complete
graphs placed on its lines. This proves the

(Not checked against the paper.)

\medskip\noindent
{\bf Two breakthroughs for triangle-free
graphs.}

\begin{theorem}[Campos--Jenssen--Michelen--Sahasrabudhe, 2025+]
\label{theorem-cjms}
$$
\Big(\frac{1}{3} + o(1)\Big)
\frac{k^2}{\log k}
\le R(3,k) \le
(1 + o(1))\frac{k^2}{\log k}.
$$
\end{theorem}

\noindent
Proof: blow-ups of sparse random graphs have
few large independent sets. One can
therefore destroy these few independent
$k$-sets with the triangle-free process, and
obtain a denser, extremely pseudorandom,
triangle-free graph than that of
Fiz Pontiveros--Griffiths--Morris and
Bohman--Keevash.

\begin{theorem}[Hefty--Horn--King--Pfender,
2025+]
\label{theorem-hhkp}
$$
\Big(\frac{1}{2} + o(1)\Big)
\frac{k^2}{\log k}
\le R(3,k) \le
(1 + o(1))\frac{k^2}{\log k}.
$$
\end{theorem}

\noindent
Proof: take two random copies of the CJMS
blow-up. Spoken: the lesson of Alon--R\"odl
is that few independent sets are almost as
good as none, since randomness destroys
them. Florian Pfender, after hearing Marcus
Michelen talk about CJMS, replaced the
triangle-free process by two random copies
of the blow-up. The copies create triangles,
but because the graph is a blow-up they can
be destroyed efficiently. The same
construction was later used to disprove a
well-known conjecture (its name is not
recoverable from the audio), and Andrades,
Campos and Morris use it for
$R(3,3,k)$. Checked: Z. Hefty, P. Horn,
D. King and F. Pfender, \emph{Improving
$R(3,k)$ in just two bites},
arXiv:2510.19718, Theorem 1.2, via a
triangle-free graph on $n$ vertices with
independence number less than
$(1+\varepsilon)\sqrt{n \log n}$. It cites
M. Campos, M. Jenssen, M. Michelen and
J. Sahasrabudhe, \emph{A new lower bound for
the Ramsey numbers $R(3,k)$},
arXiv:2505.13371, for the constant $1/3$,
and says they conjectured the constant
$1/2$.

{\bf Off-diagonal Ramsey numbers are finally
solved.}

\begin{theorem}[Brada\v{c}, 2026+]
\label{theorem-bradac}
For fixed $\ell \ge 3$,
$$
\frac{k^{\ell-1}}{(\log k)^{2\ell-4}}
\lesssim R(\ell,k) \le
\frac{k^{\ell-1}}{(\log k)^{\ell-2}}.
$$
\end{theorem}

\noindent
Proof: a construction inspired by the
approaches of Hefty--Horn--King--Pfender and
Campos--Jenssen--Michelen--Sahasrabudhe, but
also using several further extremely clever
new ideas. This proves Erd\H{o}s's
conjecture $R(\ell,k) = k^{\ell-1+o(1)}$.
Checked: D. Brada\v{c}, \emph{Off-diagonal
Ramsey numbers}, arXiv:2605.28793 (May 2026),
lower bound
$\Omega(k^{\ell-1}/(\log k)^{2\ell-4})$; for
$\ell \ge 5$ it improves Spencer's bound,
which had seen only polylogarithmic
improvements since 1977.

{\bf A geometric lemma.} Near the end Morris
showed a toy problem and a lemma.

\medskip\noindent
{\bf Toy problem.} Does every bijection
$f : \{-1,1\}^n \to \{-1,1\}^n$ satisfy
$$
\mathbb{P}\big(\left\langle{} x,y
\right\rangle{} \ge 0, \
\left\langle{} f(x),f(y) \right\rangle{}
\ge 0\big) \ge c
$$
for some absolute constant $c > 0$? Here $x$
and $y$ are independent uniformly random
elements of $\{-1,1\}^n$.

\begin{lemma}[Geometric lemma]
\label{lemma-geometric}
Let $x$ and $y$ be independent uniformly
random elements of a finite set $X$, and let
$f_1, \ldots, f_r : X \to \mathbb{R}^n$ be
arbitrary functions. Either, with
probability at least $c$,
$$
\left\langle{} f_i(x),f_i(y)
\right\rangle{} \ge -1
$$
for every $i \in [r]$, or there are a colour
$\ell \in [r]$ and a
sufficiently large $\lambda > 0$ such that
$$
\mathbb{P}\big(\left\langle{} f_\ell(x),
f_\ell(y) \right\rangle{} \ge \lambda\big)
\ge e^{-O(\sqrt{\lambda})}.
$$
\end{lemma}

\noindent
Roughly speaking, if the $r$ functions
exhibit a large amount of ``negative
correlation'', then one of them must exhibit
a significant amount of ``clustering''.

\begin{remark}
\label{remark-geometric-lemma-source}
Checked: this is a simplified form of
Lemma 3.1 of P. Balister, B. Bollob\'as,
M. Campos, S. Griffiths, E. Hurley,
R. Morris, J. Sahasrabudhe and M. Tiba,
\emph{Upper bounds for multicolour Ramsey
numbers}, arXiv:2410.17197, whose main
theorem is
$R_r(k) \le e^{-\delta k} r^{rk}$ for every
$r \ge 2$, the first exponential improvement
on Erd\H{o}s--Szekeres for $r \ge 3$ colours.
Spoken, the toy problem: for a uniformly
random bijection the two events are
independent, each of probability about
$1/2$, so the probability is about $1/4$.
Can a bijection make them so negatively
correlated that the probability tends to
$0$? The answer is no, and this is
``perhaps the fact at the bottom of this
proof'': much negative correlation forces
much clustering.
There the lemma is one existence statement,
not a dichotomy: there are $\lambda \ge -1$
and $i \in [r]$ with
$\left\langle{} \sigma_i(U), \sigma_i(U')
\right\rangle{} \ge \lambda$ and
$\left\langle{} \sigma_j(U), \sigma_j(U')
\right\rangle{} \ge -1$ for all $j \ne i$,
with probability
at least $\beta e^{-C\sqrt{\lambda+1}}$,
where $\beta = 3^{-4r}$ and
$C = 4r^{3/2}$. The first alternative above
is the case $\lambda = -1$. The toy problem
does not appear in that paper.
\end{remark}

\begin{enumerate}
\item \emph{Daniel recalls that Morris said
perhaps the key idea of this whole area of
Ramsey theory is in the Alon--R\"odl
lemma.} Confirmed by the transcript: see
the quotation before
Lemma~\ref{lemma-alon-rodl}. Here is my
reading
of the idea (an explanation, not from the
talk). Lemma~\ref{lemma-alon-rodl} turns a
three-colour problem into a question about
one graph. A green clique must be
independent in two independent random copies
of $G$, so its probability is squared. So
one needs a triangle-free $G$ whose number
of independent $k$-sets is only below the
square root of the trivial count
$\binom{n}{k}$, not below $1$; an
independence number below $k$ would be far
harder. In short: random overlays of one
good graph, with independence turning a
count into a square. This is the pattern of
the later results above.
Theorem~\ref{theorem-hhkp} takes two random
copies of the CJMS blow-up,
Theorem~\ref{theorem-acm-r33k} combines the
HHKP construction with this method, and
Theorem~\ref{theorem-cjms} and
Theorem~\ref{theorem-mattheus-verstraete}
rest on counting independent sets, which is
what the lemma asks for. Daniel's TO\_DO has
a reminder to study this lemma; the original
is Alon--R\"odl, Combinatorica 2005 (see
Remark~\ref{remark-alon-rodl-reference}).
\item \emph{The toy problem.} For $f$ the
identity, the probability is
$\mathbb{P}(\left\langle{} x,y
\right\rangle{} \ge 0)$,
which is at least $1/2$ by the symmetry
$y \mapsto -y$. The question is whether a
bijection can make the signs of
$\left\langle{} x,y \right\rangle{}$ and
$\left\langle{} f(x),f(y) \right\rangle{}$
almost always disagree. My reading, not from
the talk: it
is the case $r = 2$ of the geometric lemma,
with $f_1 = \mathrm{id}$ and $f_2 = f$, after
rescaling, and its two alternatives are a
two-colour Ramsey dichotomy: either both
colourings are often nonnegative together,
or one of them clusters. Morris's answer
(spoken): the probability cannot tend to
$0$, and this is the fact behind the
geometric lemma.
\item \emph{Relation with the Torelli
project?} I see no direct mathematical
link; the shared vocabulary hides different
roles. There one specific graph is to be
recovered from an invariant: a rigidity or
inverse problem. In Ramsey theory the graphs
are arbitrary and large, the questions are
extremal (how large before every colouring
contains a given structure), and probability
is a method of proof: random colourings and
random graphs show that good objects exist.
I know of no result connecting them.
\end{enumerate}

\medskip\noindent
{\bf Long range effects.} The opening slide
lists climate change, viral spread,

repair DNA by injecting electrons to test the

locate the fault. The long range of these

Spoken: the strand is very long and thin, so

\medskip\noindent
{\bf Electrons in a crystal.} A perfect
crystal is modelled by $\mathbb{Z}^d$. The
hopping to nearest neighbours is the
adjacency operator
$$
(A\varphi)(n) = \sum_{m \sim n} \varphi(m),
$$
where $m \sim n$ means $|m-n| = 1$ in the
$\ell^1$ norm. A non-local hopping is an
operator
$$
(T\varphi)(n) = \sum_{m \neq n} T(m,n)\,
\varphi(m),
\qquad
T(m,n) \sim \frac{1}{|m-n|^{\gamma}}.
$$
Spoken: the electron may jump anywhere, with
a probability decreasing with distance; the
interesting case is polynomial decay that is
just summable, so that $T$ is bounded.

A random crystal is modelled by the
multiplication operator
$$
(V_\omega \varphi)(n) = \omega(n)\varphi(n),
$$
with $(\omega(n))_{n \in \mathbb{Z}^d}$
independent and identically distributed.
Spoken: first think of a coin toss at each
site; later the values are drawn from a
density with some regularity.

\begin{definition}
\label{definition-anderson-type-model}
An \emph{Anderson-type model} is the
operator on
$$
\ell^2(\mathbb{Z}^d) = \Big\{\psi :
\mathbb{Z}^d \to \mathbb{C} :
\sum_{n} |\psi(n)|^2 < \infty\Big\}
$$
given by
$$
H_\omega = T + \lambda V_\omega,
$$
where $T$ is linear and bounded, $V_\omega$
is as above, and $\lambda > 0$ is the degree
of disorder.
\end{definition}

\noindent
Philip Anderson (1958): disorder affects
transport properties. \emph{Anderson
localization} is the absence of propagation
in sufficiently disordered media. Spoken: a
material may stop being a conductor and
become an insulator. With $T = A$ this is
the Anderson model proper.

\medskip\noindent
{\bf Quantum mechanics.} The Hamiltonian $H$
is self-adjoint on $\ell^2(\mathbb{Z}^d)$,
with the physicists' inner product
$\left\langle{} \varphi,\psi
\right\rangle{} = \sum_n
\overline{\varphi(n)}\psi(n)$, linear in the
second entry. The Schr\"odinger equation
$i\partial_t \psi(t) = H\psi(t)$ is solved by
$\psi(t) = e^{-itH}\psi$. Since $e^{-itH}$ is
unitary, $\|e^{-itH}\psi\| = \|\psi\|$, so
for $\|\psi\| = 1$ the numbers
$$
|\left\langle{} \psi(t),\delta_n
\right\rangle{}|^2
= |\left\langle{} e^{-itH}\psi,\delta_n
\right\rangle{}|^2
$$
sum to $1$; by Born's rule (1926) this is
the probability that the electron is at $n$
at time $t$.

\medskip\noindent
{\bf Central question.} Start with the
electron at the origin, $\psi = \delta_0$.
What happens to
$|\left\langle{} e^{-itH}\delta_0,\delta_n
\right\rangle{}|^2$ as $t \to \infty$?

\begin{enumerate}
\item For the multiplication operator $V$,
the eigenvalues are the $\omega(n)$ and the
$\delta_n$ form a complete orthonormal system
of eigenfunctions. Hence the probability to
go from $0$ to $n \neq 0$ is $0$ for all
$t$, and to stay at $0$ is $1$: no
propagation at all.
\item For $A$ on $\ell^2(\mathbb{Z})$, the
functions $\phi_k(n) = e^{ink}$ satisfy
$A\phi_k = 2\cos(k)\,\phi_k$. They are
generalized eigenfunctions (bounded,
oscillating, not in $\ell^2$), and the
energies $E_k = 2\cos(k)$ fill $[-2,2]$.
The Fourier transform
$\mathcal{F} : \ell^2(\mathbb{Z}) \to
L^2([0,2\pi])$ conjugates $A$ to
multiplication by $2\cos(k)$, and
$$
\left\langle{} f(A)\delta_0,\delta_0
\right\rangle{}
= \int_{-2}^{2} f(E)\,
\frac{dE}{\pi\sqrt{4-E^2}}.
$$
So the spectral measure of $\delta_0$ is
$\mu_{\delta_0}(dE) =
\chi_{[-2,2]}(E)\,dE/(\pi\sqrt{4-E^2})$, and
$|\left\langle{} e^{-itA}\delta_0,\delta_0
\right\rangle{}|^2 = |\hat\mu(t)|^2 \to 0$ by
the Riemann--Lebesgue lemma; in fact it is
$O(1/t)$, through a Bessel function. Spoken:
the singularities at $\pm 2$ are integrable;
this is ballistic transport, the fastest
possible spreading, linear in $t$, and it is
deterministic.
\end{enumerate}

\medskip\noindent
{\bf In general: resolvents.} Without
explicit formulas, Cauchy's integral formula
suggests resolvents. For $z$ in the upper
half plane $\mathbb{C}^+$ put
$$
F(z) = \left\langle{} (H-z)^{-1}\psi,\psi
\right\rangle{}.
$$
Then $\operatorname{Im} F(z) =
\operatorname{Im} z\,
\|(H-z)^{-1}\psi\|^2 > 0$ when $\psi \neq 0$.

\begin{remark}
\label{remark-matos-resolvent-slide}
The slide, as photographed, writes
$F(z) - F(\bar z)$ as $2i$ times the square
of an inner product, apparently without the
factor $\operatorname{Im} z$ and with the
inner product in place of the norm; the
identity above (from the resolvent identity)
is my correction.
\end{remark}

\begin{theorem}[Herglotz representation]
\label{theorem-herglotz}
An analytic function
$F : \mathbb{C}^+ \to \mathbb{C}^+$ (a
\emph{Herglotz function}) has a unique
representation
$$
F(z) = a + bz + \int_{\mathbb{R}}
\Big(\frac{1}{\lambda - z}
- \frac{\lambda}{1+\lambda^2}\Big)
d\mu(\lambda),
$$
with $a \in \mathbb{R}$, $b \ge 0$, and
$\mu$ a positive Borel measure with
$\int d\mu(\lambda)/(1+\lambda^2) < \infty$.
\end{theorem}

\noindent
Spoken: for resolvents the measure is finite
and the formula reduces to
$\int d\mu(\lambda)/(\lambda - z)$; the
absolutely continuous and pure point parts
of $\mu$ can be read off from the boundary
values of $\operatorname{Im} F$, the
singular continuous part is harder.
Examples: for $A$ the measure is the arcsine
density above; for $V$,
$\mu_{V,\delta_0} = \delta_{\omega(0)}$ and
$\mu_{V,\psi} = \sum_n |\psi(n)|^2
\delta_{\omega(n)}$. Message:
$\left\langle{} f(H)\varphi,\psi
\right\rangle{} = \int f(E)\,
d\mu_{\varphi,\psi}(E)$, and these measures
carry all the information about the
operator.

\medskip\noindent
{\bf Long range operators.} Let
$H_{\alpha,\omega} = T + \lambda V_\omega$ on
$\ell^2(\mathbb{Z}^d)$, with
$$
\frac{c_{\alpha,d}}{|m-n|^{d+2\alpha}}
\le |T(m,n)| \le
\frac{C_{\alpha,d}}{|m-n|^{d+2\alpha}},
$$
and $(\omega(n))$ i.i.d.\ with bounded
density. Physical predictions for $\lambda$
large: the probabilities
$|\left\langle{} e^{-itH}\delta_0,\delta_n
\right\rangle{}|^2$ decay polynomially in
$|n|$, uniformly in $t$; eigenfunctions
decay like $|n|^{-(d+2\alpha)}$; and the
system shows some transport. Spoken: the
disorder localizes, but the long jumps keep
throwing the electron far with small
probability, so the decay cannot be better
than polynomial.

The spreading is measured by the moments
$$
M_q(T) = \frac{2}{T}\sum_{n \in
\mathbb{Z}^d} |n|^q \int_0^\infty
e^{-2t/T}\,
\mathbb{E}\big(|\left\langle{} \delta_n,
e^{itH_\omega}\delta_0 \right\rangle{}|^2
\big)\,dt.
$$

\begin{theorem}[Disertori--Rojas-Molina--Maturana Escobar, 2024]
\label{theorem-drm-upper}
Let $\alpha > 0$ and
$s \in (d/(d+2\alpha),1)$. There is
$\lambda_0$ such that for $\lambda >
\lambda_0$,
$$
\mathbb{E}\big(|\left\langle{} \delta_n,
e^{itH_\omega}\delta_0 \right\rangle{}|^2
\big) \le \frac{C_s}{|n|^{s(d+2\alpha)}}.
$$
On average eigenfunctions decay at most
like $|n|^{-(d+2\alpha)}$, and $M_q(T) <
\infty$ for $q < 2\alpha$.
\end{theorem}

\begin{theorem}[Hislop--Matos--Rojas-Molina, 2026]
\label{theorem-hmr-lower}
Let $\alpha > d/2$ and
$s \in (2d/(d+2\alpha),1)$. There is
$\lambda_1$ such that for $\lambda >
\lambda_1$,
$$
\frac{c_s}{|n|^{s(2d+4\alpha)}} \le
\mathbb{E}\big(|\left\langle{} \delta_n,
e^{itH_\omega}\delta_0 \right\rangle{}|^2
\big).
$$
On average eigenfunctions decay at least
like $|n|^{-(2d+4\alpha)}$, and
$M_q(T) = +\infty$ for $q \ge d + 4\alpha$.
\end{theorem}

\noindent
References: neither paper was found in my
search, so both statements are as on the
slides, not checked. Spoken: the second is
on arXiv and submitted; the collaboration
with Rojas-Molina began in Rio. The lower
bound was harder than the upper bound;
$d/2$ is the critical parameter for long
range problems; and the corollary
$M_q(T) = \infty$ is the most interesting
part: genuine transport, though not
ballistic. The gap between $2\alpha$ and
$d + 4\alpha$ remains open, and so do
large deviation questions.

\medskip\noindent
{\bf Ideas of the proof.} It suffices to
estimate the Green function
$G(m,n;z) = \left\langle{} \delta_m,
(H-z)^{-1}\delta_n \right\rangle{}$, since
$$
\int_0^\infty e^{-2t/T}\,
\mathbb{E}\big(|\left\langle{} \delta_n,
e^{itH_\omega}\delta_0 \right\rangle{}|^2
\big)\,dt
= \frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{\mathbb{R}}
\mathbb{E}\big(|G(n,0;E+i/T)|^2\big)\,dE.
$$
With $\left\langle{} n \right\rangle{} =
\sqrt{1 + n_1^2 + \cdots + n_d^2}$, the key
estimate (same authors, same hypotheses) is
$$
\frac{A}{\left\langle{} n
\right\rangle{}^{s(d+2\alpha)}}
\le \mathbb{E}\big(|G(0,n;z)|^s\big)
$$
uniformly in $z \in D(0,\lambda R)
\setminus \mathbb{R}$ and $n \in
\mathbb{Z}^d$. Theme: in large disorder the
shortest path, the immediate jump from $0$
to $n$, matters most. The direct jump
contributes about
$|n|^{-s(d+2\alpha)}\lambda^{-2s}$, a path
through an intermediate site $k$ contributes
at most $|n|^{-s(d+2\alpha)}\lambda^{-3s}$,
and so on, so the direct jump dominates for
large $\lambda$. The tool is the fractional
moment method of Aizenman and Molchanov
(Comm. Math. Phys., 1993, checked), with
expansions over self-avoiding paths.

\medskip\noindent
{\bf Is science artificial?} The talk ended
with two sentences of Henri Poincar\'e (from
\emph{O valor da ci\^encia}, as said in the
talk): science predicts, and its predictions
are often belied, but ``this is a prediction
which at least will never be belied''; and
there is no precise border between the brute
fact and the scientific fact. Spoken: he
learned of the book from a lecture by
Marcelo Viana.

\medskip\noindent
{\bf Questions (spoken).}
\begin{enumerate}
\item Probabilities of successive positions
at several times: upper bounds follow from
H\"older's inequality, lower bounds are hard
because of strong correlations.
\item Relation with optimal transport (Monge,
Villani)? Not really: here transport means

\end{enumerate}

\section{A new estimate for the first eigenvalue of the Laplacian of hypersurfaces}
\label{section-jimenez-first-eigenvalue}

\noindent
Asun Jim\'enez Grande, UFF.
XXIII Oktobermat, PUC-Rio.

Sex 30 out 2026, 13:00--14:00.

12\textsuperscript{o} andar do Edif\'icio
Leme, Sala de Reuni\~oes do Decanato do CTC.

\medskip\noindent
{\bf Title on the poster.} Uma nova
estimativa para o primeiro autovalor do
laplaciano de hipersuperf\'icies. No
abstract was available.

\medskip\noindent
{\bf Sources of these notes.} One photograph
of a slide and an automatic transcript
(whisper.cpp, Portuguese) of the audio. The
talk was in Portuguese, the slides in
English. Most statements are reconstructed
from the transcript and checked against the
papers cited; spoken remarks are marked.

\medskip\noindent
{\bf Setting.} Let $\Sigma^m$ be a closed
hypersurface in a compact Riemannian
manifold, and consider the eigenvalue
problem $\Delta f + \lambda f = 0$ on
$\Sigma$. The spectrum is discrete,
$0 = \lambda_0 < \lambda_1 \le \lambda_2
\le \cdots$, and the talk is about the
first nonzero eigenvalue $\lambda_1$, which
is related to stability and to area
minimizing questions. Spoken: a surface is
\emph{minimal} when its mean curvature
vanishes, like a soap film on a wire; most
of the talk is about minimal hypersurfaces
in the round sphere $\mathbb{S}^{m+1}$.

\medskip\noindent
{\bf Yau's conjecture.} From the slide:
\begin{enumerate}
\item (Takahashi, 1966.) If
$\Sigma^m \to \mathbb{S}^{m+1} \subset
\mathbb{R}^{m+2}$ is a minimal embedding,
the restrictions of the coordinate
functions satisfy $\Delta x_i = -m x_i$, so
$\lambda_1(\Sigma) \le m$.
\item (Yau's conjecture, 1982.)
$\lambda_1(\Sigma) = m$, which is also the
Ricci curvature of $\mathbb{S}^{m+1}$.
\end{enumerate}
Spoken: embeddedness is needed, since there
are immersed minimal examples with
$\lambda_1 < m$. The conjecture holds for
the totally geodesic spheres and the
Clifford tori.

\medskip\noindent
{\bf Known results (as presented).}
\begin{enumerate}
\item Choi and Wang (1983), using Reilly's
formula: $\lambda_1 \ge m/2$ for embedded
minimal hypersurfaces; more generally a
bound in terms of a lower bound for the
Ricci curvature of the ambient manifold.
\item Barros and Bessa: an improvement
$m/2 + {}$ (a term depending on a harmonic
extension of a first eigenfunction).
\item Choe and Soret: the conjecture holds
for minimal surfaces invariant under
suitable reflections (Lawson and
Karcher--Pinkall--Sterling examples).
\item Tang and Yan, after Muto and others:
the conjecture holds for minimal
isoparametric hypersurfaces.
\item Duncan, Sire and Spruck:
$\lambda_1 \ge m/2 + a_m/(\Lambda^6 + b_m)$,
where $\Lambda = \max_\Sigma |A|$ is the
maximum norm of the second fundamental
form.
\end{enumerate}
Checked, as cited in Y. Zhao,
arXiv:2603.20890: Choi--Wang, Choe--Soret,
Tang--Yan (\emph{Isoparametric foliation and
Yau conjecture on the first eigenvalue}, J.
Differential Geom. 2013, arXiv:1201.0666),
and Duncan--Sire--Spruck
(arXiv:2308.12235, Theorem 1.1, with
$a_m \ge (m-1)m^2/32000$ and
$b_m \le 5m^2/216$). Barros--Bessa not
checked.

\medskip\noindent
{\bf Isoparametric hypersurfaces.} Spoken:
$\Sigma$ is \emph{isoparametric} if it is a
regular level set of a function $F$ with
$|\nabla F|^2$ and $\Delta F$ both functions
of $F$; equivalently, the parallel
hypersurfaces at constant distance all have
constant mean curvature; in space forms,
equivalently, all principal curvatures are
constant. In the sphere the number of
distinct principal curvatures can only be
$1$, $2$, $3$, $4$ or $6$ (M\"unzner, not
named in the talk). The case of four
principal curvatures was the last one, and
it differs because those hypersurfaces need
not be homogeneous.

\medskip\noindent
{\bf Chern's conjecture.} A closed minimal
hypersurface of the sphere with constant
$|A|$ is isoparametric. So Chern's
conjecture together with Tang--Yan would
give Yau's conjecture for constant $|A|$
(Tang--Yan, as cited by Zhao). Spoken:
nothing is known about Chern's conjecture
in general.

\begin{theorem}[Jim\'enez--Tapia--Zhou]
\label{theorem-jimenez-tapia-zhou}
Let $\Sigma$ be a closed embedded minimal
hypersurface in $\mathbb{S}^{m+1}$ and
$\Lambda = \max_\Sigma |A|$. Then
$$
\lambda_1(\Sigma) > \frac{m}{2}
+ \frac{m(m+1)}{32(12\Lambda+m+11)^2+8}.
$$
If $\Lambda \le \sqrt{m}$, then
$\lambda_1(\Sigma) = m$.
\end{theorem}

\noindent
Checked: A. Jim\'enez, C. Tapia Chinchay and
D. Zhou, \emph{A lower bound for the first
eigenvalue of a minimal hypersurface in the
sphere}, arXiv:2405.20545 (May 2024),
abstract; published in Rev. Mat.
Iberoam. (volume not checked). The case
$\Lambda \le \sqrt{m}$ is the known
rigidity case (spheres and Clifford tori).
Spoken: Carlos Tapia, a former student, is
now at PUC in Lima; Detang Zhou is a
colleague at UFF. Compared with
Duncan--Sire--Spruck, the power of
$\Lambda$ in the denominator is $2$ instead
of $6$, so the bound is better for all
large $\Lambda$; the speaker would prefer a
bound depending only on the dimension or the
topology. In dimension $2$ the norm of $A$
can be estimated by the Euler
characteristic, which gives a topological
bound.

\medskip\noindent
{\bf Idea of the proof (spoken).} The
embedded hypersurface divides the sphere
into two domains; choose the one where a
suitable quantity has the right sign. Extend
a first eigenfunction $f$ harmonically to
that domain, $u$, and use Reilly's formula,
which for minimal hypersurfaces in the
sphere becomes an inequality. The key input
is an inequality of Barros--Bessa type: a
quadratic polynomial in $t$, with
coefficients involving $\lambda_1$ and the
Rayleigh quotient of $u$, is positive for
all $t$. Optimizing in $t$ gives
$\lambda_1 > m/2 + {}$ (a term depending on
the Rayleigh quotient). The hard part is an
upper bound for that Rayleigh quotient, a
Poincar\'e inequality in the reverse
direction. It is obtained with tube
coordinates around $\Sigma$, given by the
normal exponential map up to the focal
distance (controlled by the maximal
principal curvature), with a test function
built by perturbing the vertical extension
of $f$, and with estimates of the mean
curvature of parallel hypersurfaces.

\medskip\noindent
{\bf Other ambient spaces (spoken).}
\begin{enumerate}
\item Einstein manifolds with sectional
curvature bounded in a suitable range, and
minimal hypersurfaces with \emph{adapted
curvature}: there is a basis
simultaneously diagonalizing the second
fundamental form and the ambient curvature
operator in the normal direction. A
similar lower bound holds, with an upper
bound for $\lambda_1$ in place of $m$.
Examples: $\mathbb{CP}^n$ and
$\mathbb{HP}^n$, where curvature adapted
hypersurfaces are Hopf hypersurfaces (the
rotated normal is a principal direction).
\item Isoparametric minimal hypersurfaces in
the base of a Riemannian submersion from a
sphere with totally geodesic fibres: the
lift is isoparametric in the sphere, where
Yau's conjecture holds, and the spectrum of
$\Sigma$ is contained in that of its lift;
this gives a bound for $\lambda_1$ in
$\mathbb{CP}^n$ and $\mathbb{HP}^n$ better
than the previous ones.
\end{enumerate}
These results are in Carlos Tapia's thesis,
not yet written as a paper (spoken).

\medskip\noindent
{\bf Questions (spoken).}
\begin{enumerate}
\item Why only $1,2,3,4,6$ principal
curvatures? The speaker did not know the
reason.
\item A remark from the audience: one
should expect a reverse Poincar\'e
inequality, since minimal surfaces are in a
sense optimizers of Poincar\'e-type
inequalities.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Degree of the polar map: a
conjecture of Dimca--Papadima}
\label{section-degree-polar-map}

\noindent
Hamid Hassanzadeh, UFRJ.
XXIII Oktobermat, PUC-Rio.

Sex 30 out 2026, 14:15--15:15.

12\textsuperscript{o} andar do Edif\'icio
Leme, Sala de Reuni\~oes do Decanato do CTC.

\medskip\noindent
{\bf Title on the poster.} Degree of polar
map: a conjecture of Dimca--Papadima.
No abstract was available.

\medskip\noindent
{\bf Sources of these notes.} An automatic
transcript (whisper.cpp, Portuguese) of the
audio, which is poor in places; two
photographs of the main theorem slide; and
Daniel's notes. The talk was in Portuguese,
the slides in English. Daniel's question
after the talk was not recorded. Results are
attributed as in the talk; references
checked against a source say so. The main
theorem is work in progress of the speaker
with a former student and other
collaborators (names not recoverable from the
audio); I found no preprint. The speaker
calls it the start of an algebraic attack on
these problems. See also the notes of his
minicourse \emph{Birational maps from the
commutative algebra viewpoint} (OPEGA 2026)
in HP, and of Nivaldo Medeiros's talk on
homaloidal hypersurfaces at the IV Jornadas
de Lefschetz, which cover the same objects.

\medskip\noindent
{\bf Two cubics.} The talk opened with two
plane cubics: the smooth Fermat cubic and the
triangle of lines,
$$
f = x^3 + y^3 + z^3,
\qquad
g = xyz.
$$
Usually the smooth one is considered the
nicer curve. The point of the talk: other
invariants prefer singular curves.

\medskip\noindent
{\bf The polar map.} For a homogeneous
polynomial $f$ of degree $d$ in
$x_0, \ldots, x_n$, the partial derivatives
define the \emph{polar map} (or gradient
map)
$$
\nabla f : \mathbb{P}^n \dashrightarrow
\mathbb{P}^n,
\quad
p \longmapsto
\Big[\frac{\partial f}{\partial x_0}(p) :
\cdots :
\frac{\partial f}{\partial x_n}(p)\Big],
$$
defined away from the common zeros of the
derivatives. Projective space is used
because the equations are homogeneous: the
solutions are lines through the origin. If
the map is dominant, its \emph{degree} is
the number of preimages of a general point
of the target: there is a dense open set of
the target over which the map is
$k$-to-$1$, and $k$ is the degree.
\begin{enumerate}
\item For the Fermat cubic,
$\nabla f = [x^2 : y^2 : z^2]$, and the
general point $[1:1:1]$ has the four
preimages $[1:\pm1:\pm1]$ (the eight sign
choices give four points of
$\mathbb{P}^2$). So $\nabla f$ has degree
$4$.
\item For the triangle,
$\nabla g = [yz : xz : xy]$. Dividing by
$xyz$ gives $[1/x : 1/y : 1/z]$, so the
general point has one preimage: $\nabla g$
is the standard quadratic Cremona map. It is
not defined at the three coordinate points,
but it is an isomorphism between dense open
sets.
\end{enumerate}
A map which induces an isomorphism of dense
open sets is \emph{birational}. A polynomial
(or hypersurface) whose polar map is
birational is called \emph{homaloidal}.

\medskip\noindent
{\bf More singularities, smaller degree.}
For a hypersurface $V(f) \subset
\mathbb{P}^n$ of degree $d$ with only
isolated singular points,
$$
\deg \nabla f
= (d-1)^n - \sum_{p} \mu_p,
$$
where $\mu_p$ is the Milnor number at the
singular point $p$. The term $(d-1)^n$ is
the maximum possible, the number of
solutions of $n$ general equations of degree
$d-1$ in $\mathbb{P}^n$. For the triangle:
three nodes with $\mu = 1$, so
$\deg \nabla g = 2^2 - 3 = 1$. Checked: this
is the formula in J. Huh, \emph{Milnor
numbers of projective hypersurfaces with
isolated singularities}, Duke Math. J. 163
(2014), no.~8; arXiv:1210.2690.

\medskip\noindent
{\bf Topology: Dimca--Papadima.} The talk is
about A. Dimca and S. Papadima,
\emph{Hypersurface complements, Milnor
fibers and higher homotopy groups of
arrangements}, Annals of Math. 158 (2003),
473--507 (checked). As stated in the talk:
the complement $U = \mathbb{P}^n \setminus
V(f)$ is obtained, up to homotopy, from
$U \cap H$, for a general hyperplane $H$, by
attaching $\deg \nabla f$ cells of dimension
$n$. So the degree of the polar map is a
topological invariant. In particular it
depends only on the reduced hypersurface, as
Dolgachev had conjectured. (Checked as cited
in Siersma--Steenbrink--Tib\u{a}r,
arXiv:1805.08175, which writes the
Dimca--Papadima result as a rank formula for
the homology of $V \setminus H$. I did not
read the cell statement in the Annals paper
itself.) Spoken, in answer to a question:
iterating with further hyperplane sections
describes the homotopy type of the
complement completely; Huh used the sequence
of these numbers (the projective degrees).

\medskip\noindent
{\bf The conjecture.} Homaloidal means the
bouquet of $n$-spheres has a single sphere.
Smooth hypersurfaces have many. Dimca and
Papadima conjectured that isolated
singularities are not enough to bring the
number down to one.

\begin{theorem}[Huh, conjectured by
Dimca--Papadima]
\label{theorem-huh-dimca-papadima}
There are no homaloidal hypersurfaces of
degree $d \ge 3$ in $\mathbb{P}^n$,
$n \ge 3$, with only isolated singular
points.
\end{theorem}

\noindent
Checked: Huh, Duke 2014, arXiv:1210.2690,
which also says that polar degree $1$ with
isolated singularities occurs only for
smooth quadrics or certain plane cubics. In
the plane the answer was already known: the
reduced homaloidal plane curves are, up to
linear change of coordinates, exactly three
(I. Dolgachev, \emph{Polar Cremona
transformations}, arXiv:math/0005048; list as
recorded in HP from Nivaldo's talk, not
rechecked in Dolgachev's paper):
\begin{enumerate}
\item a smooth conic;
\item a smooth conic with a tangent line;
\item three nonconcurrent lines.
\end{enumerate}
Spoken: the list was a surprise when
Dolgachev's paper appeared, since so few
cases were expected.

\medskip\noindent
{\bf Quasi-homogeneous singularities.}
Spoken: Dimca was the first to attack the
conjecture, and proved it under the extra
assumption that all singularities are
quasi-homogeneous (reference not given in
the talk, not checked). A singularity is
\emph{quasi-homogeneous} if, in suitable
local coordinates, its equation is
homogeneous for some positive weights. The
example in the talk was a cusp: locally
$x^2 - z^3$, homogeneous when $x$ has weight
$3$ and $z$ has weight $2$.

\medskip\noindent
{\bf Huh.} Spoken: June Huh (Fields Medal
2022) proved the conjecture in the Duke paper
above, in three steps: a generalisation of a
topological theorem of L\^e, a reduction to
quasi-homogeneous singularities, and Dimca's
case. The talk also mentioned Huh's earlier
paper using the same projective degrees for
graphs, \emph{Milnor numbers of projective
hypersurfaces and the chromatic polynomial of
graphs}, arXiv:1008.4749 (journal not
checked).

\medskip\noindent
{\bf Polar degree two.} Huh then asked which
hypersurfaces with isolated singularities
have polar degree $2$, and conjectured a list
(\emph{Conjecture 20} of the Duke paper,
named after its number there).

\begin{theorem}[Siersma--Steenbrink--Tib\u{a}r]
\label{theorem-polar-degree-two}
Huh's list of projective hypersurfaces with
only isolated singularities and polar degree
$2$ is complete. Such hypersurfaces exist
only for
$$
(n,d) \in \{(2,3),(2,4),(2,5),(3,3)\}.
$$
\end{theorem}

\noindent
Checked: D. Siersma, J. Steenbrink and
M. Tib\u{a}r, \emph{On Huh's conjectures for
the polar degree}, J. Algebraic Geom. 30
(2021); arXiv:1805.08175. The list contains
three normal cubic surfaces (singularity
types $E_6$, $A_5 + A_1$, $3A_2$), plane
cubics (a cuspidal cubic; a conic with a
secant line), six plane quartic
configurations, and one plane quintic. They
also prove that for each $k \ge 2$ only
finitely many $(n,d)$ occur with polar degree
$k$. The talk's attribution of this proof
(names and year) was not recoverable from the
audio.

\medskip\noindent
{\bf An algebraic approach.} Spoken: nobody
went beyond degree $2$, and there is no
reason for degree $2$ to be special. So the
speaker and collaborators looked for
commutative algebra methods for every finite
degree. The translation has three steps.
\begin{enumerate}
\item The degree of the map, via
\emph{mixed multiplicities}: the
multiplicity theory of bigraded rings (the
Rees algebra of the base ideal), with a
bivariate Hilbert polynomial in place of the
usual one. For this the map need not be a
gradient: any rational map
$F = [f_0 : \cdots : f_n]$ by forms of the
same degree will do.
\item Quasi-homogeneity, via a local
condition: over a field of characteristic
zero, an isolated singularity is
quasi-homogeneous exactly when the Jacobian
ideal is locally a complete intersection.
The ideal is generated by $n+1$ derivatives
in a local ring of dimension $n$, and one
generator is redundant. (Spoken; this is
Saito's characterisation, that $f$ lies in
its local Jacobian ideal exactly in the
quasi-homogeneous case; reference not given
and not checked.)
\item Linkage, explained below.
\end{enumerate}
Characteristic zero is needed only to return
to geometry; the algebra works over any
infinite field.

\medskip\noindent
{\bf Linkage.} The talk introduced linkage
with the opening example of the famous paper
of Peskine and Szpiro. On a smooth cubic
surface $S \subset \mathbb{P}^3$ take one of
its $27$ lines $L$. A plane through $L$ cuts
$S$ in $L$ and a conic. Intersecting further
surfaces through such curves with $S$ and
taking the residual curve produces new
curves; the talk's description of the next
steps is not clear in the audio. Spoken:
finding curves with given properties this
way was a 19th century problem, and linkage
gives the theory behind it.

\begin{definition}
\label{definition-linkage-hamid}
Let $R$ be a Gorenstein local or graded ring.
Ideals $I$ and $J$ are \emph{linked} if there
is a regular sequence
$\mathbf{a} = (a_1, \ldots, a_g)$ inside
$I \cap J$ with
$$
J = (\mathbf{a}) : I,
\qquad
I = (\mathbf{a}) : J.
$$
\end{definition}

\noindent
Geometrically, the complete intersection
$V(\mathbf{a})$ is the union of $V(I)$ and
$V(J)$. Linkage theory tells how geometric
and algebraic properties pass from $I$ to
$J$; for example (Peskine--Szpiro) $R/I$ is
Cohen--Macaulay exactly when $R/J$ is. See
the linkage section of HP's commutative
algebra notebook, and Claudia Polini's talk
at the 16th ALGA meeting, for more.
Reference: C. Peskine and L. Szpiro,
\emph{Liaison des vari\'et\'es alg\'ebriques
I}, Invent. Math. 26 (1974), 271--302
(bibliographic data checked; the talk dated
it 1976 and Daniel's notes 1978).

\medskip\noindent
Spoken: under the generically complete
intersection condition, the colon $(\mathbf
a) : I$ already equals the saturation
$(\mathbf a) : I^\infty$; the degree of the
polar map then equals a multiplicity of the
linked ideal, in the quasi-homogeneous case.
This is what brings in linkage.

\medskip\noindent
{\bf Main theorem.} From the slide.

\begin{theorem}[Hassanzadeh et al., work in
progress]
\label{theorem-hassanzadeh-main}
Let $k$ be an infinite field and
$F : \mathbb{P}^n_k \dashrightarrow
\mathbb{P}^n_k$ a dominant rational map
defined by homogeneous polynomials of degree
$d-1$. Assume that the base locus of $F$
consists only of points and that the ideal
of the base locus is generically a complete
intersection. Then
$$
\deg F \ge (n-1)(d-2).
$$
Moreover:
\begin{enumerate}
\item if $\deg F = 1$, then the ideal of $F$
is a linearly presented perfect ideal of
height $2$ generated by $3$ quadrics;
\item if $\deg F = (n-1)(d-2)$, then the
ideal of $F$ is saturated and
$(n,d) = (2,2)$, $(3,2)$, or
$(n,d) = (2,d)$ with $d \ge 3$ (part of this
line was hidden in the photographs), and
$R/I$ has a free resolution beginning
$$
0 \to R(-2d-1) \to
R(-d-1) \oplus R(-2d)^2 \to \cdots
\to R/I \to 0
$$
(the middle terms were hidden).
\end{enumerate}
\end{theorem}

\noindent
Spoken: the bound recovers all known cases.
For $\deg F = 1$ it gives
$(n-1)(d-2) \le 1$, so $d = 2$, or $n = 2$
and $d = 3$, which matches Dolgachev's list
and Huh's theorem. The map need not be a
gradient, and the field is arbitrary
infinite. Beyond the degree, linkage
determines the structure, given here as free
resolutions rather than equations.

\medskip\noindent
{\bf Daniel's notes, with answers.} The
explanations below are mine, not from the
talk, unless marked otherwise; they are
sketches, not checked by anyone else.

\medskip\noindent
{\it 1. ``More singularities = less degree of
the dual map; degree 1 = birational map;
there are 3 exceptions.''} A correction of
terms: the map is the \emph{polar} (gradient)
map $\mathbb{P}^n \dashrightarrow
\mathbb{P}^n$. Its restriction to the
hypersurface is the Gauss map, whose image
is the dual variety; that restriction is the
``dual map''. The three exceptions are
Dolgachev's three plane curves above, and the
triangle $xyz$ gives the standard Cremona
map. With the smooth quadrics, which are
homaloidal in every dimension, they are all
homaloidal hypersurfaces with only isolated
singularities.

\medskip\noindent
{\it 2. The Dimca--Papadima conjecture as
noted.} The note reads: if $f$ has degree $d$
with at most isolated singularities, the
polar map has degree at least $2$ except in
three cases. Precisely: for $n \ge 3$ and
$d \ge 3$ there are no exceptions
(Theorem~\ref{theorem-huh-dimca-papadima});
the three exceptions are plane curves, and
the quadrics ($d = 2$) are exceptions in
every $n$.

\medskip\noindent
{\it 3. ``Is the image under the dual map
good in any sense? Why do we worry about
constructing this birational map?''} The
image of the polar map is all of
$\mathbb{P}^n$ whenever the Hessian
determinant is not identically zero; the
image of the hypersurface itself is its dual
variety. The reason to care is that
homaloidal hypersurfaces are a source of
Cremona transformations, which are hard to
construct for $n \ge 3$: elements of
$\mathrm{Bir}(\mathbb{P}^n)$ with a single
equation behind them. Conversely (Nivaldo's
talk) every Cremona map of $\mathbb{P}^n$
gives a homaloidal hypersurface in
$\mathbb{P}^{2n+1}$.

\medskip\noindent
{\it 4. Why the answer to Daniel's question
recalled $\mathrm{Bir}(\mathbb{P}^2)$.} My
guess, since the exchange was not recorded:
part 1 of the main theorem says that in this
setting a birational map is a plane quadratic
Cremona map, given by $3$ quadrics through
$3$ points with linear syzygies
(Hilbert--Burch).
By the theorem of Noether and Castelnuovo,
$\mathrm{Bir}(\mathbb{P}^2)$ is generated by
$\mathrm{PGL}_3$ and the standard quadratic
map $[yz : xz : xy]$ (see the notebook
\emph{Factoring Cremona transformations}).
So the degree-$1$ maps of the theorem are
the generators of the plane Cremona group.

\medskip\noindent
{\it 5. ``Linkage allows Hamid to understand
rational dominant maps (if degree 1,
birational).'' What is the relationship
between linkage and birational maps?}
Here is the mechanism, as I understand it.
Let $F = [f_0 : \cdots : f_n]$ be given by
forms of degree $e = d-1$, with base ideal
$I = (f_0, \ldots, f_n)$ defining finitely
many points. Fix a general point
$q = [q_0 : \cdots : q_n]$ with
$q_0 \neq 0$. The points mapping to $q$, and
the base points, are the zeros of
$$
g_j = q_0 f_j - q_j f_0,
\qquad j = 1, \ldots, n,
$$
the $2 \times 2$ minors of the matrix with
rows $(f_0, \ldots, f_n)$ and
$(q_0, \ldots, q_n)$. These are $n$ forms of
degree $e$ in $n+1$ variables, so
$\mathbf a = (g_1, \ldots, g_n)$ is a
complete intersection of $e^n = (d-1)^n$
points counted with multiplicity, and
$\mathbf a \subset I$. Its zero set splits
into the base locus $V(I)$ and the fibre
$F^{-1}(q)$: the fibre is the
\emph{residual} of the base locus in the
complete intersection,
$$
I_{F^{-1}(q)} = (\mathbf a) : I^\infty .
$$
Under the hypotheses of the main theorem the
colon equals the saturation (spoken, see
above), so the fibre and the base locus are
linked by $\mathbf a$. At each base point
$p$ where $I$ is locally a complete
intersection, the $n$ general combinations
$g_j$ generate $I_p$ (Nakayama), so the base
locus takes exactly its own multiplicity out
of the complete intersection, and
$$
\deg F
= (d-1)^n - e\big(R/I^{\mathrm{sat}}\big).
$$
For the polar map with quasi-homogeneous
singularities, the local length of the
Jacobian algebra (the Tjurina number) equals
the Milnor number, and this is the formula
$(d-1)^n - \sum \mu_p$ above.

So: \emph{$F$ is birational exactly when its
base locus is linked to a single reduced
point.} Linkage then transfers the structure
of that point to the base ideal. Example:
$n = 2$, $d = 3$. Two general conics through
the base points meet in $4$ points; one is
the fibre, so the base locus is $3$ points,
whose ideal has the Hilbert--Burch
resolution
$$
0 \to R(-3)^2 \to R(-2)^3 \to R
\to R/I \to 0 .
$$
For the three coordinate points, the
$2 \times 2$ minors of
$$
\begin{pmatrix}
x & 0 \\
-y & y \\
0 & -z
\end{pmatrix}
$$
are $yz$, $-xz$, $xy$: three quadrics,
linearly presented, perfect of height $2$,
as in part 1 of the main theorem, and the map
is the standard Cremona map. This is how I
read Daniel's note: linkage relates the
\emph{base locus} of a map to its
\emph{general fibre}, and birationality is
the statement that the fibre is one point.

\medskip\noindent
{\it 6. ``Is it true that the Peskine--Szpiro
paper (birth of linkage) posed the question
of whether a curve is linked to two
different cubics in $\mathbb{P}^3$?''} Not
confirmed. The paper is from 1974 (Invent.
Math. 26), not 1978, and the talk said it
opens with the cubic surface example above.
I could not read the paper itself, and the
audio does not settle whether a question was
posed there. What is standard: a curve lies
on many complete intersections, so it has
many direct links; by Rao's theorem the even
liaison class of a curve in $\mathbb{P}^3$
is determined by its Rao module, whichever
links are used (see the linkage section of
the commutative algebra notebook).

\medskip\noindent
{\it 7. Homaloidal versus birational.} By
definition here (and in Nivaldo's talk) a
hypersurface is homaloidal when its polar map
is birational. The older meaning: a linear
system on $\mathbb{P}^n$ is
\emph{homaloidal} when it defines a Cremona
transformation; its members are the
preimages of hyperplanes (``homaloids''). A
homaloidal hypersurface is one whose polar
linear system, spanned by the partial
derivatives, is homaloidal in this older
sense.

\medskip\noindent
{\it 8. Linkage and smoothings.} Daniel was
advised to try linkage to construct the
smoothing of the Stanley--Reisner scheme of
the Gr\"unbaum sphere. That is a different
use of linkage from this talk's. Here
linkage relates two zero-dimensional schemes
inside one complete intersection: base locus
and fibre. For smoothings one links
subschemes of positive dimension of the same
dimension, hoping that the linked scheme is
simpler (for instance rational) and that
links can be carried along a family. What
the two uses share is the mapping cone: a
link turns a known free resolution into one
for the residual scheme. I did not check the
literature on linkage in families.

\medskip\noindent
{\bf Sources of these notes.} Only an
automatic transcript (whisper.cpp, English)
of the audio; no photographs. The discussion
was in English. The beginning was not
recorded. Names are as heard in the
transcript and may be misspelled;
affiliations are as the panelists described
themselves. Everything below paraphrases
what was said, nothing is checked, and the
opinions are the panelists'.

\medskip\noindent
{\bf Panelists.}
\begin{enumerate}
\item A computer scientist (``Alberto'' in
the transcript) who teaches reinforcement
learning and an algorithms course at
PUC-Rio; his introduction was not recorded.
\item Bianca (surname not recorded), a
computer engineer from PUC-Rio with a PhD
in computer science from UC San Diego,
formerly at IBM Research in the US and in
Brazil and a professor at UFF, now teaching
\emph{Introduction to Artificial
Intelligence} in PUC-Rio's new
undergraduate programme in AI.
\item Glen Wheeler (remote, from
Australia), a geometric analyst at the
University of Wollongong who leads its
mathematics department, with an earlier
career in network security.
\end{enumerate}

\medskip\noindent
{\bf Opening statements.}
\begin{enumerate}
\item First panelist. Language models first
impressed by sounding human; then training
aimed at solving problems, mathematics
included, changed the picture. The issue is
knowledge: a student who copies an answer
learns nothing, and the same happens in
research. Reviewing papers with these tools
is another problem: every tool has a bias,
and identical reviews remove the diversity
of perspectives that reviewing needs.
Humans must stay in control, also because of
responsibility: when generated code fails,
``is it Claude'' to blame?
\item Bianca. AI is older and wider than
language models: recommendation systems,
image analysis for medicine and for
geologists in oil and mining (designed to
augment human experts), and recent weather
models that rival numerical weather
prediction. IBM's Watson was brittle and
domain specific; she did not expect
transformers to become so good, and ``no
one knows why exactly'' they work so well.
Mathematics and programming are special
because outputs can be \emph{verified}: a
proof in a formal language can be checked
by a program, so reinforcement learning can
use correctness as a reward and search huge
spaces of attempts, at large computational
cost. In other sciences checking needs
experiments. She said that the creator of
the formal language used for this
(presumably Lean) was a student of a
PUC-Rio computer science professor
(``Herrmann'' in the transcript).
Companies use mathematics as a benchmark,
as IBM once used chess, not to help
mathematics; and someone must understand
and explain any result.
\item Glen Wheeler. Progress in mathematics
has always been exponential. AI shines a
light on problems the community already
had: more production than anyone can follow,
how careers and CVs are evaluated, what
mathematicians are for. The main output of
mathematical work is not papers but people,
trained to think clearly and abstractly;
``we produce people'', and papers are a
by-product.
\end{enumerate}

\medskip\noindent
{\bf Questions and answers.}
\begin{enumerate}
\item A question on recently announced AI
results (the questioner mentioned claimed
singularities for the Navier--Stokes
equations): people attacking famous
problems discover useful things on the way,
as chemistry came out of alchemy; does
solving the problem directly lose this?
Wheeler: companies are benchmarking, not
helping mathematics. A conjecture central
to his research fellowship was proved by an
OpenAI system (its name is garbled in the
transcript), with no transparency about who
prompted it. The paper is averagely written
but contains ``a genius idea'', a new energy
with a mysterious expression, buried under
technical geometric measure theory; the
community should digest it. He also said
that the paper reuses a method of proof from
one of his papers without citing it, and
that he complained. He has not checked every
detail.
\item What will PUC-Rio's new AI institute
do? Bianca: AI for science, mainly
molecules, chemistry and biology,
multi-modal representation learning, and
applied work with industry and the new
medical school; nothing specific to
mathematics yet.
\item How to keep students from relying on
AI? First panelist: paper and pencil, and
projects followed by tricky oral questions.
Bianca: in her course, presentations in
class and written or oral exams; copying was
a problem in computer science before AI.
Wheeler: universities must show their value;
understanding and speaking can still be
assessed, but there is no good answer yet
for teaching how to \emph{write} mathematics
without secure assessment. In interviews for
Wollongong's curriculum review, employers
said they used to hire mathematicians for
abstract and critical thinking, and that
recent graduates ``just prompt'' and do not
know mathematics; he took this as support
for teaching core skills.
\item How should one treat AI announcements?
Wheeler: his community (geometric analysis)
had organized internationally to discuss AI
beforehand. The consensus: do not treat a
result as true until it is understood, and
encourage expository articles. He tried to
write one and felt like ``a stomach'',
merely digesting output; he will instead
study where the idea comes from and what
else it can do. History of the conjecture,
as he told it: the simplest form was known
in dimensions $2$, $3$, $4$; in 2026 an
AI-assisted team did dimensions $5$ to $9$,
and he proved a version in all dimensions;
the OpenAI paper claims the general, much
stronger form. He hopes for large
mathematical research centres, like those in
physics, employing young mathematicians.
\end{enumerate}

\medskip\noindent
{\bf Closing: one benefit and one risk.}
\begin{enumerate}
\item First panelist. Benefit: we will learn
more and faster. Risk: in education, copying
is now very easy.
\item Bianca. Benefit: democratization of
knowledge, if one stays skeptical and
checks. Risk: loss of control and malicious
use, for instance attacks on systems; there
was much hiring in AI and cybersecurity this
year.
\item Wheeler. Risk: cognitive decline, like
losing the ability to read maps after
relying on GPS. Benefit: a revolution that
forces mathematics to reflect and forge a
new identity, more relevant and impactful;
an opportunity for students now starting a
master's or PhD.
\end{enumerate}
