\section{Heating a gas}
A gas in a cylinder can receive energy
through heating or by compression. Write $Q$
for heat entering the gas and $W$ for work
done by the gas. The first law gives
$$
\Delta U=Q-W.
$$
Clamp the piston and supply ten joules of
heat. With no other work, the internal energy
rises by ten joules. Let the piston move and
let the gas do three joules of work during
the same heat input. Its internal energy now
rises by seven joules.

\section{Counting microscopic arrangements}
Pressure describes a gas without tracking the
momentum delivered by each molecule hitting
the wall. Statistical mechanics connects such
bulk measurements to microscopic states.

For a simple counting example, consider four
independent gas molecules in a box. Each is
equally likely to be in either half. There
are sixteen equally likely side assignments.
Six put two molecules on each side, and only
one puts all four on the left. With $N$
independent molecules the probability of
finding all of them on the left falls to
$2^{-N}$. A macroscopic distribution can be
predictable even when individual positions
fluctuate.

See \cite[Vol. I, Sections 44-1, 44-6 and
45-1]{feynman-lectures}.
