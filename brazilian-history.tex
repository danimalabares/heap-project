\section{Salvador as an entry point}
These notes began from walking through Salvador on September 26, 2026. The
guiding question is how Salvador, Atlantic history, African diasporas,
Catholic institutions, and later Brazilian cities fit into one chronology.

\section{A first colonial timeline}
Portuguese arrival on the coast conventionally begins with Cabral's expedition
in 1500, but sustained colonial occupation developed gradually. Sao Vicente
was founded in 1532. Salvador was founded in 1549 by Tome de Sousa as the seat
of Portuguese colonial government, and Jesuits arrived with him. Sao Paulo
grew from the Jesuit college founded in 1554. Rio de Janeiro was founded in
1565 amid conflict around Guanabara Bay.

Salvador therefore did not mark the first Portuguese presence in Brazil, but
it marked a new phase: a deliberately founded administrative capital. It
remained the colonial capital until 1763, when the seat of government moved
to Rio de Janeiro.

The occupation of the territory later called Brazil was highly uneven. Sugar
made Bahia and Pernambuco early colonial centres; mining later intensified
occupation of the interior. Modern Brazil should not be projected back onto
the sixteenth century as if the whole present territory were already one
uniformly occupied colony.

\section{Religion, education, and colonial power}
The Cathedral Basilica was originally the church of the Jesuit college. This
is a reminder that colonial religious institutions were also educational and
political institutions. The Society of Jesus arrived in 1549 and became
deeply involved in missionary work and education.

\section{Africa is not one place}
A central lesson of the MUNCAB visit was to replace the vague word Africa with
a geographical and historical map. The museum displayed Yoruba regions around
present-day Nigeria and Benin; Akan regions around Ghana and Cote d'Ivoire;
and numerous Central African peoples in the Congo and Angola zones.

These cultural regions do not coincide with modern state borders. People who
arrived in Bahia through the Atlantic trade came from different societies,
languages, political formations, and religious traditions. Brazilian
categories such as Nago, Jeje, Angola, and Congo belong to this diasporic
history and are not simply modern African nationalities.

\section{Bahia and Benin}
The Casa do Benin makes visible the historical Bahia--Benin relationship. Its
key is movement across the Atlantic: forced migration from Africa to Bahia,
the transformation and preservation of African practices in Brazil, and later
return journeys and intellectual, religious, and artistic exchanges.

Pierre Verger's formula of flux and reflux is useful: the question is not only
what travelled from Africa to Brazil, but also what later travelled from
Brazil to West Africa and how both shores changed.

\section{Zumbi and Nzambi}
Zumbi dos Palmares was a historical leader associated with the seventeenth-
century Quilombo dos Palmares and became a major symbol of Black resistance.
He must not be confused with \emph{Nzambi} or \emph{Nzambi a Mpungu}, a
divine name from Central African/Kongo traditions. The similarity of the words
prompted the question, but they are different referents.

\section{Modern cultural memory}
The museums showed that Atlantic and diasporic history is actively interpreted
in religion, visual art, carnival, music, museums, and heritage institutions.
Mae Stella de Oxossi is one example: a twentieth-century religious leader
whose life linked Salvador, Nigerian Yoruba centres, Benin, international
conferences, literature, and debates over religious autonomy.

\section{Questions to develop}
The next layer should study the Atlantic trade to Bahia with actual chronology
and quantitative data: major ports and regions of origin, changes over time,
the sugar economy, abolition, the Reconcavo, and Black institutions in
Salvador. The MUNCAB slogan that Bahia is the most Black state outside Africa
should be checked against IBGE definitions and an explicit comparison
population before being repeated as fact.
