36.22 Fibres and specialization

For flatness of modules, see Commutative Algebra, Section 12.16. Here we study the fibres of a family and how a point on its generic fibre can specialize to its special fibre.

Epiphany: in the category of commutative rings pushout is tensor product. So think of $\mathop{\mathrm{Spec}}$ as a functor from $\mathit{CRing}^{\text{op}}$ to $\mathit{Sch}$, then pushout goes to pullback, and what's an example of a pullback? Fibre! So, the coordinate ring of a fiber is essentially given by the residue field at the point that parametrizes it! (tensored with the coordinate ring of the deformation space). See Commutative Algebra, Proposition 12.15.24 and Remark 12.15.25. There we take the reduced fibre of affine varieties; for scheme-theoretic fibres we keep the tensor product, without removing its nilpotents.

Remark 36.22.1. A formal power series

\[ f=a_0+a_1s+a_2s^2+\cdots \in \mathbb {C}[[s]] \]

is a unit if and only if $a_0\neq 0$. It need not be constant: its inverse may itself be an infinite formal series.

If $fg=1$, comparison of constant terms forces $a_0g(0)=1$, so $a_0\neq 0$. Conversely, write $f=a_0(1+w)$ with $w\in s\mathbb {C}[[s]]$. Then

\[ f^{-1}=\frac{1}{a_0} \sum _{n=0}^{\infty }(-w)^ n. \]

Each coefficient is a finite sum, since $w^ n$ is divisible by $s^ n$. This is the geometric-series argument; no analytic convergence is needed.

See Atiyah–Macdonald, Introduction to Commutative Algebra, Chapter 1, Exercise 5(i). Over a general commutative coefficient ring, the constant term must be a unit, rather than merely nonzero.

Exercise 36.22.2 (The two fibres of a formal family). Let

\[ R=\mathbb {C}[[t]],\qquad K=\mathbb {C}((t)). \]

  1. Show that $\operatorname {Spec}R$ has exactly two points: $\eta =(0)$ and $s=(t)$.

  2. Show that $\eta $ is generic and $s$ is closed.

  3. Compute their residue fields.

  4. For an affine family $\operatorname {Spec}A\to \operatorname {Spec}R$, describe its generic and special fibres using these residue fields.

The base has two points; a family over it has a fibre over each.

Proof.

  1. By definition, points of $\operatorname {Spec}R$ are prime ideals of $R$, not elements of $R$. We determine all of them.

    First, $(0)$ is prime because $R$ is a domain: the product of two nonzero power series has a nonzero leading term.

    Now let $\mathfrak {p}$ be a nonzero prime ideal and choose $0\neq f\in \mathfrak {p}$. Write

    \[ f=a_ nt^ n+a_{n+1}t^{n+1}+\cdots =t^ n u, \qquad a_ n\neq 0. \]

    The series $u$ has nonzero constant term $a_ n$, so it is a unit by Remark 36.22.1. This is the valuation picture from Commutative Algebra, Example 12.28.6.

    Since a proper ideal cannot contain a unit, $n>0$ and $u\notin \mathfrak {p}$. As $t^ n u=f\in \mathfrak {p}$ and $\mathfrak {p}$ is prime, we obtain $t^ n\in \mathfrak {p}$. Applying primality again gives $t\in \mathfrak {p}$, so $(t)\subset \mathfrak {p}$. But evaluation at zero gives

    \[ R/(t)\cong \mathbb {C}. \]

    Hence $(t)$ is maximal, and necessarily $\mathfrak {p}=(t)$. It is itself prime because its quotient is a field. Therefore there are exactly two prime ideals, hence two points:

    \[ \eta =(0),\qquad s=(t). \]

  2. Recall that in the Zariski topology,

    \[ \overline{\{ \mathfrak {p}\} } =V(\mathfrak {p}) =\{ \mathfrak {q}\in \operatorname {Spec}R: \mathfrak {p}\subseteq \mathfrak {q}\} . \]

    Indeed, a closed set $V(I)$ contains $\mathfrak {p}$ precisely when $I\subseteq \mathfrak {p}$; it then contains every prime containing $\mathfrak {p}$. Thus $V(\mathfrak {p})$ is the smallest closed set containing that point.

    Every prime ideal contains $(0)$, so

    \[ \overline{\{ \eta \} }=\operatorname {Spec}R. \]

    This is why $\eta $ is called generic. Since $(t)$ is maximal, the only prime containing it is itself, so $\overline{\{ s\} }=\{ s\} $: the point $s$ is closed.

  3. Their residue fields are

    \[ \kappa (\eta )=K,\qquad \kappa (s)=\mathbb {C}. \]

    To check the first equality, recall that the residue field is the local ring modulo its maximal ideal:

    \[ \kappa (\eta )=R_{(0)}/(0)R_{(0)}. \]

    Localizing at $(0)$ inverts every element outside $(0)$, hence every nonzero element of $R$. Therefore

    \[ R_{(0)}=\operatorname {Frac}(R)=K. \]

    Its maximal ideal is $(0)$, so

    \[ \kappa (\eta )=K/(0)=K. \]

    We quotient the local ring $R_{(0)}$, not $R$ itself.

  4. For an affine family

    \[ \mathcal{X}=\operatorname {Spec}A \longrightarrow \operatorname {Spec}R, \]

    the two fibres are

    \[ \mathcal{X}_{\eta } =\operatorname {Spec}(A\otimes _ R K), \qquad \mathcal{X}_ s =\operatorname {Spec}(A/tA). \]

    The first makes $t$ invertible; the second sets $t=0$. The generic fibre does not mean setting $t$ equal to a particular nonzero complex number. For the definition of a fibre, see Stacks Project, Definition 26.18.4, Tag 01K0.

$\square$

Exercise 36.22.3 (A generic-fibre point specializes by taking its closure). With $R$ and $K$ as above, consider

\[ \mathcal{X} =\operatorname {Spec}(R[x,y]/(xy-t)). \]

This exercise illustrates specialization by a direct calculation.

  1. Write down its generic and special fibres. Check that $P=(t,1)$ is a $K$-point of the generic fibre.

  2. Inside the total space impose $x=t$ and $y=1$, obtaining a closed subscheme $Z$. Show that

    \[ R[x,y]/(xy-t,x-t,y-1)\cong R. \]

  3. Thus $Z$ has two points. Identify its generic point with $P$. Find the coordinates of its closed point by reducing modulo $t$.

  4. Check that this closed point satisfies the special-fibre equation.

  5. Show that $Z$ is the closure of $P$ in the total space: use that the generic point of $\operatorname {Spec}R$ is dense and that $Z$ is closed.

The goal is to see how a point that is closed in the generic fibre can acquire a specialization in the total space. No singularity assumption is needed for this exercise.

Proof.

  1. Put

    \[ A=R[x,y]/(xy-t). \]

    We compute fibres by tensoring over $R$ with the residue field of the base point, as in Exercise 36.22.2 and Stacks Project, Definition 26.18.4, Tag 01K0.

    For the special point, the residue field is $\mathbb {C}=R/(t)$. Hence

    \[ A\otimes _ R\mathbb {C} \cong A/tA \cong \mathbb {C}[x,y]/(xy). \]

    Tensoring with $\mathbb {C}$ here does not leave $A$ unchanged: the tensor product is over $R$, not over $\mathbb {C}$. In the tensor product,

    \[ t\otimes 1=1\otimes (t\cdot 1)=0, \]

    because $t$ acts as zero on $R/(t)$. Thus the relation $xy=t$ becomes $xy=0$. The special fibre is

    \[ \mathcal{X}_ s =\operatorname {Spec} (\mathbb {C}[x,y]/(xy)). \]

    For the generic point, the residue field is $K=\mathbb {C}((t))$. Thus

    \[ B:=A\otimes _ R K \cong K[x,y]/(xy-t), \]

    and the generic fibre is $\mathcal{X}_{\eta }=\operatorname {Spec}B$. Here $t$ becomes invertible, not equal to $1$. We can nevertheless set $z=y/t$, obtaining

    \[ B\cong K[x,z]/(xz-1). \]

    This is a change of coordinates over $K$, not the substitution $t=1$.

    The notation $P=(t,1)$ specifies coordinates in the affine plane over $K$. It does not denote the ideal $(t,1)$, which contains $1$ and is therefore the whole ring. Since $t\cdot 1=t$, these coordinates satisfy the generic-fibre equation. Evaluation gives a surjective map

    \[ B\longrightarrow K,\qquad f\longmapsto f(t,1). \]

    Its kernel is

    \[ \mathfrak {m}_ P=(x-t,y-1). \]

    Indeed, quotienting by these relations replaces $x$ by $t$ and $y$ by $1$, leaving exactly $K$. Consequently

    \[ B/\mathfrak {m}_ P\cong K. \]

    To connect this with the definition of the residue field, localizing at $P$ inverts every $g\notin \mathfrak {m}_ P$, that is, every $g$ with $g(t,1)\neq 0$. Evaluation therefore extends to

    \[ B_{\mathfrak {m}_ P}\longrightarrow K, \qquad \frac{f}{g}\longmapsto \frac{f(t,1)}{g(t,1)}. \]

    This map is surjective, since constants already give all of $K$. Its value is zero exactly when $f(t,1)=0$, or $f\in \mathfrak {m}_ P$. Its kernel is therefore $\mathfrak {m}_ P B_{\mathfrak {m}_ P}$. Thus

    \[ \begin{aligned} \kappa (P) & =B_{\mathfrak {m}_ P}/ \mathfrak {m}_ P B_{\mathfrak {m}_ P}\\ & \cong B/\mathfrak {m}_ P \cong K. \end{aligned} \]

    Localizing can change the ring, but does not change this quotient: the denominators already become nonzero, hence invertible, elements of the field $K$.

$\square$

Exercise 36.22.4 (A unit cannot be cancelled by higher-order terms). Work in

\[ V=\mathbb {C}[[s]],\qquad \mathfrak {m}=(s). \]

For a nonzero series, let $\nu $ be its smallest exponent, as in Commutative Algebra, Example 12.28.6.

  1. For

    \[ x=s^3(2+s),\qquad y=5+s^2, \]

    find their reductions modulo $(s)$, their valuations, and which is a unit.

  2. Set $t=s^2$. Express $x^2$ as a power of $t$ times a unit of $V$. Compare $\nu (x^2)$ with $\nu (t^2)$.

  3. Suppose $u,r\in V$, with $u$ a unit. Show that

    \[ 0=t^2u+t^3r \]

    is impossible. Hint: divide by $t^2$, then reduce modulo $(s)$.

The last item illustrates how orders of vanishing can give a contradiction: a higher-order term cannot cancel a lower-order term with unit coefficient.

Proof.

  1. Reduction modulo $(s)$ keeps only the constant term, so

    \[ \overline{x}=0,\qquad \overline{y}=5. \]

    The valuation is the smallest exponent with nonzero coefficient. Since

    \[ x=2s^3+s^4,\qquad y=5s^0+s^2, \]

    we obtain

    \[ \nu (x)=3,\qquad \nu (y)=0. \]

    The constant term counts as exponent zero.

    By Remark 36.22.1, $y$ is a unit in $V$ because its constant term is $5\neq 0$. In contrast, $x$ is not a unit because its constant term is zero. The reduction $\overline{y}=5$ is also a unit in the residue field, whereas $\overline{x}=0$ is not.

  2. Since $t=s^2$, we have

    \[ x^2=s^6(2+s)^2=t^3(4+4s+t). \]

    The factor $4+4s+t=4+4s+s^2$ has nonzero constant term $4$, so it is a unit in $V$ by Remark 36.22.1. Moreover,

    \[ \nu (t^2)=\nu (s^4)=4,\qquad \nu (x^2)=6. \]

    Thus $\nu (x^2)>\nu (t^2)$.

  3. Suppose

    \[ 0=t^2u+t^3r=t^2(u+tr). \]

    Since $V$ is a domain and $t^2\neq 0$, cancelling $t^2$ gives $u=-tr$. As $t=s^2$, reduction modulo $(s)$ then gives

    \[ \overline{u}=0. \]

    But $u$ is a unit, so reducing $uu^{-1}=1$ gives

    \[ \overline{u}\, \overline{u^{-1}}=1 \]

    in $V/(s)=\mathbb {C}$. Thus $\overline{u}$ cannot be zero. This is the contradiction.

$\square$

Exercise 36.22.5 (Singularity forces an impossible reduction). This model exercise combines specialization with the DVR contradiction. It is not a calculation from the Grunbaum draft.

Let $R=\mathbb {C}[[t]]$ and fix any polynomial $Q\in \mathbb {C}[X,Y,Z]$. Consider the family

\[ \mathcal{Y}=\operatorname {Spec} (R[X,Y,Z]/(F)), \]

where

\[ F=XY-t^2Z+t^3Q(X,Y,Z). \]

Let $V=\mathbb {C}[[s]]$, $L=\mathbb {C}((s))$, and $t=s^ e$ for an integer $e\geq 1$.

Suppose a point on the generic fibre after extending scalars to $L$ has coordinates

\[ P=(a(s),b(s),c(s)),\qquad a,b,c\in V, \]

with

\[ \overline{a}=\overline{b}=0,\qquad \overline{c}\neq 0. \]

Here $X,Y,Z$ are variables in the equations; $a,b,c$ are the series obtained by evaluating them at $P$. Assume this extension over $V$ has already been supplied. The goal is to show that $P$ cannot be singular.

  1. Explain why these coordinates give a map $\operatorname {Spec}V\to \mathcal{Y}$ over $R$. Identify the image $P_0$ of the closed point by reducing the coordinates. Check that $P_0$ lies on the special fibre $XY=0$. Which of $a,b,c$ are units in $V$?

  2. Suppose $P$ is singular. The hypersurface Jacobian criterion requires $F_ X(P)=F_ Y(P)=F_ Z(P)=0$. We will only need the first two. Differentiate in $X,Y$, holding $t$ fixed, and substitute $a,b,c$. Explain why the resulting equalities are equalities in $V$, even though singularity is tested over $L$.

  3. Use those two equalities to show that $a,b\in t^3V$. Write

    \[ a=t^3\alpha ,\qquad b=t^3\beta , \qquad \alpha ,\beta \in V. \]

    This is stronger than merely knowing that $a,b$ reduce to zero.

  4. Substitute into $F(P)=0$, cancel $t^2$, and reduce modulo $(s)$. Show that the resulting equality contradicts the fact that $c$ is a unit. You should not need to know the coefficients of $Q$.

  5. Identify the role of each assumption: having a specialization, knowing its nonzero coordinate, lying on the family, and being singular. Which step supplied the extra divisibility that made the contradiction possible?
