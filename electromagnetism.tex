\section{Lighting a bulb}
\label{section-lighting-a-bulb}

Start with the little screw-base bulb from a
school lab. Hold it by the glass. The metal
tip at its foot is one contact. The threaded
metal shell is the other. An insulating ring
keeps them apart. Inside the glass, a thin
wire called the filament joins the two
contacts through a pair of leads.

\begin{center}
\includegraphics[width=.78\linewidth]
{electromagnetism-assets/bulb.png}
\end{center}

Trace the drawing with a finger. Start at the
plus sign on the cell. Follow the copper wire
to the switch. Lower the switch blade to
bridge the gap. Continue to the tip, through
the filament, out through the shell and back
along the wire to the minus sign. The cell
itself completes the loop between its
terminals. Use a bulb rated for this one
cell. Leave the switch open and the bulb
stays dark. Close it and the filament lights
up.

What is in the copper before we connect it?
Even an unconnected wire contains mobile
electrons among positive atomic cores.
Electric charge is a property of matter that
determines how it pushes or pulls
electrically. Charges of the same sign repel
and opposite signs attract. An electron
carries negative charge. In ordinary copper
the positive and negative charges balance
overall. Electrons move even in the dark
wire, with no preferred direction along it.

What does the cell contribute? A battery
contains one or more chemical cells. In our
cell two different electrodes touch an
electrolyte, a material in which charged
atoms or molecules, called ions, can move.
At the negative electrode a reaction frees
electrons. At the positive electrode another
reaction takes up electrons. With the switch open, a small
separation of charge builds up until its
electrical effect balances this chemical
drive.

The resulting difference in electric
potential between the terminals is called the
voltage. It measures the change in electric
potential energy per unit positive charge.
The unit of charge is the coulomb. A terminal
voltage of 1.5 volts means that for each
coulomb going through the external circuit
from plus to minus, 1.5 joules of energy are
available to the circuit.

Now look at the open switch. One side is
wired to plus. The other is connected through
the filament to minus. With no steady flow,
there is no voltage drop along the filament
or wires. A voltmeter placed across the gap
would read nearly the whole cell voltage. Yet
no charge crosses the air gap. Current is the
net charge crossing a section of wire per
unit time. One coulomb per second is one
ampere. Here the steady current is zero.

Close the switch. The change in the electric
field travels along the circuit. As it
reaches the filament, the field exerts a
force on electrons already there. They start
to drift while electrons from the cell have
barely moved along the wire. In the metal
this drift is from minus toward plus.
Conventional current points the other way,
the direction a positive charge would move.
As electrons leave the negative terminal,
its reaction replaces them. The reaction at
the positive terminal takes up the arriving
electrons. Ions carry charge between the
electrodes inside the cell. Together these
processes sustain the potential difference
as long as the reactants last.

Why does the filament glow? The field does
work on its moving electrons. Scattering in
the metal transfers energy to the vibrating
atoms. Most of the voltage drop in the
external loop is across the thin filament, so
most of the electrical heating occurs there.
Its temperature rises until it radiates
visibly. Count charge entering and leaving
the hot filament in one second of steady
operation. The amounts are equal. What the
cell spends is chemical energy, which reaches
the surroundings as light and heat.

See \cite[Sections 9.2, 9.5 and 10.1]
{university-physics-two}.

the bulb and add

{a friend who knows when to look}.

\section{Electrostatics}
What would a small charge do if we placed it
here? The electric field $\mathbf E$ answers
this question. A stationary positive test
charge $q$ feels the force $q\mathbf E$. An
electron has charge $-e$ and accelerates in
the opposite direction.

Outside a stationary point charge $Q$ in
vacuum the field at distance $r$ is
$$
\mathbf E=
\frac{Q}{4\pi\varepsilon_0 r^2}\hat r,
$$
where $\hat r$ points away from the charge.
Doubling the distance divides the force on
the same test charge by four.

\section{Voltage across two plates}
Give two large parallel conducting plates
opposite charges. Away from their edges the
field in the gap is nearly uniform and points
toward the negative plate. For plate
separation $d$ its magnitude is approximately
$$
E=\frac{|V_1-V_2|}{d}.
$$
One volt means one joule per coulomb. An
electron moving freely from the lower
potential to a potential one volt higher
gains one electronvolt of kinetic energy.

\section{Electrodynamics}
A moving charge also feels a magnetic force.
For velocity $\mathbf v$ the full force is
$$
\mathbf F=q(\mathbf E+
\mathbf v\times\mathbf B).
$$
The magnetic term is perpendicular to the
velocity, so it can bend a trajectory while
preserving speed.

Changing magnetic fields can also produce
electric fields. Hold a wire loop still and
push a bar magnet toward it. The changing
magnetic flux through the loop induces an
electromotive force. Pull the magnet away and
the induced current reverses. This is the
induction studied in electrodynamics.

See \cite[Vol. II, Sections 1-1 to 1-4 and
17-1]{feynman-lectures}.
