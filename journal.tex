\section{Artin's crietion: toy example}

Consider the simplicial complex whose
Stanley-Reisner ideal is generated by
\begin{equation}
I=(x_0x_2,\ x_0x_3,\ x_1x_3)
\subset \mathbb{C}[x_0,x_1,x_2,x_3].
\end{equation}
We deform its generators. Consider instead
$$
\begin{aligned}
x_0x_2\xymatrix{\ar@{~>}[r]&}&x_0x_2+tx_1^2\\
x_0x_3\xymatrix{\ar@{~>}[r]&}&x_0x_3\\
x_1x_3\xymatrix{\ar@{~>}[r]&}&x_1x_3+sx_2^2
\end{aligned}
$$

\begin{theorem}[
Artin's criterion for flatness]
\label{theorem-artin-criterion}
Let $R=\mathbb{C}[x_1,\ldots,x_n]$.
Let $A$ be a local Artinian algebra of finite type over $\mathbb{C}$.
Let $I \subset R$ and $I_A \subset R_A:=R\otimes A$.
Suppose that there is an exact sequence

\begin{equation}
\label{equation-artin-criterion1}
\xymatrix{
R^\ell\ar[r]
&R^m\ar[r]
&R\ar[r]
&R/I\ar[r]
&0
}
\end{equation}

\noindent
and a complex
\begin{equation}
\label{equation-artin-criterion2}
\xymatrix{
R^\ell_A\ar[r]
&R^m_A\ar[r]
&R_A\ar[r]
&R_A/I_A\ar[r]
&0
}
\end{equation}

\noindent
such that the part
$$
\xymatrix{
R^m_A\ar[r]
&R_A\ar[r]
&R_A/I_A\ar[r]
&0
}
$$
is exact.
Then, if
(\ref{equation-artin-criterion2})
$\otimes_A\mathbb{C}$
=
(\ref{equation-artin-criterion1}),
the module $R_A/I_A$ is $A$-flat.
\end{theorem}

\section{Torsion cleaning}
\label{section-torsion-cleaning}

\noindent
I'll put some notes here trying to explain
the theory behind the computations that
I'm currently doing in codex.

\medskip\noindent
Let
$$
M=\mathbb{C}[t,x_0,…,x_7]/J
=\mathbb{C}[t,x]/J
$$
be the coordinate ring of our deformation.
(Yes, for some reason there's only one
deformation parameter.)
Restricting to the special fiber
$t=0$ means literally:
$$
M \otimes_{\mathbb{C}[t]}
\mathbb{C}[t]/(t)
$$
i.e. that's the ring of the special fiber,
which is the ideal of 
$\operatorname{SR}(\mathcal{M})$.
This means that anything having $t$ will die.

\begin{exercise}
Let
$$
P=\mathbb{C}[x_0,\dots,x_7,t]
$$
and let $J\subset P$. Put $M=P/J$.
Show that the fiber of
$$
\operatorname{Spec} M\longrightarrow
\mathbb{A}^1_t
$$
over $t=a$ has coordinate ring
$$
M\otimes_{\mathbb{C}[t]}
\mathbb{C}[t]/(t-a)
\simeq
P/(J+(t-a)).
$$
\end{exercise}

\begin{proof}
Since $M=P/J$, we have
$$
M\otimes_{\mathbb{C}[t]}
\mathbb{C}[t]/(t-a)
=
(P/J)\otimes_{\mathbb{C}[t]}
\mathbb{C}[t]/(t-a).
$$
Tensoring with $\mathbb{C}[t]/(t-a)$ means
imposing the relation $t=a$. Hence
$$
(P/J)\otimes_{\mathbb{C}[t]}
\mathbb{C}[t]/(t-a)
\simeq
P/(J+(t-a)).
$$
In particular, the special fiber over $t=0$
has coordinate ring
$$
P/(J+(t)).
$$
\end{proof}

\medskip\noindent
In practice, we do the following:
compute the coordinate ring of the special
fiber, it will be some ring.
Now, the torsion witness $w$ is a polynomial
which dies in the coordinate ring
of the special fiber.
This tells me: the polynomial is one of
the defining polynomials of the special
fiber, but it is not a defining polynomial
of any of the other fibers.
That must kill flatness.
NO! The real deal is:
what kills flatness is a polynomial $w$
which is not a defining polynomial
of the {\bf whole family} (as a variety)
but suddenly becomes a defining polynomial
when we restrict to (any) fiber.

Let $R=\mathbb{C}[t]$ and let
$M=R/(t^2)$. We think of $M$ as the
coordinate ring of a toy family over
$\operatorname{Spec} R$.

The special fiber over $t=0$ has coordinate
ring
$$
M\otimes_R R/(t)
\simeq R/(t^2)\otimes_R R/(t)
\simeq R/(t^2,t)
\simeq \mathbb{C}.
$$
Equivalently, we set $t=0$.

Now consider the class $w=t$ in $M$. It is
non-zero in the total ring $M$, because
$t\notin (t^2)$. But it is killed by $t$:
$$
tw=t^2=0.
$$
Thus $w$ is $t$-torsion.

On the special fiber, however, the same class
becomes zero, since the special fiber is
computed by imposing $t=0$:
$$
w|_{t=0}=0.
$$
So $w$ is non-zero in the total family but
invisible on the special fiber. This is the
toy model for a torsion witness
$w\notin J$, $tw\in J$.

\medskip\noindent
Here's another AI understanding-flatness
paragraph:

Flatness is a property of the morphism
$$
X \longrightarrow \mathbb{A}^1_t,
$$
not only of the individual fibers.

If
$$
X=\operatorname{Spec}(S[t]/J),
$$
then the total family is encoded by the
$\mathbb{C}[t]$-module
$$
M=S[t]/J.
$$
The fiber over $t=a$ is obtained by tensoring:
$$
M_a
=
M\otimes_{\mathbb{C}[t]}
\mathbb{C}[t]/(t-a).
$$

Thus the fibers are derived from the total
module $M$. They do not remember everything
about $M$.

Flatness means that $M$ varies cleanly in the
$t$-direction. In particular, multiplication
by $t$ should not kill a non-zero class:
$$
\bar w\neq 0,\qquad t\bar w=0
$$
is forbidden.

Equivalently, for the ideal $J$, flatness
rules out
$$
w\notin J,\qquad tw\in J.
$$

So we care about membership in $J$ because
$J$ defines the total family. The fibers see
what happens after setting $t=a$, but
flatness asks whether these fibers come from
a clean total object over the whole
$t$-line.

\medskip\noindent
Now let's explain {\bf torsion cleaning}:
Let $P=S[t]$ and let $J\subset P$ define a
family over $\mathbb{A}^1_t$. Put
$$
M=P/J.
$$
A class $\bar w\in M$ is $t$-torsion if
$$
\bar w\neq 0,\qquad t^N\bar w=0
$$
for some $N\geq 1$. In terms of ideals, this
means
$$
w\notin J,\qquad t^Nw\in J.
$$

To remove all such torsion, we replace $J$
by its saturation with respect to $t$:
$$
J:t^\infty
=
\{\,w\in P\mid t^Nw\in J
\text{ for some }N\geq 0\,\}.
$$
Thus $J:t^\infty$ is obtained by adding to
$J$ every polynomial which becomes a
consequence of $J$ after multiplying by a
power of $t$.

Equivalently,
$$
J:t^\infty=JP[t^{-1}]\cap P.
$$
So geometrically we keep the part of the
family over $t\neq 0$, where $t$ is
invertible, and then take its closure back
over $\mathbb{A}^1_t$.

This is the torsion-cleaned family. If its
special fiber is different from the original
one, then the naive family was not the flat
deformation we wanted.

\medskip\noindent
{\bf Our problem.}
The condition that the family $J\subset S[t]$
has special fiber $S/I$ is
$$
\frac{J+(t)}{(t)}=I
\quad\text{inside }S[t]/(t)\simeq S.
$$
Equivalently, viewing $I$ as an ideal of
$S[t]$ by extension, this says
$$
J+(t)=I+(t).
$$
Thus, after imposing $t=0$, the ideal
defining the special fiber is exactly the
Stanley--Reisner ideal $I$.

BUT when we do the torsion-cleaning,
we obtain a new deformation family
$K = J:t^{\infty}$, which is flat, but,
unfortunately, gives
$$
K+(t) \neq I+(t).
$$

\medskip\noindent
So: \textbf{Question.}
Can we replace the naive ideal
$J=(F_1(t),\dots,F_{16}(t))$
by an ideal J' such that
\begin{enumerate}
\item $ J'_{t\neq 0}=J_{t\neq 0}$,
\item $J'+(t)=I+(t)$,
\item $J' : t^\infty = J'$,   
\end{enumerate}

\noindent
i.e. same generic family, same special
fiber, but already torsion-clean?

\medskip\noindent 
{\bf The trip now.}
The trip is to
add polynomials to the deformation family's
ideal $J$ until you get a flat family whose
special fibre remains being $I$. So I guess
you're just doing that in stages tagged by
the degree of the variable $t$ used to defined
the $t$-torsion correction
$J:t^{\operatorname{order}}$, right?!

That's why I told Codex: "I believe this is
your trip: add $t$-torsion correction terms
slowly (degree by degree) and try to detect
exactly where the thing breaks."
But there was already something known
about this layer business, apparently:

\paragraph{Current torsion picture.}
Let
$$
P=S[t],\qquad J\subset P
$$
be the naive sparse cubic family, and let
$I\subset S$ be the Stanley--Reisner ideal.
The raw special fiber is correct:
$$
J+(t)=I+(t).
$$
However $J$ is not $t$-saturated:
$$
J:t^\infty\neq J.
$$

The torsion-cleaning filtration is
$$
J\subset J:t\subset J:t^2
\subset\cdots\subset J:t^\infty.
$$
The new terms appearing in
$$
(J:t^n)/(J:t^{n-1})
$$
are called layer $n$.

In the current computation, layers $1$ and
$2$ are legal: their specializations lie in
$I$. The first illegal layer is layer $3$.
After cleaning through layer $2$, the
monomials
$$
u_1=x_1x_3x_4x_7,
\qquad
u_2=x_1x_3x_4^2
$$
satisfy
$$
tu_1\in J_2,\qquad tu_2\in J_2,
$$
but
$$
u_1,u_2\notin I.
$$
Thus adding them would change the special
fiber. This is the first visible failure of
the naive family.

\paragraph{Next computational plan.}
The next step is not to find the bad layer;
that is already known. The next step is to
ask whether the bad layer can be avoided by
changing the legal corrections chosen in the
earlier layers.

Instead of adding a raw legal witness $m$,
one allows a lifted correction
$$
h=m+tq,
$$
where $q$ is chosen from a finite monomial
space. This does not change the
specialization at $t=0$, since
$$
h(0)=m(0).
$$
But it may change the next colon computation.

Thus one forms a modified ideal
$$
J'=J+(h_1,\dots,h_5),
$$
where the $h_i$ lift the legal layer $1$ and
layer $2$ witnesses. The target is to test
whether the first illegal witnesses still
appear:
$$
tu_1\in J' \quad ?,
\qquad
tu_2\in J' \quad ?.
$$

If some choice of the $q_i$ makes these
relations disappear, then the layer-$3$
obstruction was an artifact of the naive
legal corrections. If no such choice exists
inside the chosen ansatz, then we get a
finite no-go certificate for this branch.

In short: the current branch breaks first at
layer $3$; the new task is to modify the
legal corrections in layers $1$ and $2$ so
that the layer-$3$ illegal witnesses do not
appear.

\medskip\noindent
{\bf Results.} 
We found a lifted legal repair $J'$ whose raw
special fiber is still $I$ and for which the
previous layer-$3$ witnesses $u_1,u_2$ are no
longer in $J':t$. Thus the layer-$3$ failure
of the raw repair is not forced in this
finite ansatz. The remaining question is
whether $J'$ is actually $t$-saturated, or
whether the torsion has merely moved to a
later layer.

Meaning: he removed two witnesses, $u_1$
and $u_2$
but... he didn't check for full flatness.

\section{Hartshorne's book and Fausk's
method}
\label{section-hartshorne-and-fausk}

Hartshorne's book is relevant here in two
different ways.

First, it sets up the general language of
deformations. First-order embedded
deformations are controlled by the normal
sheaf; see \cite[\S 2]{Hartshorne-deformation}.
For affine schemes, the local deformation
theory is expressed using the
Lichtenbaum--Schlessinger $T^i$ functors;
see \cite[\S\S 3,5]{Hartshorne-deformation}.
Higher-order lifting and flatness enter in
\cite[\S 6]{Hartshorne-deformation}.

Second, Chapter 2 isolates special
situations where the equations have a
stable homological format. In these cases
one deforms the format, and flatness follows
from the resulting resolution. The main
examples are:

\begin{itemize}
\item complete intersections, controlled by
the Koszul complex;
\cite[\S 9]{Hartshorne-deformation};

\item Cohen--Macaulay schemes of
codimension two, controlled by the
Hilbert--Burch matrix;
\cite[\S 8]{Hartshorne-deformation};

\item Gorenstein schemes of codimension
three, controlled by the
Buchsbaum--Eisenbud Pfaffian resolution;
\cite[\S 9]{Hartshorne-deformation}.
\end{itemize}

To make sure, here's a useful quote
from section 9:

\begin{quotation} ``As in the previous
section, Cohen–Macaulay in codimension two,
there are some other situations in which the
particular structure of the resolution of an
ideal allows us to show that all deformations
have the same structure, and that
deformations can always be extended by
lifting the corresponding resolutions. These
are the case of complete intersections and
Gorenstein schemes in codimension 3.''
\end{quotation}


This explains the mechanism used by Fausk.
In the codimension three Gorenstein case,
one lifts the skew-symmetric matrix. The
Pfaffian resolution persists, and this gives
a flat deformation.

Our Stanley--Reisner scheme is different.
It has codimension four, so it is not covered
by these automatic formats. Hartshorne
therefore does not provide a direct
replacement for the Pfaffian construction.

What remains useful is the obstruction
framework. The local obstruction to lifting
lies in $T^2$, while the global obstructions
are organized through the groups involving
$T^0,T^1,T^2$; see
\cite[\S 10]{Hartshorne-deformation}.
Thus Hartshorne explains what the
computations mean, but does not give the
codimension four construction.

For our problem, the likely replacement is
not another matrix format from Hartshorne.
It is the versal deformation theory of the
Stanley--Reisner scheme, probably via
Christophersen. Hartshorne's Hilbert
functor results then explain why an actual
branch of the Hilbert functor gives a flat
family; see
\cite[\S 17]{Hartshorne-deformation}.
