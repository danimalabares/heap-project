\noindent
Rafael Potrie, Universidad de la República,
Uruguay. Geometric Structures on Manifolds,
IMPA. September 26, 2024.

\medskip\noindent
{\bf Abstract.}
Anosov subgroups are a special kind of
subgroups of Lie groups that extend convex
cocompact groups in rank 1. In this informal
talk I will discuss a bit about these kind of
groups and what are possible questions
towards a characterization. Depending on
interest, I could discuss some recent work
with L. Carvajales and P. Lessa in this
direction.

\medskip\noindent
{\bf Groups as geometric/dynamical objects.}
$\Gamma<G$, $G$ is a Lie group,, we can think
$G=\operatorname{SL}(d,\mathbb{R}),
\operatorname{SL}(d,\mathbb{C})$.
Think $\Gamma$ is finitely generated.

\medskip\noindent
{\bf Algebra questions.}

\begin{remark}[Burnside Problem]
\label{remark-potrie-burnside}
$\Gamma$ is finitely generated in $G$ and
every element is torsion, then $\Gamma$ is
finite.
\end{remark}

\medskip\noindent
{\bf Selberg Lema.}

\medskip\noindent
{\bf Tits alternative.}

Now suppose $\Gamma$ is also discrete and
quasi-isometric. This makes it more
geometric. We want to look at deformations of
$\Gamma$ within $G$.

Let's explain what quasi-isometric means.
$\Gamma$ is a finitely generated group. Let
$F=\{f_i\}$ be finite and symmetric ($f\in F
\iff f^{-1}\in F$) generators. So
$\Gamma=\left\langle{} f_i | r_j
\right\rangle{}$ where $r_j$ are relations.
Consider $\rho:\Gamma\to G$ where $\rho(f_i)$
verify the relations $r_j$.

We are interested in
$\operatorname{Hom}(\Gamma,G)\subseteq
G^{|F|}$

We can define a distance in this group
$$d_F(\gamma,\eta)=|\eta^{-1}\gamma|_F=
\inf\{n:\eta^{-1}\gamma
=f_{i_1}\ldots f_{i_n}, f_{i_k}\in F\}$$

\begin{definition}
\label{definition-potrie-qi-embedding}
A \textit{quasi-isometric embedding} is
$q:(X,d_x)\to (Y,d_y)$ such that there exist
two numbers $(0,1) \ni a,b>0$ such that
$$
\begin{array}{c}
a\, d_x(\mathfrak{x},\mathfrak{x}')-b \\
<d_y(q(\mathfrak{x}),q(\mathfrak{x}')) \\
<a^{-1}d_x(\mathfrak{x},\mathfrak{x}')+b
\end{array}
$$
\end{definition}

Now we put $\rho:\Gamma\to
\operatorname{Isom}(X)$ so thinking of $G$ as
$\operatorname{Isom}(X)$

\begin{definition}
\label{definition-potrie-orbit-map}
The \textit{orbit map} for $\mathfrak{x}\in
X$ is $\Phi:\gamma \mapsto \rho(\gamma
)(\mathfrak{x})$. And $\rho$ is
quasi-isometric if the orbit map is a
\textit{quasi-isometric embedding}
\end{definition}

\begin{example}
\label{example-potrie-sl2}
In $G=\operatorname{SL}(2,\mathbb{R})$
equivalent to $\exists k>0$ so that $\forall
\gamma \in\Gamma$ $\| \rho(\gamma
)\|>e^{k|\gamma|_F}$
\end{example}

\begin{example}[Teichmüler space]
\label{example-potrie-teichmuller}
$$\Gamma=\pi_{1}(S_g)= \left\langle{}
a_1,b_1,\ldots,a_g,b_g:\prod_{i} [a_i,b_i]
=\operatorname{id}\right\rangle{}$$
These representations are very well studied:
$$
\begin{array}{l}
\operatorname{Isom}(\mathbb{H})
\operatorname{Hom}(\Gamma,
\operatorname{PSL}(2,\mathbb{R}))/
\operatorname{PSL}(2,\mathbb{R}) \\
\supseteq \operatorname{Teich}(S_g)
\cong \mathbb{R}^{6g-6} \\
\cong \operatorname{Hom}_{\operatorname{fd}}
(\Gamma,\operatorname{PSL}(2,\mathbb{R}))/
\operatorname{PSL}(2,\mathbb{R})
\end{array}
$$
where $\operatorname{Teich}S_g$ is the space
of hyperbolic metrics in $S_g$ modulo
isotopy. (See Svarc-Milnor).
\end{example}

\begin{example}[Hyperbolic space]
\label{example-potrie-hyperbolic-space}
Recall that $\operatorname{SO}(1,2)$ are the
isometries of a quadratic form of signature
$(1,2)$ acting on $\mathbb{R}^{3}$. These

Restrict $Q$ to the hyperboloid $Q=1$ inside

{\bf Title.}
Investigations of

\medskip\noindent
{\bf Abstract.}
The studies of convex-cocompact
$\operatorname{SO}(3,1),\operatorname{SO}(2,
2)$,
and $\operatorname{SO}(2,1)\times
\mathbb{R}^{2,1}$ structures are the
Lie-theoretic analogues of the studies of de
Sitter, anti de Sitter, and Minkowski
spacetimes respectively. The analytic
treatments of these three theories are almost
identical, suggesting a unified treatment
involving Anosov
$\operatorname{SL}(4)$-structures. The main
challenge in constructing such a unification
is the absence of any invariant metric. It is
thus necessary to enquire what structures may
take their place. The purpose of this talk is
to present some preliminary investigations
into the theory of $\operatorname{SL}(4)$
structures aimed at resolving this problem.

{\bf Quasifuchsian representations.}
Start with older stuff: quasifuschian

Finally we define hyperbolic ends

\begin{definition}
\label{definition-smith-hyperbolic-end}
Let $X$ be hyperbolic. Consider a function
$h:X\longrightarrow ]0,\infty[$ such that
\begin{enumerate}
\item $\|\nabla h\|=1$.
\item $h$ is convex $C^{1,1}$.
\item For all $\varepsilon>0$,
$h^{-1}([\varepsilon,\infty[)$ is complete.
\end{enumerate}
We say $X$ is a \textit{hyperbolic end} if it
has a \textit{height function} $h$. Typically
this $h$ is distance to the convex hull.
\end{definition}

\begin{remark}
\label{remark-smith-hopf-rinow}
These three conditions are just enough to
apply Hopf-Rinow theorem.
\end{remark}

of the shape operator of these surfaces is
constant.
\end{remark}

\section{Quasi-homogeneous cones}
\label{section-frolov-quasi-hom-cones}
%% speaker: Ivan Frolov, IMPA
%% place: Geometric Structures on Manifolds, IMPA
%% date: October 24, 2024

\noindent
Ivan Frolov, IMPA. Geometric Structures on
Manifolds, IMPA. October 24, 2024.

\medskip\noindent
{\bf Abstract.}
A cone $V$ in a Euclidean space is
quasi-homogeneous if it contains a compact
subset $K$ such that every point of $V$ can
be mapped into $K$ by a linear automorphism
of $V$. I will discuss a theorem of
Kac-Vinberg, which says that the
projectivisation of a quasi-homogeneous cone
in $\mathbb{R}^{3}$ with piecewise $C^2$
boundary is either an ellipse or a triangle.

\begin{definition}
\label{definition-frolov-cone}
$V\subset \mathbb{R}^{3}$ is a \textit{cone}
if
\begin{enumerate}
\item $V$ is open.
\item $\forall v\in V$, $\lambda>0$, $\lambda
v \in V$.
\item Closure of the convex hull of $V$
contains no lines.
\end{enumerate}
Its automorphism group is composed of linear
automorphisms of $\mathbb{R}^{3}$ that
preserve $V$. $V$ is \textit{homogeneous} if
$\operatorname{Aut}(V)$ acts transitively on
$V$, and \textit{quasi-homogeneous} if there
exists a compact subset $K\subset V$ such
that $\operatorname{Aut}(V)\cdot K=V$. So
it's ``generated'' by a compact subset? ``The
intuiton is that the quotient is compact,
though we don't know if it is Hausdorff.''
\end{definition}

\begin{remark}[Misha]
\label{remark-frolov-misha-vinberg}
Most of the work on cones was done by
Vinberg.
\end{remark}

The projectivization of a cone is contained
in projective plane, $PV\subset
\mathbb{R}P^{2}$, and it is a ``convex
subset''. Its boundary $\Gamma=\partial(PV)$
is a Jordan curve.

\begin{theorem}[Kac-Vinberg, 1965?]
\label{theorem-frolov-kac-vinberg}
Let $V$ be a quasi-homogeneous cone. If
$\Gamma$ is piecewise $C^2$, then $\Gamma$ is
either an ellipse or a triangle.
\end{theorem}

We shall unsuccesfully try to prove this
theorem.

\begin{lemma}
\label{lemma-frolov-convex}
$V$ quasi-homogeneous cone $\implies $ $V$ is
convex.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
Let $V'$ be the convex hull of $V$. Then $V'$
is a convex cone and $\operatorname{Aut}(V)$
acts on $V'$. Now condier its
projectivization $PV'$. There is a distance
in $PV'$:
$$d(a,b)=\operatorname{log}\frac{|ay|\cdot
|bx|}{|ax|\cdot |by|}$$
So $d(PK,PV'\setminus
\overline{PV})>\varepsilon$, so there exists
$x \in PV'\setminus \overline{PV}$. Then
there exists a point $y \in PV$ very close to
$\varepsilon$. I think here convexity was
used, perhaps we can take $y $ in the
boundary.

$g\in\operatorname{Aut}(V)$ such that $gy \in
K$. But then
$$\underbrace{d(x,y)}_{<\varepsilon}=
\underbrace{d(gx,gy)}_{<\varepsilon}$$
which means $gx\in PV'\setminus PV$.
\end{proof}

Now let $V$ be a quasi-homogeneous cone,
$G\subset \operatorname{Aut}(V)$ such that
$\exists K\subset V$ with $GK=V$.

If $G'$ is a finite index subgroup of $G$,
then $G'$ also satisfies the property above.

\begin{lemma}
\label{lemma-frolov-eigenspace}
$g \in G$. If $g$ has a 2-dimensional
eigenspace, then $g^2=\operatorname{Id}$.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
Let $\ell$ be the projectivization of the
eigenspace, $g$ fixes $\ell$ pointwise. Then
$\forall x\in\ell\setminus PV$, $g^2$ fixes
tangents from $x$ to $PV$. Hence
$g^2=\operatorname{Id}$.
\end{proof}

\begin{lemma}
\label{lemma-frolov-two-eigenvalues}
$g\in G$ cannot have exactly two different
eigenvalues.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
$$g=\begin{pmatrix} \lambda &1&0\\0& \lambda&
0\\0 &0& \mu \end{pmatrix} ,\qquad
\lambda,\mu \in\mathbb{R}$$
$x \in \mathbb{R}P^{2}$, then for a basis
$v_1,v_2,v_3$,
$$\lim_{n \to \infty} g^n(x)=[v_1]$$
if $x\neq v_3$, and
$$\lim_{n \to \infty} g^{-n}=[v_3]$$
if $x \not\in(v_1,v_2)$.
Then $x$ can be on $\Gamma \implies
[v_1],[v_3]\in\Gamma$.

Then $g$ preserves $[v_1]$ and $[v_3]$. There
is a curve $\Gamma$ preserved by $g$. Then
$g$ also preserves the tangents. And $g^2$
preserves tangents. But the tangents, like
tangents to a circle, intersect, and that
gives a third point fixed by $g^2$, but
that's impossible because there are only two
eigenvalues ($g^2$ has the same form as $g$).
But the end of this proof was sketchy.
\end{proof}

\begin{lemma}
\label{lemma-frolov-unipotent}
$G$ cannot be unipotent (=exponent of Lie

If

$$G=\left\{ \begin{pmatrix} 1& a & b\\0 & 1 &

\label{lemma-frolov-diagonal}
If the $G$ is diagonal then $\Gamma$ is a

\begin{lemma}
\label{lemma-frolov-upper-triangular}
If $G$ is upper-triangular then $\Gamma$ is
either an ellipse or a triangle.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
If $G$ is commutative take some non-unipotent
element, If it was exactly 2 eigenvalues it's

diagonal out of commutativity and apply

$$g=\begin{pmatrix} 1 & a & 0 \\ 0 & 1 & a \\
0 & 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix}$$
Now note that $[G,G]$ is commutative, since
$$[ [G,G],[G,G]]\in\begin{pmatrix} 1 & b &
*\\ 0 & 1 & 0\\ 0 & 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix}$$

Then
$$\begin{pmatrix} \lambda_1 & * & *\\ 0 &
\lambda_1 & *\\ 0 & 0 & \lambda_1
\end{pmatrix} \begin{pmatrix} 0 & a & * \\ 0

\frac{\lambda_2}{\lambda_3}a & *\\ & 0 &
\frac{\lambda_2}{\lambda_3}a\\ & & 0
\end{pmatrix}$$
where
$$hgh^{-1}=\begin{pmatrix} 1 & \lambda a &
*\\ 0 & 1 & \lambda a\\ 0 & 0 & 1
\end{pmatrix}$$
Now do this several times to obtain
$$h^ngh^{-n}=\begin{pmatrix} 1 & \lambda^na &
*\\ 0 & 1 & \lambda^n a\\0 & 0 & 1
\end{pmatrix}$$
and you can see perhaps that it is not

\end{proof}

\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
Since distance is preserved, $\{g^n\}$ is not

\begin{lemma}
\label{lemma-frolov-large-finite-order}
If $G$ has an element of arbitrarily large

\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
If $g \in G$ with $g^n=\operatorname{Id}$, $v
\in V$, then the cone
$$v+gv+\ldots +g^{n-1}v=v'\qquad g(v')=v'$$
$$g^{k_n}_n=\operatorname{Id}\qquad k_n\to
\infty\qquad g_n(v_n)=v_n$$
somehow we map them to $K$, $v_n\in K$ and
then we can assume they converge $v_n\to v$.
So there is a subsequence $g_n^{m_n}$ that
will converge to an irrational rotation
around $v$. Then we use that the group is
closed and apply the previous lemma.
\end{proof}

\begin{lemma}
\label{lemma-frolov-real-eigenvalues}
If $g \in G$ preserves $x\in \Gamma$, then

\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
We already assume that the curve is smooth,

\end{proof}

\label{lemma-frolov-unipotent-or-finite}
If every $g \in G$ is either unipotent or of

\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
By
Lemma~\ref{lemma-frolov-large-finite-order}
there exists $N$ such that every $g\in G$ of

$$(g^N-1)^3=0$$
So there is an algebraic group… let

and $G_0$ connected component of $1\in

components, which implies that the index
$[\overline{G}:G_0]<\infty$ is finite. So
$G_0\cap G$ is unipotent. Apply
Lemma~\ref{lemma-frolov-upper-triangular}.
\end{proof}

\section{Loop spaces: conformal center of
mass and infinite dimensional GIT for the
space of isometric loops}
\label{section-korshunov-loop-spaces}
%% speaker: Dmitrii Korshunov, IMPA
%% place: Seminário das Sextas, PUC-Rio
%% date: November 22, 2024

\noindent
Dmitrii Korshunov, IMPA. Seminário das
Sextas, PUC-Rio. 22 November 2024.

\medskip\noindent
{\bf Abstract.}
We show, following Millson and Zombro, how to
construct a Kähler structure on the moduli
space of isometric maps of a circle into
Euclidean space. The proof upgrades the
Kirwan/Kempf--Ness theorem relating
symplectic reduction and GIT to the infinite
dimensional setting. The construction
crucially uses the Douady--Earle's notion of
conformal center of mass, which is of
independent interest.

\medskip\noindent
{\bf Three spaces.}

\begin{definition}
\label{definition-korshunov-millson-zombro}
\textit{Millson-Zombro} space is the space of
isometric maps $(S^1,g)\to\mathbb{R}^3$
modulo $\mathsf{SO}(3)\ltimes \mathbb{R}^3$,
that is,
$$\left\{ (S^1,g)\to\mathbb{R}^3
\right\}\Big/\mathsf{SO}(3)\ltimes
\mathbb{R}^3$$
\end{definition}
\begin{enumerate}
\item It is a Kähler manifold.
\item $J$ is integrable. This means there are
holomorphic coordinates, \textit{not}
algebraic integrability (N tensor vanishes).
\end{enumerate}
The upshot is that there is no
Newlander-Niremerg theorem in infinite
dimension.

\begin{definition}
\label{definition-korshunov-brylinski}
\textit{Brylinskly loop space} is: for $M$
3-dimensional Riemannian manifold,
$$\left\{ S^1\to M \right\}
\Big/\operatorname{Diff}^+(S^1)$$
(Without isometry condition.)
\end{definition}
Take a normal vector field $V_1$ to a curve
$\gamma:S^1\to M$,
\begin{enumerate}
\item Define a complex structure $J v_1:=v_1
\times \frac{\dot \gamma}{|\dot \gamma|}$.
This is formally integrable (N tensor
vanishes) but never has a holomorphic
structure (infinite dimensional!)
\item $\int_{S^1}\left\langle{} V_1,V_2
\right\rangle{} dt$ where $V_2$ is another
vector field along $\gamma$, and perhaps we
must choose arclength parametrization. A
Riemannian structure.
\item Now consider the volume form on $M$.
The form
$V(\cdot,\cdot,
\frac{\dot\gamma}{|\dot\gamma|}$
is skew symmetric. So, a symplectic
structure.
\end{enumerate}

\begin{definition}
\label{definition-korshunov-kapovich-millson}
\textit{Kapovich-Millson space of polygons}.
First, a \textit{polygon} is all the
embeddings of $n$ points joined by edges of
lengths $\ell_1,\ldots,\ell_n$ in that order
and possibly intersecting each other. Now fix
a point …? The polygon space is
$$P=\operatorname{polygons}\Big/
\mathsf{SO}(3)\times
\mathbb{R} .$$
\end{definition}

\begin{remark}[Question]
\label{remark-korshunov-question-r}
Why $\times\mathbb{R}$?
\end{remark}

$$P\subset S^2_{\ell_1}\times\ldots\times
S^2_{\ell_n}\curvearrowleft \mathsf{SO}(3)$$
where action is diagonal action.

In fact, this action is hamiltonian. Because
the action $\mathsf{SO}(3)\curvearrowright
S^2$ is hamiltonian with moment map the
identity. Then the moment map of the product
of spheres is just the sum of moment maps.
Also it's simple because
$\mathfrak{so}(3)^*\cong \mathbb{R}^3$. So we
may perform symplectic reduction and obtain a
symplectic manifold
$\mu^{-1}(0)/\mathsf{SO}(3)$. \textit{And the
resulting space is the space of polygons!}
That's the symplectic structure.

\begin{remark}[Question]
\label{remark-korshunov-question-structures}
what about complex and Riemannian strutures?
are they more straightforward to show?
\end{remark}

\medskip\noindent
{\bf Back to MZ space = the loop space.}
Consider a loop that does not necessarily
close up, so a map $\gamma:\mathbb{R} \to
\mathbb{R}^3$. And suppose it is
\textit{smoothly periodic} so unit veolicity
and derivative $\dot\gamma:S^1\to S^2$
periodic. Now Gauss map gives us
$\mathbb{R}\to S^2$, but since veolicity is
periodic it's actually $S^1 \to S^2$. Now
let's put some structures on the space of all
$S^1 \to S^2$:

{\bf (1)}
(Almost complex structure) Tangent vectors to
this space are vector fields tangent to
$S^2$---tangent to the loop! Again: loop is
point, vector at this point is a vector field
tangent to the loop. Anyway we can act with
the complex structure of $S^2$ on that vector
and that's the complex structure.

{\bf (2)}
The riemannian structure is inherited from
the sphere: pair them and integrate. The
question is wether that depends on
parametrization. Last time we just declared
that we use unit speed parametrization.

{\bf (3)}
Symplectic structure:
$$\omega(v_1,v_2)=\int
\dot\gamma\cdot(v_1\times v_2) dt$$
where $(v_1\times v_2)$ is the triple product
presented before.

\begin{proposition}
\label{proposition-korshunov-omega-closed}
$\omega$ is closed.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
Consider
$$\operatorname{ev}:LS^2\times S^1\to S^2$$
$$\pi:LS^2\times S^2\to LS^2.$$
Then
$$\omega_{LS^2}=\pi_*\operatorname{ev}^*(
\omega_{S^2})\wedge
dt$$
where $\omega$ is the symplectic structure on
$S^2$ and $\pi$ is ``fiber integration over
?'' .
\end{proof}

\begin{corollary}
\label{corollary-korshunov-integral}
$\frac{\omega}{8\pi^2}$ is integral. (There
will be a prequantization)
\end{corollary}

And there's an action $LS^2 \curvearrowleft
\mathsf{SO}(3)$. And it's hamiltonian with
moment map $\mu:LS^2 \to \mathbb{R}^3\cong
\mathfrak{so}(3)^*$. So the fundamental
vector field is $\xi \times \beta$. And the
moment map is, for $\beta \in LS^2$
$$\mu(\beta)=\int_{0}^{2\pi}\beta(t)dt$$
$$\int v\cdot\xi ds =
\int_{0}^{2\pi}\beta\cdot \Big((\xi \times
\beta)\times v\Big)ds$$

\section{Cusps of hyperbolic 4-manifolds and
rational homology spheres}
\label{section-ferrari-cusps-hyperbolic}
%% speaker: Leonardo Ferrari
%% place: Geometric Structures on Manifolds Seminar, IMPA
%% date: November 28, 2024

\noindent
Leonardo Ferrari. Geometric Structures on
Manifolds Seminar, IMPA. November 28, 2024.

\medskip\noindent
{\bf Abstract.}
A non-compact finite-volume complete
hyperbolic n-manifold has a finite number of
ends called cusps, whose sections are flat
manifolds called cusp types. The possible
configurations of cusp types on hyperbolic
manifolds, however, is not entirely
understood, and it was an open question
whether a hyperbolic manifold could have only
rational homology spheres as cusp types. An
affirmative answer, in particular, would
contradict some established results on the
spectrum of the Laplacian on k-forms for
cusped hyperbolic manifolds. We will briefly
introduce the geometry of hyperbolic
manifolds, describe the problem of cusp
realisation, and then explain a combinatorial
tool to construct manifolds by gluing copies
of right-angled polytopes - a technique
called colouring. We will then construct a
4-manifold such that all cusp types are the
Hansche-Wendt manifold, the unique flat
rational homology 3-sphere up to
diffeomorphism.

\medskip\noindent
{\bf Reminder on hyperbolic geometry.}
Hyperbolic $n$-space is
$(\mathbb{H}^n,g^{\mathbb{H}^n})$. Here
$\mathbb{H}^N\cong B_1^N(0)$ and
$g^{\mathbb{H}^N}_x=\left(
\frac{2}{1-\|x\|^2} \right)^2
g^{\mathbb{R}^N}_x$. This is called the
Poincaré ball.

Half-space model: $\{(x_1,\ldots,x_{N-1},y)
\in \mathbb{R}^N:y>0\}$. Here the metric is
$g_{(x_1,\ldots,x_{N-1},y)}=
\frac{1}{y^2}g^{\mathbb{R}^N}_
{(x_1,\ldots,x_{N-1},y)}$.

$M$ is \textit{hyperbolic} if
$M=\mathbb{H}^N/\Gamma$ for some discrete,
freely-acting $\Gamma \subset
\operatorname{Isom}(\mathbb{H}^N)$.

\medskip\noindent
{\bf Cusp geometry.}

\begin{remark}
\label{remark-ferrari-cusped}
$M$ can have finite volume without being
compact. These manifolds are called
\textit{cusped}.
\end{remark}

\begin{example}
\label{example-ferrari-truncated-cusp}
The \textit{truncated cusp}, that is, the
hyperbolic funnel. (But it is not complete.)
\end{example}

\begin{proposition}
\label{proposition-ferrari-thick-thin}
Every complete finite-volume hyperbolic
manifold has a decomposition into compact
part and a union of truncated cusps $T_i$.
This is called the \textit{thick-thin
decomposition}. Moreover, each
$T_i\overset{\operatorname{iso}}{\cong}F
\times [a, +\infty)$ where $F$ has a flat
metric $g_T=\frac{1}{\mu}g_F$ (so its like a
torus product half line).

$F$ is called the \textit{cusp type} and
$(F,g_F)$ the \textit{cusp shape}.
\end{proposition}

\begin{remark}[Problem]
\label{remark-ferrari-problem-cusp-types}
Which flat manifolds are cusp types of some
hyperbolic manifold?
\end{remark}

In dimension 3, the only orientable one is
the torus. The only other example in dim 3 is
any complement of a hyperbolic knot (not
torus knot, not satelite knot).

\begin{theorem}[M C Reynolds, '09]
\label{theorem-ferrari-reynolds}
Every orientable flat $N$-manifold arises as
cusp type of some hyperbolic
$(N+1)$-manifold.
\end{theorem}

\label{theorem-ferrari-nimershwin}

\medskip\noindent
{\bf Classical gauge theory}
\cite{yangmills}. In electromagnetism the
gauge group is $\mathsf{U}(1)$ and the field
is a connection $A$, locally a $1$-form on
space-time. Its curvature $F=dA$ has the
electric and magnetic fields as components.
In Yang--Mills theory, or non-abelian gauge
theory, $\mathsf{U}(1)$ is replaced by a
compact group $G$ and $A$ by a connection
on a principal $G$-bundle. Maxwell's
equations without charges, and the
Yang--Mills equations, read
$$
\begin{aligned}
F&=dA, & dF&=0, & d{*}F&=0,\\
F&=dA+A\wedge A, & d_AF&=0,
& d_A{*}F&=0,
\end{aligned}
$$
where $*$ is the Hodge star and $d_A$ the
gauge-covariant extension of $d$. The
Yang--Mills equations are the
Euler--Lagrange equations of
$$
S(A)=\frac{1}{4g^2}\int
\operatorname{Tr}F\wedge{*}F,
$$
with $\operatorname{Tr}$ an invariant
quadratic form on the Lie algebra of $G$.
They are nonlinear, and like Maxwell's
equations they describe massless waves
travelling at the speed of light.

\medskip\noindent
{\bf The problem} \cite{yangmills}. A
quantum field theory on $\mathbb{R}^4$ has a
Hilbert space $\mathcal{H}$ with a
representation of the Poincar\'e group. The
Hamiltonian $H$ and momentum $\vec{P}$
generate time and space translations. One
requires positive energy, $H\geq 0$, and a
Poincar\'e-invariant vacuum vector
$\Omega\in\mathcal{H}$, unique up to a
phase; then $H\Omega=0$. The theory has a
{\it mass gap} $\Delta>0$ if the spectrum of
$H$ meets $[0,\Delta)$ only in $0$.

{\it Yang--Mills existence and mass gap.}
Prove that for any compact simple gauge
group $G$, a non-trivial quantum Yang--Mills
theory exists on $\mathbb{R}^4$ and has a
mass gap $\Delta>0$. Existence includes
establishing axiomatic properties at least
as strong as the Wightman axioms and the
Osterwalder--Schrader axioms.

\medskip\noindent
{\bf Douglas' report} \cite{reportdouglas}.
On the lattice $\mathbb{Z}^4$ a
$G$-connection is a map from edges to $G$,
and the action $S$ is a sum of squared
curvatures of plaquettes. On a finite
subgraph $\gamma$ the partition function
$Z=\int\prod_i dU_i\, e^{-S/g^2}$ is an
integral over holonomies $U_i$ with Haar
measure. Existence asks for a limit over
larger $\gamma$ satisfying the
Osterwalder--Schrader axioms. Similar
theories are the two-dimensional sigma
model with target $G$ and four-dimensional
supersymmetric Yang--Mills theories; for
the latter Seiberg and Witten (1994)
addressed the mass gap.

\medskip\noindent
{\bf Gross' lecture} \cite{gross-video}.
Electromagnetism has a dimensionless
coupling, $\alpha=e^2/\hbar c\approx
1/137.036$, and no intrinsic scale. Quantum
electrodynamics, electromagnetism coupled
to electrons, has never been constructed
and is believed not to exist down to
arbitrarily short distances; for the same
reasons non-abelian gauge theories are
believed to exist. Their mass gap is a
scale produced by quantum effects: the
spectrum of the mass operator
$P_\mu P^\mu$ is $0$ (the vacuum), an
isolated value $m_G^2$ (one particle), and
a continuum from $(2m_G)^2$ (several
particles).

\medskip\noindent
{\bf Lucas, January 17.} {\it Standard
model.} Fermions are the matter particles:
quarks $u,d,c,s,t,b$ and
leptons $e^-,\mu^-,\tau^-$,
$\nu_e,\nu_\mu,\nu_\tau$. A proton is $uud$
and a neutron $udd$, so neither is
elementary. Neutrinos were long assumed to
be massless. Gauge bosons carry the forces:
the photon $\gamma$ for electromagnetism,
with gauge group $\mathsf{U}(1)$; $W^\pm$
and $Z$ for the weak force, which together
with $\gamma$ form the electroweak theory
with gauge group
$\mathsf{SU}(2)\times\mathsf{U}(1)$; gluons
$g$ for the strong force, quantum
chromodynamics, with gauge group
$\mathsf{SU}(3)$. There is also the scalar
Higgs boson $H$. Every particle has an
antiparticle of the same mass and opposite
charge.

{\it Classical versus quantum.} A classical
theory has a space of fields $A$, an action
$S(A)=\int_M\mathcal{L}(A)$, and equations
of motion $\delta S=0$. In the Euclidean
quantum theory one wants
$$
Z=\int e^{-S(A)/\hbar}\,dA,\qquad
\left\langle{} O \right\rangle{}
=\frac{1}{Z}\int O(A)
e^{-S(A)/\hbar}\,dA.
$$
The measure $dA$ is usually ill-defined. As
$\hbar\to 0$ the integrand concentrates at
the minima of $S$, which recovers the
equations of motion.

\begin{example}
\label{example-ym-chern-simons}
Let $M$ be a $3$-manifold and $G$ a compact
group. The Chern--Simons action is
$$
S_{CS}(A)=\frac{k}{4\pi}\int_M
\operatorname{tr}\left(A\wedge dA
+\frac{2}{3}A\wedge A\wedge A\right),
$$
with $Z=\int e^{iS_{CS}(A)/\hbar}\,dA$; see
\cite[(3.7)]{moore-chern-simons}.
\end{example}

Other fields can be Riemannian metrics on $M$
(quantum gravity); maps $M\to N$ to a fixed
manifold $N$ (sigma models; in dimension
one this is quantum mechanics); or, for a
principal $G$-bundle $P\to M$, connections
on $P$, the gauge fields, together with
sections of associated vector bundles, the
matter fields (quantum gauge theory).

\label{section-spheres-with-minimal-equators}
%% speaker: Lucas Ambrozio, IMPA

\noindent
Lucas Ambrozio, IMPA.
Differential Geometry Seminar, IMPA.
June 24, 2025.

\medskip\noindent
{\bf Abstract.} We will discuss the
connection between Riemannian metrics on the
sphere with respect to which all equators are
minimal hypersurfaces, and
algebraic curvature tensors with positive
sectional curvatures.

\bigskip\noindent

\begin{definition}
\label{definition-equator}
An {\it $(n-k)$-equator} orthogonal to $\Pi$
is
$$
\Sigma_\Pi:=\{p\in\mathbb{S}^n:\left\langle{}p,
x\right\rangle{}=0\forall
x\in\Pi\}
$$
for $\Pi$ a $k$-dimensional linear subspace
of $\mathbb{R}^{n+1}$.
\end{definition}

\begin{remark}
\label{remark-equat-total-geode-usual-metr}
Equators are totally geodesic hypersurfaces
with the usual sphere metric, which
implies they are minimal hypersurfaces.
\end{remark}

{\bf Problem.} Characterize the set
$\mathcal{M}_k(U)$ of metrics $g$ on an
open set $U\subset\mathbb{S}^n$ such that all
$k$-equators $\Sigma_\Pi$ with
 $\Sigma\cap U\neq\emptyset$ yield are
 minimal hypersurfaces $\Sigma\cap U$
on $(U,g)$.

\begin{remark}
\label{remark-why-not-Rn}
This problem can be thought of as a problem
of finding metrics on $\mathbb{R}^n$
such that $k$-planes are minimal. To see why
project the $k$-equators to
$T_p\mathbb{S}^n$ and pullback those metrics
to the sphere.
\end{remark}

\medskip\noindent

Let $g\in\mathcal{M}_k(U)$ for
$U\subset\mathbb{S}^n$ open and $n\geq 2$.

\begin{theorem}[Beltrami, Schäfli]
\label{theorem-Beltrami-Schafli}
If $k=1$ then $g$ has constant sectional
curvature.
\end{theorem}

\begin{theorem}[Hongan]
\label{theorem-Hongan}
If $1<k<n-1$ then $g$ has constant sectional
curvature.
\end{theorem}

Then Hongan also managed to produce a
classification of these metrics for
$k=n-1$.

\begin{remark}
\label{remark-linea-inver-prese-equat-mg}
If $T\in \text{GL}(n+1,\mathbb{R})$, then
$$
\begin{aligned}
\varphi: \mathbb{S}^n &\longrightarrow
\mathbb{S}^n \\
x &\longmapsto \frac{Tx}{|Tx|}
\end{aligned}
$$
is a diffeomorphism that maps $k$-equators
into $k$-equators. Thus if
$g\in\mathcal{M}_k(\mathbb{S}^n)$ then so is
$\varphi(T)^*g$.
\end{remark}

\begin{theorem}
\label{theorem-equivariant-bijection}
There exists a $\text{GL}(n+1,\mathbb{R})$
equivariant bijection
$$
\mathcal{M}_{n-1}(\mathbb{S}^n)
\leftrightarrow
\text{Curv}_+(\mathbb{R}^{n+1})
$$
where the set on the right-hand-side is the
set of algebraic curvature tensors
(also called curvature-like, i.e. with the
same symmetries as the Riemannian
curvature tensor) on $\mathbb{R}^{n+1}$ with
positive sectional curvature.

The group action is given as follows for
$T\in\text{GL}(n+1,\mathbb{R})$:
$$
(R\cdot
T)(x,y,z,w)=\frac{1}{|\det(T)|^{\frac{1}{n+1}
}}R(Tx,Ty,Tz,Tw)
$$
\end{theorem}

The point is that
$\text{Curv}_+(\mathbb{R}^{n+1})$ is an open
cone on a linear
space. Here are two simple corollaries:

\begin{corollary}
\label{lemma-corollaries}
\begin{enumerate}
\item $\mathcal{M}_{n+1}(\mathbb{S}^n)$ is in
bijection with an open positive
cone of an
$\frac{n(n+2)(n+1)^2}{12}$-dimensional real
vector space.
\item Every metric on
$\mathcal{M}_{n-1}(\mathbb{S}^n)$ is
invariant by the
antipodal map.
\end{enumerate}
\end{corollary}

{\bf Algorithm.} From any $R\in
\text{Curv}_p(\mathbb{R}^{n+1})$ we obtain a
symmetric positive definite
(positive-definitiness comes from the
positiveness
of the curvature of $R$) 2-tensor  $k_R$
satisfying
$$
(k_R)_p(v,v)=R(pv,pv)>0
$$
Also, $k_R$ has the  {\it Killing property},
i.e. that $\overline{\nabla}k(X,X,X)=0$ for
all
$X\in\mathfrak{X}(\mathbb{S}^n)$.

Then we define a positive function on
 $\mathbb{S}^n$ by
\begin{equation}
\label{equation-def-DR}
D_R:=\left(\frac{d\text{Vol}_{k_R}}{dV_g}
\right)^{\frac{4}{n-1}}
\end{equation}
and finally a Riemannian metric on
$\mathbb{S}^n$ in
$\mathcal{M}_{n-1}\mathbb{S}^n$ by
$$
g_R=\frac{1}{D_R}k_R
$$
And to go back, for $g \in
\mathcal{M}_{n-1}(\mathbb{S}^n)$ define a
positive
function on $\mathbb{S}^n$
$$
F_g:=\left(\frac{dV_g}{dV_{\overline{g}}}
\right)^{\frac{4}{n-1}}
$$
Then let $k_g:=\frac{1}{F_g}g>0$, which is a
positive definite Killing 2-tensor,
from which we may define
$R_g\in\text{Curv}_+(\mathbb{R}^{n+1})$ with
$R_g(pv,pv)=(k_g)_p(v,v)$ for all $p,v\in
T\mathbb{S}^n$.

More corollaries:
\begin{corollary}
\label{lemma-corollaries2}
\begin{enumerate}
\item $g\in\mathcal{M}_{n-1}(\mathbb{S}^n)$
is analytic because it is a Killing
tensor on $\mathbb{S}^n$, which are
well-known.
\item If $g$ is left-invatiant on
$\mathbb{S}^3$, seen as unit quaternions,
then
$g\in\mathcal{M}_2(\mathbb{S}^3$. Moreover,
for $a\geq b\geq c>0$,
$$
aL_i\odot L_i+bL_j\odot L_j+cL_k\odot L_k=k
$$
is Killing, $k>0$,  $D_k$ constant and thus
$g=\frac{1}{\text{const.}}k
\in\mathcal{M}_2(\mathbb{S}^3)$.
\item $R$ curvature tensor of
$(\mathbb{C}P^{2},g_{FS})$. We may not
remember
what's the curvature tensor, but we know the
sectional curvature is $1\leq
\text{sec}(R)\leq 4$,
$$
(k_R)_p(v,w)=\overline{g}(v,w)+3\overline{g}
(Jp,v)\overline{g}(Jp,w)
$$
and $D_R=4^{\frac{4}{3-1}}=4$, so that by
\ref{equation-def-DR} we obtain
$g_R=\frac{1}{4}k_R$, which is a Berger
metric on $\mathbb{S}^3$ with scalar
curvature 0.
\end{enumerate}
\end{corollary}

$$
\Sigma_V=\{p\in\mathbb{S}^n:\left\langle{}p,v\right\rangle{}=
0\}=V^{-1}(0)
$$
where $V(x):=\left\langle{}x,v\right\rangle{}$ for all
$x\in\mathbb{S}^n$. Then the normal

given by

$$
and its mean curvature by
\begin{equation}
\label{equation-mean-curva-level-sets-V}

\end{equation}
For every $v \in \mathbb{S}^n$ and

V|^2_g(p)\Delta_gV(p)-\text{Hess}_gV(\nabla

$$
And for $\overline{g}$,
$$
\text{Hess}_{\overline{g}}V+V\overline{g}=
0\implies
\text{Hess}_{\overline{g}}V(X,X)=0
$$
for all $X\in T_p\mathbb{S}^n$ and
$p\in\Sigma_v$. Then
$$
J_g(X,Y,Z)=g(\nabla_XY-\overline{\nabla}_XY,
Z)
$$
$$
J_g(X,Y,\nabla
V)=\text{Hess}_{\overline{g}}-\text{Hess}

\section{Manifolds of constant sectional
curvature}
\label{section-manif-const-secti-curva}
%% speaker: Dimitri Korshunov, IMPA
%% place: Geometric Structures Seminar, IMPA
%% date: July 3, 2025

The following theorem and proof where
explained by Dimitri Korshunov (IMPA) at
Geometric Structures Seminar on July 3, 2025.

{\bf Abstract.} Crystallographic group is a
discrete co-compact subgroup of the
group of isometries of a Euclidean space. A
theorem of Bieberbach says that it
contains a subgroup of translations of finite
index. Or, in other words, any
compact flat Riemannian manifold is a finite
quotient of a flat torus. We will
prove this theorem following a streamlined
geometric argument of Vinberg.

\begin{theorem}[Bieberbach]
\label{theorem-Bieberbach}
In particular, any flat manifold is a
quotient of a torus.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}[Sketch of proof]
First we show that it suffices to find only
one pure translation, that is, an
inductive argument shows that if we have a
pure translation, then we can find
others. Perhaps this is the hardest part.

Then we obtain some bounds in operator norm
for the pure translations, this part
looks straightforward.  One of these bounds
is that $\|[A,B]-\text{Id}\|\leq
2\|\text{Id}-A\|\|\text{Id}-B\|$. The other
of the bounds is roughly as follows.
For $g=Ax+a$ and $h=Bx+b$, we have that
$[g,h]=[A,B]x+c$ for some $c$ whose norm
we shall bound by $r$ later.

We use the first bound to show that if two
elements in the group are close to
the identity, then their commutator will be
even closer. This shows that there
is a finite set of small generators
$g_1,\ldots,g_k$. Each of them is of the
form $g_i=A_ix+a_i$.

To arrive at a contradiction suppose that
there are no pure translations to
obtain a contradiction.

Let $r(g)$ be the norm of the translational
part of a transformation $g$. Let
$r$ be the maximum of the translational parts
of the $g_i$.
Let $S:=\{g:r(g)<r\}$. It's a finite set. Let
$g_0$ be minimal in $S$. After
some discussion we show that the linear part
of $g_0$ commutes with the linear
part of $g_i$ for all $i$. In fact, this
implies that $g_0$ itself is in the
center of the group.

Finally we obtain a contradiction with
compactness… simple argument.
\end{proof}


\section{Systolic inequality for tori}
\label{section-systolic-inequality}
%% speaker: Dimitri Korshunov, IMPA
%% place: Minimal Surfaces end of course
%% place: presentation
%% date: October 24, 2025

\noindent
Dimitri Korshunov, IMPA.
Minimal Surfaces end of course presentation.
October 24, 2025.

\medskip\noindent
This is due to L. Guth.

\begin{definition}
\label{definition-systole}
Given a Riemannian manifold,
a {\it systole} $\text{Sys}(M,g)$ is the
infimum of the lengths
of noncontractible paths.
\end{definition}

\begin{theorem}[Gromov]
\label{theorem-gromov}
For any metric on a torus $T^n$,
$$
\text{Sys}(T^n,g) \leq C_n
\text{Vol}(T^n,g)^{\frac{1}{n}},
$$
where $C_n$ is a constant depending only on
the dimension
of the torus $n$.
\end{theorem}

\noindent
This also holds for $\mathbb{R}P^n$. We will
use induction,
for which we need the following stronger
statement:
given a number bounded by half the systole,
there exists a ball
which is {\it big}:

\begin{theorem}[Guth]
\label{theorem-guth}
For $(T^n,g)$, given
$R<\frac{1}{2}\text{Sys}(T^n,g)$,
then there exists a point $p$ on $T^n$
such that the volume of ball of radius $r$
with center in $p$
satisfies
$$
\text{Vol}B(p,R) \geq C_nR^n.
$$
\end{theorem}

\noindent
It was conjectured by Gromov that $C_n=1$.

\medskip\noindent
Before stating the theorem that we will
prove, we introduce the
following definition that will allow us to
measure cohomological cycles:

\begin{definition}
\label{definition-measu-cohom-cycle}
Let $\alpha \in H^{1}(M_g,\mathbb{Z}_2)$.
$$
L(\alpha)
=\text{inf}\{|\lambda|:\lambda\text{
1-cycles, }\alpha(\lambda)\neq 0\}.
$$
\end{definition}

\noindent
The theorem that we will prove, which implies
Theorem \ref{theorem-guth}
is the following

\begin{theorem}
\label{theorem-bound-classes}
There exists a constant $\varepsilon_n>0$
such that given a manifold $(M,g)$, and
$\alpha_1,\ldots,\alpha_n \in
H^1(M,\mathbb{Z}_2)$,
\begin{enumerate}
\item $L(\alpha_i)>2R$
\label{item-positive}
\item
\label{item-cup} $\alpha_1 \cup  \alpha_2
\cup \ldots \cup \alpha_n \neq 0 \in
H^n(M,\mathbb{Z})$.
\end{enumerate}

\noindent
Then there exists $p \in M$ such that
$$
\text{Vol}B(p,R) \geq \varepsilon_nR^n.
$$
\end{theorem}

\noindent
In particular $T^n$ and $\mathbb{R}P^n$
satisfy (\ref{item-cup}).

Before we proceed to the proof we need the
following definition.
Essentially we ask that the surface minimizes
volume in its homology
class up to some constant $\delta$.

\begin{definition}
\label{definition-delta-minimal-surface}
A  {\it $\delta$-minimal surface} is an
embedded hypersurface $Z$
such that for any $Z'$ such that $[Z'-Z] = 0$
in  $H_{n-1}(M,\mathbb{Z}_2)$,
then  $\text{Vol}Z < \text{Vol}Z'+\delta$.
\end{definition}

\noindent
Moreover, we need the following

\begin{lemma}[Stability]
\label{lemma-stability}
Let $(M,g)$ de closed Riemannian manifold and
$\alpha \in H^1(M,\mathbb{Z}_2)$.
Suppose $Z$ is Poincaré dual to $\alpha$ and
that $Z$ is $\delta$-minimal.
If $R<\frac{1}{2}L(\alpha$) then the
following
holds for any $p \in M$:
$$
|Z \cap \text{Vol}B(p,\frac{R}{2}|\leq
\frac{2}{R}|\text{Vol}B(p,R)|+\delta.
$$
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}[Proof of the stability lemma]
\medskip\noindent
Step 1. Using commas formula, namely
$$
\text{Vol}(B_R)=\int_0^R S_tdt,
$$
we see that there exists $t$ such that
$\frac{R}{2}<t<R$ and
$$
|S(p,t)|_R< \frac{2}{R}|\text{Vol}B(p,R)|.
$$

\medskip\noindent
Step 2. Consider
$[Z \cap B(p,t)] \in
H^{n-1}(B(p,t),S(p,t),\mathbb{Z})$.
This can be interpreted geometrically since
we are using relative homology.

\medskip\noindent
Step 3. We claim that $[Z \cap B(p,t)]=0$.
By contradiction,
if it weren't trivial, then there would exist
a cycle $H_1(B,\mathbb{Z})$
such that $Z' \cap \gamma \neq 0$ since
Poincaré pairing is nondegenerate ---
namely any cocycle that is not zero pairs
nontrivially with some cycle.
Then we will just split the cocycle in very
small parts,
and we will reach a contradiction with the
fact that
$R<\frac{1}{2}L(\alpha)$.

We will state this as another lemma.
Essentially it says that any
cycle in homology can be decomposed as a sum
of cycles
with small lengths.

\begin{lemma}[Curve factoring, Gromov]
\label{lemma-curve-factoring-gromov}
Let $(M,g)$ be a Riemannian manifold and
$\gamma$ a cycle in $B(p,t)$.
Then $[\gamma]=\sum \sigma_i$ is such that
 $L(\sigma_i)<2t+a$ for all $i$.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
We spit a cycle (a curve) into some quantity
of intervals
such that each has length smaller than $a$,
then add paths joining the center point of
length $t$,
so we count $t$ twice: to reach the point and
to get away from it.
\end{proof}

\noindent
We apply this to $\gamma$ and reach a
contradiction
with the hypothesis that
$R<\frac{1}{2}L(\alpha)$ making $a$ smaller
and
smaller.

\medskip\noindent
Step 4. Finally we use the minimality of $Z$
and the property
derived from Commas lemma to arrive at a
contradiction.
\end{proof}

\noindent
Now we prove Theorem
\ref{theorem-bound-classes}:

\begin{proof}
By induction. The case $n=1$ is trivial.
Now take the last one of our classes,
$\alpha_n$.
Let $Z$ be Poincaré dual of $\alpha_n$ and
$\delta$-minimal.
Notice that $\alpha_1\cup \ldots \cup
\alpha_{n-1}\neq 0$
since if it were, then $\alpha_1\cup
\ldots\cup \alpha_n$ would also vanish.

By induction hypothesis we get a point $p \in
Z$
such that $\text{Vol} B_Z(p,\frac{R}{2}) >
R^{n-1}$.
This means that , $|Z \cap
B(p,\frac{R}{2})|>\varepsilon_n(R/2)^{n-1}$.

By the Stability Lemma \ref{lemma-stability},
$$
\begin{aligned}
\frac{2}{R}|B(p,R)|&>\varepsilon_n\frac{R^{n-
1}}{2^{n-1}}-\delta\\
\implies
|B(p,B)|&>\frac{\varepsilon_n}{2^n}R^n-
\frac{\delta
R}{2}
> \frac{\varepsilon_n}{2^n}R^n.
\end{aligned}
$$
\end{proof}

\section{Hyperbolic surfaces and Mirzakhani's
curve counting}
\label{section-hyper-surfa-mizak-curve}
%% speaker: Viveka Erlandson, University of
%% speaker: Bristol
%% place: 8th Brazilian School of Dynamical
%% place: Systems, IMPA
%% date: November 4, 2025

\noindent
Viveka Erlandson, University of Bristol, UK.
8th Brazilian School of Dynamical Systems,
IMPA.
November 4, 2025.

\medskip\noindent
{\bf Abstract.}
It’s a classical problem to study the growth
of the number of periodic orbits of
bounded length $L$ under a geodesic flow on a
manifold. When it comes to
(closed) hyperbolic surfaces Huber proved
that the number is asymptotic to $e^L
L$ and this results has since been
generalized in many directions, maybe most
famously by Margulis to negatively curved
manifolds as well as more general
flows. However, a fundamentally different
result is due to Mirzakhani who
counted the subset of those closed geodesics
on hyperbolic surfaces which have
no self-intersections (or more generally,
those inside a fixed mapping class
group orbit) and showed that in this setting
the growth is asymptotic to a
polynomial in L of degree only depending on
the topology of the surface. In this
mini-course we will give the necessary
background on hyperbolic surfaces to
understand these results, give an idea of the
proof of Mirzakhani’s theorem and
introduce some tools used such as measured
laminations and train tracks. Time
allowing we present some generalizations (for
example, to non-simple closed
geodesics or to various metrics) and
applications to the distribution of closed
geodesics.

\medskip\noindent
Our goal is to count certain closed geodesics
on hyperbolic surfaces.
By ``certain'' we main, for example, simple
or fixed topological type;
we will define these properly. Also, we may
treat more general
types of curves than only hyperbolic, e.g.
combinatorial, algebraic.
As ``closed geodesics'', we mean free
homotopy classes of curves.

As historical note,
results on coarse polynomial growth for
simple curves
were are due to Birman-Serres, Rees in the
80's.
In 2001, McShane-Runn studied asymptotic
growth
for simple curves on punctured torus.
Asymptoric growth on any hyperbolic
surface was proved by Mirzakhani for simple
curves in 2008
and then in 2016 for any topological type of
curve.

Here's an outline of this minicourse:
\begin{itemize}
\item Introduction, motivation.
\item Measured laminations, train tracks,
Thurston measure.
\item  (Geodesic) currents.
\item Curve counting theorem.
\end{itemize}

\medskip\noindent
Throughout $S$ will be a topological
(connected, orientable
of finite type, i.e. finite genus). Let
$g<\infty$ be the genus
and $r< \infty$ the number of punctures.
We assume that $\chi(S)=2-2g-r<0$,
which is equivalent to saying that $S$
admits hyperbolic metrics.
We will not consider the pair of pants,
since there's only three simple closed
geodesics on it,
making it uninteresting for our means.

Let $X$ be a hyperbolic surface homeomorphic
to $S$.
Consider the universal cover
$\tilde{X}=\mathbb{H}^2$ of $X$,
and
$\pi_1(X)=\Gamma\underbrace{<}
_{\substack{\text{discrete}
\\ \text{free}}}
\text{PSL}(2,\mathbb{R})=\text{Isom}^+
(\mathbb{H}^2)$.
Recall that a $4g$-gon yields a genus $g$
surface $S_g$ by
adequate identification of its sides.

For us, a {\it curve} $\gamma$ is a free
homotopy class
of a closed immersion $\mathbb{S}^1
\hookrightarrow S$,
which are closed $X$-geodesics.
In turn, a {\it multi-curve}
is a formal sum $\sum_{i=1}^na_i\gamma_i$
for $\gamma_i$ distinct curves and $a_i>0$,
$a_i \in \mathbb{Q}$.
The {\it length} of a curve $\gamma$ is
$\ell_X(\gamma)$ is the length of
$X$-geodesic representing $\gamma$,
and the length of a multi-curve is the sum of
the lengths
of the curves in the formal sum.

We won't use this: we can think of $X$ as
an element of Teichmüller space
$\mathcal{T}(S)\cong \mathbb{R}^{6g-6+2r}$
parametrizing complex structures on $S$.

\medskip\noindent
Notice that given $X \in\mathcal{T}(S)$,
the number of curves with length bounded by a
given number $L$,
$\#\{\gamma|\ell_X(\gamma)\leq L\}$,
is finite. This can be checked going to the
universal cover.
However, as $L \to \infty$, the number also
tends to infinity.

Huber, Hekhal (59' or later) showed that if
$X$ is hyperbolic
on $S_{g,r}$, then
$\#\{\gamma|\ell_X(\gamma)\leq L\}\sim
\frac{e^L}{L}$
asymptotically, that is,
$$
\lim_{L\to \infty}\frac{\#
\{\gamma|\ell_X(\gamma)\leq L\}}{e^L/L}=1.
$$

\medskip\noindent
What is we restrict to a fixed topological
type?

Recall that the {\it mapping class group} of
$S$ is
$\text{Map}(S):=\text{Homeo}^+(S)/
\text{homotopy}$.
Then $\gamma$ and $\gamma_0$ are of the same
(topological)
type if $\gamma \in \text{Map}(S)\gamma_0$.

We define the {\it (geometric) intersection}
of $\alpha,\beta$
distinct curves by
$$
i(\alpha,\beta)=\text{min}\{\#(\alpha' \cap
\beta'|
\substack{\alpha'\text{ homotopic to }\alpha
\\
\beta'\text{ homotopic to }\beta}\}.
$$
(In fact, this minimum is always realized by
geodesics,
but it's more a topological definition.)
The {\it self-intersection} of a curve is
$$
i(\alpha,\alpha)=\frac{1}{2}\text{min}
\{\#(\alpha' \cap
\alpha''|\alpha',\alpha''\text{
homotopic to }\alpha\}.
$$

We say $\gamma$ is {\it simple} if
$i(\gamma,\gamma)=0$.

It is known that $i(\cdot,\cdot)$ is
$\text{Map}(S)$-invariant,
and that $\{\gamma:i(\gamma,\gamma)=k\}$ is a
finite union
of topological types.

\begin{theorem}[Mirzakhani, '08, '16]
\label{theorem-mirzakhani}
Let $X$ be a hyperbolic surface, $X \cong
S_{g,r}$
and $\gamma_0$ any (multi-)curve.
Then
$$
\#\{\gamma\text{ type
}\gamma_0|\ell_X(\gamma)\leq L\}
\sim CL^{6g-6+2r},
$$
where $C>0$,
$C=\frac{c(\gamma_0)}{b_\gamma}B(X)$.
\end{theorem}

\noindent
As a corollary, we obtain
$$
\begin{aligned}
\#\{\gamma\text{ simple}|\ell_X(\gamma)\leq
L\}&
\sim\text{const.}L^{6g-6+2r}\\
\#\{\gamma|i(\gamma,\gamma)\leq
k,\ell_X(\gamma)\leq L\}
&\sim (\text{const. depending on
$k$})L^{6g-6+2r}.
\end{aligned}
$$

{\bf Abstract.}
Let $f$ be a partially hyperbolic
diffeomorphism preserving a splitting
$
TM = E^s \oplus E^c \oplus E^u,
$
where the unstable bundle $E^u$ is
one-dimensional.
When $f$ is homogeneous, like the time-one
map of the geodesic flow on a surface of
constant negative curvature or a linear
automorphism of the torus, the unstable
foliation admits a natural parametrization
that makes invariant measures with
absolutely continuous conditionals
(the so-called $u$-Gibbs measures)
correspond exactly to invariant measures
of this flow.
How can this picture be generalized when
$f$ is not homogeneous?
In this talk, I will explain a construction
of a suitable suspension flow of $f$ for
which the unstable foliation becomes the
orbit foliation of a new flow, called the
unstable flow.
Ergodic $u$-Gibbs measures are in
one-to-one correspondence with measures
simultaneously invariant under both flows
and ergodic for the suspension.
As an application, I will deduce the
following measure rigidity statement:
if $E^c$ is expanding and the bundle
$E^s \oplus E^u$ is integrable, then any
$u$-Gibbs measure whose entropy exceeds its
unstable entropy is necessarily an SRB
measure.
This is joint work with Sébastien
Alvarez, Sylvain Crovisier, Martin
Leguil, and Davi Obata.

\end{definition}

\end{definition}

\times 
\underbrace{\text{SO}(3)^{[K:\mathbb{Q}]-r}}_{
\text{compact}}
$$
That is $\text{SL}_2(\mathbb{R})^r$

$\Gamma$ being arithmetic.

$r$ is the arithmetic dimension.

\begin{definition}
\label{definition-arithmetic-group}
Let $\Gamma$ be a Fuchsian group of finite
covolume, and let $K$ be its invariant trace
field. We say that $\Gamma$ is
{\it arithmetic} if $\Gamma^{(2)}$ is
commensurable with $\mathrm{SL}_2(\mathcal{O}_K)$.
\end{definition}

What is happening here is that one starts
with a group of matrices over the number
field $K$ and then views it inside the much
larger real Lie group
$\mathrm{SL}_2(K \otimes_\mathbb{Q}\mathbb{R})$.
After extending scalars, this ambient group
splits into one or more noncompact
$\mathrm{SL}_2(\mathbb{R})$ factors and a
product of compact factors. The projection
diagram is the way of choosing the real
factor that gives the actual action on the
hyperbolic plane, while the other factors are
auxiliary arithmetic data. In the case
$r=1$, there is only one noncompact factor,
and that is exactly the arithmetic case;
when $r>1$, the same construction gives the
semi-arithmetic situation.

\medskip\noindent
{\bf On multiplicities in length spectra of
semiarithmetic surfaces.}
This was a talk by Misha Belolipetsky on
joint work with Gregory Cosac, Cayo Dória
and Gisele Teixeira Paula. The main result
is that certain semiarithmetic Riemann
surfaces have exponential growth of the
mean multiplicities in their length spectra.
Previously this phenomenon was known for
arithmetic surfaces; the point of the talk
was that it also occurs in some genuinely
semiarithmetic examples.

\medskip\noindent
The geometric object is a hyperbolic surface
$$
S=\mathbb{H}^2/\Gamma,
$$
where $\Gamma$ is a Fuchsian group, i.e. a
discrete subgroup of
$\operatorname{PSL}_2(\mathbb{R})$ of finite
coarea. We write $\mathcal{N}(\ell)$ for the
number of closed geodesics on $S$ of length
at most $\ell$. The Huber--Selberg theorem
says
$$
\mathcal{N}(\ell)\sim \frac{e^\ell}{\ell}.
$$
Thus the total number of closed geodesics
grows exponentially. The question of the talk
is subtler: if we list only the distinct
lengths
$$
0<\ell_1<\ell_2<\cdots,
$$
and write
$$
\mathcal{N}'(\ell)=\#\{n:\ell_n<\ell\},
$$
then how large is the average multiplicity
of a length? If $g(\ell_n)$ denotes the
number of closed geodesics with length
$\ell_n$, then morally
$$
\langle g(\ell)\rangle
\sim
\frac{\mathcal{N}(\ell)}{\mathcal{N}'(\ell)}.
$$
So the problem becomes: there are very many
closed geodesics, but how many different
lengths do they produce?

\medskip\noindent
The bridge from geometry to arithmetic is
given by traces. Closed geodesics correspond
to hyperbolic conjugacy classes in
$\Gamma$. If $\gamma\in\Gamma$ is
hyperbolic, then $|\operatorname{tr}\gamma|>2$
and
$$
\ell(\gamma)
=
2\operatorname{arccosh}
\left(\frac{|\operatorname{tr}\gamma|}{2}\right).
$$
Thus length is controlled by trace. In
particular, many different conjugacy classes
can have the same trace, hence the same
length. For example, in the modular group
$\operatorname{PSL}_2(\mathbb{Z})$, traces
are integers. Therefore the number of
possible trace values up to size
$2\cosh(\ell/2)$ is at most on the order of
$e^{\ell/2}$. Combining this with
Huber--Selberg gives
$$
\langle g(\ell)\rangle
\geq
\frac{e^\ell/\ell}{e^{\ell/2}}
=
\frac{e^{\ell/2}}{\ell}.
$$
This is the basic mechanism: if arithmetic
forces the trace set to be sparse, then many
geodesics must share the same length.

\medskip\noindent
Luo and Sarnak observed that one does not
need the trace set to be literally discrete
in $\mathbb{R}$. It is enough to have the
bounded clustering property:
$$
\#(\mathcal{L}(\Gamma)\cap[N-1,N])
\leq C
$$
for all integers $N$. Here
$\mathcal{L}(\Gamma)$ is the set of absolute
trace values of hyperbolic elements of
$\Gamma$. This condition says that traces
may cluster, but only in a uniformly bounded
way. Bounded clustering is enough to force
exponential growth of the average
multiplicity.

\medskip\noindent
This is where the commutative algebra enters.
Given a Fuchsian group $\Gamma$, its invariant
trace field is
$$
K=
\mathbb{Q}
\bigl(\{\operatorname{tr}(\gamma^2):
\gamma\in\Gamma\}\bigr).
$$
The semiarithmetic condition says that $K$ is
totally real and that the traces are
``integers'' in $K$, meaning
$$
\operatorname{tr}(\gamma)\in\mathcal{O}_K.
$$
Here $\mathcal{O}_K$ is the ring of integers
of the number field $K$. This is exactly the
notion of integrality from commutative
algebra: an element is integral over a ring
if it satisfies a monic polynomial with
coefficients in that ring. Thus
$$
\mathcal{O}_K
=
\{x\in K:
x\text{ satisfies a monic polynomial in }
\mathbb{Z}[t]\}.
$$
Equivalently, $\mathcal{O}_K$ is the
integral closure of $\mathbb{Z}$ inside
$K$. This was the important conceptual point:
these are not ordinary integers, but they
are the elements of $K$ that behave like
integers relative to $\mathbb{Z}$.

\medskip\noindent
The reason this helps is that algebraic
integers in a fixed number field form a
lattice-like object. Since $K$ is totally
real, it has several real embeddings
$$
\sigma_i:K\hookrightarrow\mathbb{R}.
$$
So one trace has several real shadows
$\sigma_i(\operatorname{tr}\gamma)$. One of
these embeddings gives the actual Fuchsian
group acting on $\mathbb{H}^2$, while the
other embeddings provide arithmetic
constraints. In the arithmetic case there is
only one noncompact real factor. In the
semiarithmetic case there may be more than
one noncompact factor, and this number is
called the arithmetic dimension. The proof
uses the fact that the traces lie in
$\mathcal{O}_K$ and that their conjugates are
controlled. By a pigeonhole/lattice-counting
argument this gives bounded clustering of
trace values.

\medskip\noindent
A guiding example is the golden triangle
group
$$
\Gamma=\Delta(2,5,\infty).
$$
Its trace field is
$$
K=\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{5}),
$$
with two real embeddings:
$$
\sigma_1(\sqrt{5})=\sqrt{5},
\qquad
\sigma_2(\sqrt{5})=-\sqrt{5}.
$$
The first embedding gives the actual
Fuchsian group. The second embedding gives
another real realization, which is not
discrete. This is typical of the
semiarithmetic situation: the group still has
arithmetic trace data, but it is not
arithmetic in the classical sense.

\medskip\noindent
The main story of the talk can therefore be
summarized as follows. Hyperbolic geometry
turns closed geodesics into conjugacy classes.
The length of a closed geodesic is determined
by a trace. If the traces live inside the
integer ring $\mathcal{O}_K$ of a number
field, commutative algebra gives arithmetic
control over how those traces can be
distributed. This control implies bounded
clustering, and bounded clustering forces
large average multiplicities in the length
spectrum. The new theorem says that this
mechanism works not only for arithmetic
surfaces, but also for certain
semiarithmetic surfaces.

\section{Ambient geometry via min-max
widths of embedded circles}
\label{section-ambient-circle-widths}
%% speaker: Ivan Miranda de Almeida, IMPA
%% place: Differential Geometry Seminar, IMPA,
%% place: room 236
%% date: September 22, 2026, 15:30

\noindent
Ivan Miranda de Almeida, IMPA.
Differential Geometry Seminar, IMPA.
September 22, 2026, 15:30, room 236.

\medskip\noindent
This is joint work with Lucas Ambrozio
and Rafael Montezuma.

\medskip\noindent
{\bf Basic pipeline of the project.}

The guiding idea is to extract global
information about the ambient metric from
an extremely simple configuration: two
points on an embedded circle.

Start with an embedded circle $\Gamma$ and
look at unordered pairs $\{p,q\}$ on it.
The ambient metric assigns to each pair the
number $d_g(p,q)$. Instead of studying one
pair, consider sweepouts by such pairs. A
sweepout is a continuous movie
$\{p_t,q_t\}$ of pairs: initially the two
points coincide, and as $t$ varies the arc
of $\Gamma$ between them grows continuously
from a point until it covers the whole
circle. For each such movie take the largest
ambient distance $d_g(p_t,q_t)$ attained,
and then minimize this maximum over all
sweepouts. This gives the number
$\mathcal{S}_g(\Gamma)$.

The min-max procedure forces this number to
be realized by a geometrically special pair,
namely a critical pair for the ambient
distance function. One can then compare
$\mathcal{S}_g(\Gamma)$ with invariants of
the whole ambient sphere, such as its
Birkhoff width.

Thus the rough pipeline is: begin with two
points; organize pairs into sweepouts; take
a min-max of their ambient distances; obtain
a critical geometric configuration; compare
the resulting width with global invariants
of the ambient manifold; and use sufficiently
strong equality or extremality statements to
seek rigidity of the ambient metric.

In particular, one possible kind of final
result in this program is a rigidity theorem:
under suitable extremal hypotheses, the
width data may force a metric on a sphere to
be geometrically special, potentially even
the round metric. This last sentence is a
motivation for the direction of the project,
not a statement of the theorem being proved
in this talk.

\medskip\noindent
{\bf First motivation: pairs of points.}

Let $\Gamma$ be an embedded circle.
Consider the space $P$ of subsets
$\{p,q\}\subset\Gamma$ with at most two
points. Equivalently, these are ordered
pairs modulo interchange of the two
entries. The space $P$ is a M\"obius band.
Its boundary consists of the singletons
$\{p\}$, that is, the pairs with $p=q$.

If all boundary points are identified to
one point, the quotient is
$
P^*\cong\mathbb{R}P^2.
$
This is the reason both the M\"obius band
and $\mathbb{R}P^2$ appear in the setup.

If $\Gamma\subset(M,g)$, define
$
D(\{p,q\})=d_g(p,q).
$
Thus the min-max problem is for the
ambient distance between pairs of points
of the embedded circle.

\medskip\noindent
{\bf Critical points without smoothness.}

The distance function need not be smooth
at the cut locus. Grove--Shiohama
introduced a substitute for the usual
notion of critical point.

\begin{definition}
Fix $p\in M$. A point $q\neq p$ is a
{\it critical point of the distance
function from $p$} if, for every nonzero
$v\in T_qM$, there is a minimizing
geodesic $\gamma$ from $q$ to $p$ such
that
$
\angle(v,\dot\gamma(0))\leq\frac{\pi}{2}.
$
\end{definition}

\noindent
The point is that this notion gives a
deformation theory for distance functions
even where the distance is not smooth:
regions containing no critical points can
be deformed through level sets, in the
spirit of Morse theory.

\begin{theorem}[Grove--Shiohama, 1977]
\label{theorem-grove-shiohama-diameter}
Let $(M,g)$ be complete and connected.
If
$
\sec_g\geq 1
$
and
$
\operatorname{diam}(M,g)>\frac{\pi}{2},
$
then $M$ is homeomorphic to a sphere.
\end{theorem}

\medskip\noindent
{\bf Sweepouts by pairs of points.}

\begin{definition}
A {\it sweepout of $\Gamma$ by pairs of
points} is a continuous family
$
\{p_t,q_t\}\subset\Gamma,
\qquad
t\in[0,1],
$
with $p_0=q_0$ and $p_1=q_1$, together
with a continuous choice of arcs
$C_t\subset\Gamma$ bounded by
$\{p_t,q_t\}$ such that
$
C_0=\emptyset,
\qquad
C_1=\Gamma.
$
\end{definition}

\noindent
Equivalently, a sweepout is a
homotopically nontrivial loop in
$P^*\cong\mathbb{R}P^2$ based at the
collapsed boundary point.

\begin{definition}
The {\it min-max width} of the embedded
circle $\Gamma\subset(M,g)$ is
$
\mathcal{S}_g(\Gamma)
=
\inf_{\{p_t,q_t\}}
\max_{t\in[0,1]}
d_g(p_t,q_t),
$
where the infimum is over sweepouts.
\end{definition}

\begin{theorem}[Ambrozio--Montezuma--Santos]
\label{theorem-ams-width-critical-pair}
For every smoothly embedded circle
$\Gamma$ in a complete Riemannian
manifold,
$
\mathcal{S}_g(\Gamma)>0.
$
Moreover, there is a critical pair
$\{p,q\}\subset\Gamma$ for $D$ such that
$
d_g(p,q)=\mathcal{S}_g(\Gamma).
$
\end{theorem}

\noindent
Here ``critical'' is the nonsmooth
distance-functional analogue of the
usual variational notion. Their second
main theorem gives a finer description
when $\Gamma$ bounds a totally convex
disc and there is no stable
free-boundary geodesic: nontrivial
critical pairs lie at distance at least
$\mathcal{S}_g(\Gamma)$, and a pair
realizing the width satisfies strong
restrictions on its minimizing
geodesics.

\medskip\noindent
{\bf Motivation for the present work.}

Fix the ambient Riemannian manifold
$(M,g)$. How large can the width of an
embedded circle be? Clearly,
$
\sup_{\Gamma\subset M}
\mathcal{S}_g(\Gamma)
\leq
\operatorname{diam}(M,g),
$
where the supremum is over embedded
circles.

The ellipsoid example suggests how one
might approach the diameter: take an
elongated ellipsoid, choose a large
embedded circle passing through
diametrically opposite points, and use a
foliation to organize the relevant
pairs.

\medskip\noindent
{\bf The Birkhoff invariant.}

For a Riemannian two-sphere, a Birkhoff
sweepout is a continuous one-parameter
family of closed curves that sweeps the
sphere from one point to another. One
can obtain the standard model by taking
latitudes on a sphere and composing with
a degree-one map.

The Birkhoff min-max invariant is
$
w_B(S^2,g)
=
\inf_{\{\gamma_t\}}
\max_{t\in[0,1]}
L_g(\gamma_t),
$
where the infimum is over Birkhoff
sweepouts.

The present problem is therefore to
compare two min-max constructions:
the width of an embedded circle, built
from distances between pairs of points,
and the Birkhoff invariant, built from
lengths of closed curves sweeping the
ambient sphere.

\medskip\noindent
{\bf An ambient upper bound.}

\begin{theorem}[Ambrozio--Miranda--Montezuma]
\label{theorem-amm-ambient-upper-bound}
For every Riemannian sphere $(S^2,g)$
and every embedded circle
$\Gamma\subset S^2$,
$
\mathcal{S}_g(\Gamma)
\leq
\frac{1}{2}w_B(S^2,g).
$
\end{theorem}

\noindent
For the round sphere of radius $R$ and a
great circle $\Gamma$,
$
\mathcal{S}_g(\Gamma)=\pi R,
\qquad
w_B(S^2,g)=2\pi R.
$
Thus equality holds in this example.
More generally, equality holds for convex
spheres with antipodal symmetry.

\medskip\noindent
{\bf Sweepout induced by a Morse function.}

\begin{theorem}[Induced sweepout]
\label{theorem-induced-sweepout-morse}
Let
$
f:S^1\longrightarrow\mathbb{R}
$
be a Morse function such that each critical
level contains a unique critical point.
Then there exists a sweepout
$\{p_t,q_t\}$ of $S^1$ such that
$
f(p_t)=f(q_t)
$
for every $t\in[0,1]$.
\end{theorem}

\medskip\noindent
{\bf AI summary of the paper.}


The paper develops a min-max invariant of
embedded circles and then asks what this
apparently one-dimensional invariant can say
about the ambient Riemannian manifold.

\medskip\noindent
{\bf Step 1. Define the invariant.}
Start with an embedded circle
$\Gamma\subset(M,g)$ and the ambient
distance function on unordered pairs of
points of $\Gamma$. Consider continuous
one-parameter families
$
t\longmapsto\{p_t,q_t\},
$
called sweepouts, in which the arc between
the two points starts as a point and grows
until it covers the whole circle. Define
$\mathcal{S}_g(\Gamma)$ by taking, for each
sweepout, the largest distance
$d_g(p_t,q_t)$ attained and then minimizing
this maximum over all sweepouts.

\medskip\noindent
{\bf Step 2. Show that the min-max number
comes from an actual special pair.}
For every sweepout, look at the moment when
$d_g(p_t,q_t)$ is largest. Then minimize this
largest value over all sweepouts. A priori,
$\mathcal{S}_g(\Gamma)$ is just a number
produced by this optimization procedure.
The important theorem says that there is an
actual pair $\{p,q\}\subset\Gamma$ with
$
d_g(p,q)=\mathcal{S}_g(\Gamma)
$
which is critical for the distance function.
Thus the min-max construction produces a
geometrically special pair of points, not
just a number. The ordinary derivative of
the distance function cannot always be used,
because distance is not smooth at the cut
locus. This is why the paper uses the
Grove--Shiohama notion of critical point for
distance functions.

\medskip\noindent
{\bf Step 3. Produce explicit sweepouts in
order to prove upper bounds.}
Since $\mathcal{S}_g(\Gamma)$ is an infimum
over all sweepouts, to prove an upper bound
for it we only need to exhibit one sweepout
for which all the distances
$d_g(p_t,q_t)$ are controlled. Thus we need
a systematic way to construct useful
sweepouts.

This is where Morse functions enter. Suppose
$
f:S^1\longrightarrow\mathbb{R}
$
is a Morse function with one critical point
on each critical level. Look at pairs of
points on the same level of $f$, so that
$
f(p_t)=f(q_t).
$
As the level changes, the two points move
around the circle. The Morse assumptions
allow these pairs to be chosen continuously
through the critical levels, in such a way
that the arc between them starts as a point
and eventually covers the whole circle.
Therefore these pairs form a sweepout.

This becomes useful in the ambient sphere
when $f$ comes from a family of level curves,
for example from a foliation. Restricting
$f$ to the embedded circle $\Gamma$ produces
a sweepout of $\Gamma$. If the geometry of
the level curves controls the distance
between the two intersection points, then it
controls $\mathcal{S}_g(\Gamma)$ from above.

\medskip\noindent
{\bf Step 4. Use the constructed sweepouts to
compare with classical geometric quantities.}
Once we know how to manufacture a sweepout
from a useful family of level curves, we can
estimate the distances between its two
points. This converts known geometric
constructions into upper bounds for
$\mathcal{S}_g(\Gamma)$.

For a plane curve, this compares the new
min-max width with the classical width of
the curve. On a Riemannian two-sphere, start
with a Birkhoff sweepout of the whole sphere
by closed curves. From it one constructs a
sweepout by pairs on $\Gamma$. Controlling
the distance between the two points by the
length of the corresponding Birkhoff curve
gives
$$
\mathcal{S}_g(\Gamma)
\leq
\frac{1}{2}w_B(S^2,g).
$$
Thus an invariant defined using pairs of
points on one embedded circle is controlled
by a min-max invariant of the whole ambient
sphere. The round sphere attains equality,
so the estimate is sharp.

\medskip\noindent
{\bf Step 5. Ask what equality tells us
about the geometry.}
An inequality becomes especially interesting
when we understand when equality can occur.
For the round sphere of radius $R$ and a
great circle,
$$
\mathcal{S}_g(\Gamma)=\pi R,
\qquad
w_B(S^2,g)=2\pi R,
$$
so equality holds in the previous estimate.
The project therefore asks whether equality,
or other extremal width behavior, forces the
ambient metric to have special geometry.
This is the rigidity direction of the work.
The philosophy is that very simple data from
distances between pairs of points may be
strong enough, in extremal situations, to
characterize much more of the ambient
metric.

\medskip\noindent
{\bf Step 6. Stop fixing one circle and ask
what all circle widths tell us about the
whole manifold.}
So far $\Gamma$ was fixed. Now consider a
complete noncompact manifold $(M,g)$ and ask
whether there is one constant $C$ such that
$$
\mathcal{S}_g(\Gamma)\leq C
$$
for every embedded circle $\Gamma\subset M$.
This is a global condition on the ambient
manifold, expressed entirely using the
widths of its embedded circles.

The theorem says that this happens precisely
under strong large-scale restrictions. In
particular, $M$ has at most two ends, and
there is a map from $M$ to a finite graph
whose fibers all have uniformly bounded
diameter. Very roughly, when viewed from far
away, $M$ can therefore be compressed onto a
one-dimensional graph without having to
collapse sets of arbitrarily large diameter.

This is the connection with Urysohn width:
the widths of all embedded circles detect
whether the ambient manifold is, in a
large-scale quantitative sense, essentially
one-dimensional.

\medskip\noindent
Putting the paper together, start with two
points on a circle. Let the pair move through
a sweepout. Apply min-max to their ambient
distance. Obtain a special critical pair.
Construct useful sweepouts using Morse
functions and level-set families. Use them to
compare the new width with classical global
invariants. Study equality to obtain rigidity
information. Finally, vary the embedded
circle and use all of these widths together
to detect the large-scale geometry of the
ambient manifold.



\section{Bifurcations of the Clifford torus as

Caio B. Rodrigues.
Differential Geometry Seminar, IMPA.

{\bf Abstract.}
The Clifford torus in a Berger sphere with

Willmore functional for every $\tau>0$,
yielding a smooth path of Willmore surfaces.
By estimating the Morse index along this
path, we apply bifurcation theory to produce
new symmetric Willmore tori emerging from
the Clifford torus.

\end{enumerate}



\section{Translation surfaces, billiards and
questions in differential geometry}
\label{section-matheus-translation-surfaces}

%% place: Differential Geometry Seminar, IMPA
%% date: November 3, 2026

\medskip\noindent
{\bf Date.} Tuesday 3 November 2026.

\medskip\noindent
{\bf Seminar.} Differential Geometry Seminar,

\medskip\noindent
{\bf Title and abstract.} Not available: the
IMPA seminar pages did not show this talk.
The section title above is descriptive, not
the official title.

\medskip\noindent
{\bf Sources of these notes.} An automatic
transcript (Whisper) of the audio, about 70
minutes, and Daniel's notes. There are no
photographs of the board, so formulas are
reconstructed from the transcript and from
the standard literature, and the transcript
is often garbled. Everything below is
``spoken'' unless marked otherwise.
Statements marked ``standard'' are well known
facts added by me (Claude) to make the talk

The speaker said it is a survey talk with few

\medskip\noindent
{\bf Billiards and unfolding.} Take a
billiard table, for example a square, and a
ball moving in a straight line, reflecting at
the walls. The trick: instead of reflecting
the trajectory, reflect the table. The
trajectory then continues as a straight line
through the reflected copy. For the square,
four copies (the square and its reflections),
glued up to translation, give a flat torus:
opposite sides of a big square are identified
by translations. So billiard trajectories in
the square become straight lines on a flat
torus.

For a polygon whose angles are rational
multiples of $\pi$ (a \emph{rational
polygon}) only finitely many reflected copies
appear up to translation, so the unfolded
surface is compact. The example in the talk
was a triangle whose unfolding is the regular
octagon with opposite sides identified.
Standard: the right triangle with angles
$\pi/2$, $\pi/8$, $3\pi/8$ unfolds to the
regular octagon. This surface has genus $2$.
The metric is flat everywhere except at one
point: all eight vertices of the octagon are
identified, and the total angle around that
point is
$$
8\cdot\frac{3\pi}{4}=6\pi,
$$
instead of $2\pi$. So the curvature is
concentrated at one cone point.

\medskip\noindent
{\bf Translation surfaces.}

\begin{definition}
\label{definition-translation-surface-matheus}
A \textit{translation surface} is a pair
$X=(M,\omega)$ where $M$ is a compact Riemann
surface and $\omega$ is a nonzero holomorphic
$1$-form on $M$. Equivalently, it is a finite
collection of polygons in $\mathbb{C}$ with
sides glued in pairs by translations, up to
cutting and pasting by translations.
\end{definition}

Spoken, why the two descriptions agree. From
polygons to $(M,\omega)$: away from the
vertices the charts are the polygons
themselves, and the transition maps are
translations, which are holomorphic. This is
a Riemann surface structure away from
finitely many points, which extends to them
by the removable singularity theorem. The
form $dz$ on $\mathbb{C}$ is invariant under
translations, so it descends to a holomorphic
$1$-form $\omega$. From $(M,\omega)$ to
charts: let $\Sigma$ be the set of zeros of
$\omega$. Near a point $p_0\in
M\setminus\Sigma$, the local primitive
$$
z(p)=\int_{p_0}^{p}\omega
$$
does not depend on the path in a simply
connected neighbourhood, since $\omega$ is
closed. These are charts on
$M\setminus\Sigma$, and two such charts
differ by a constant, so the changes of chart
are translations. This is the reason for the
name: the atlas of a translation surface has
translations as its changes of chart.

The flat metric is $|\omega|^2$, i.e.
$dx^2+dy^2$ in the charts above; it is well
defined since translations are isometries. It
is singular at $\Sigma$. The area form is
$dx\wedge dy$, which is also translation
invariant.

Near a zero $p$ of order $k$ one can choose a
coordinate with $\omega=z^k\,dz$. Standard:
then $w=z^{k+1}/(k+1)$ is a natural
coordinate, and the map $z\mapsto w$ is
$(k+1)$-to-$1$. So a neighbourhood of $p$ is
made of $k+1$ half-planes' worth of angle:
$p$ is a cone point of angle
$$
2\pi(k+1).
$$
Spoken: this is a cone with angle bigger than
$2\pi$, unlike the cones in $\mathbb{R}^3$,
so it is ``hyperbolic'' in the sense of
negative curvature concentrated at the point.

\medskip\noindent
{\bf Moduli spaces.} Standard: a holomorphic
$1$-form on a surface of genus $g$ has $2g-2$
zeros counted with multiplicity. For a
partition $\kappa_1+\dots+\kappa_n=2g-2$, the
\emph{stratum}
$$
\mathcal{H}(\kappa_1,\dots,\kappa_n)
$$
is the moduli space of translation surfaces
of genus $g$ whose zeros have orders
$\kappa_1,\dots,\kappa_n$, that is, with $n$
cone points of angles $2\pi(\kappa_i+1)$. Two
polygon presentations give the same point
when they differ by cutting and pasting by
translations.

Example (spoken): the square torus with
vertices $0,1,1+i,i$, and the parallelogram
with vertices $0,1,2+i,1+i$, are the same
translation surface: cut the parallelogram
along the vertical segment from $1$ to $1+i$
and translate the triangle on the right by
$-1$; you get the unit square with opposite
sides glued. A second example: three unit
squares in an L shape. Applying the matrix
$$
\begin{pmatrix} 1 & 2 \\ 0 & 1\end{pmatrix}
$$
shears the picture, but after cutting and
pasting one gets back the same surface.
Spoken: the top square is sheared ``twice''
and the bottom rectangle ``once''. My
explanation (not from the talk): the L shape
is made of two horizontal cylinders, one of
width $2$ and height $1$ and one of width $1$
and height $1$. The shear by $2$ is a full
Dehn twist on the first cylinder and a double
Dehn twist on the second, and a Dehn twist on
a cylinder is undone by cutting the cylinder
and regluing.

\medskip\noindent
{\bf The action of
$\mathrm{SL}_2(\mathbb{R})$.} A matrix in
$\mathrm{SL}_2(\mathbb{R})$ maps polygons to
polygons, and sends parallel sides of equal
length to parallel sides of equal length. So
it acts on translation surfaces, preserving
each stratum and the area. Spoken: orbit
closures are surprisingly nice, unlike in
many group actions where orbit closures can
be fractal. Eskin--Mirzakhani(--Mohammadi), a
paper of more than 200 pages, a heavy
generalization of Ratner's theorems: every
orbit closure is a submanifold (in fact an
affine invariant submanifold in period
coordinates), and it supports a unique
$\mathrm{SL}_2(\mathbb{R})$-invariant ergodic
probability measure in the Lebesgue class.
(Attribution as I understand the transcript;
the precise papers are Eskin--Mirzakhani,
Publ. Math. IHES 2018, and
Eskin--Mirzakhani--Mohammadi, Ann. of Math.
2015; not checked here.)

The diagonal subgroup
$$
g_t=\begin{pmatrix} e^t & 0 \\
0 & e^{-t}\end{pmatrix}
$$
acts as the \emph{Teichm\"uller geodesic
flow}: it is the geodesic flow of the
Teichm\"uller metric, the ``flat''
counterpart of the Weil--Petersson metric,
which is built from hyperbolic structures.
Spoken joke: Yau once began a talk saying
there are 17 metrics on moduli space and he
computed the curvature of all of them.

\medskip\noindent
{\bf Veech surfaces.} Let $X$ be the
translation surface given by a regular
polygon (for the octagon: glue opposite
sides, which are parallel and of equal
length, by translations). Veech (1989): the
orbit $\mathrm{SL}_2(\mathbb{R})X$ is already
closed, and it is isomorphic to
$\mathrm{SL}_2(\mathbb{R})/\mathrm{SL}(X)$,
where the \emph{Veech group} $\mathrm{SL}(X)$
is the stabilizer of $X$, i.e. the matrices
whose action on $X$ can be undone by cutting
and pasting. For the regular $2n$-gon
(spoken, garbled) $\mathrm{SL}(X)$ is
generated by the parabolic
$$
\begin{pmatrix} 1 & 2\cot(\pi/n) \\
0 & 1\end{pmatrix}
$$
and a rotation; the parabolic cuts and pastes
all horizontal cylinders at the same time.
$\mathrm{SL}(X)$ is a lattice (a triangle
group, with finite covolume), so the orbit
projects to a hyperbolic surface of finite
area inside the moduli space, a
\emph{Teichm\"uller curve} (an algebraic
curve in moduli space). A second example:
square-tiled surfaces, made of finitely many
unit squares; their Veech groups are finite
index subgroups of
$\mathrm{SL}_2(\mathbb{Z})$, so again the
orbit is a finite-volume hyperbolic surface
in moduli space.

\medskip\noindent
{\bf Applications to billiards.} Let
$0<\ell_1\leq\ell_2\leq\dots$ be the lengths
of the closed billiard trajectories (the
length spectrum). Veech introduced this
technology to count them: for the billiard in
the regular $n$-gon, the number $N(T)$ of
periodic trajectories of length at most $T$
satisfies
$$
N(T)\sim c_n\,T^2
$$
as $T\to\infty$, with explicit constants
$c_n$ (the transcript gives something like
$n(n^2-1)/(48\pi)$, with a different formula
for $n=4$; not checked). The constants $c_n$
are \emph{Siegel--Veech constants}. Further
applications mentioned: diffusion rates for
the wind-tree model in statistical mechanics,
where the distance travelled grows like
$t^{2/3}$, not $t^{1/2}$ as for Brownian
motion (Delecroix--Hubert--Leli\`evre, not
checked); and the classification of
commensurability classes of non-arithmetic
lattices in $\mathrm{PU}(2,1)$ of
Deligne--Mostow (not checked).

\medskip\noindent
{\bf The linear Weyl law.} On
$M\setminus\Sigma$ there are commuting vector
fields $\partial_x$, $\partial_y$, which give
Sobolev spaces $H^s(M)$ and the flat
Laplacian $\Delta=\partial_x^2+\partial_y^2$.
Because of the cone points, $\Delta$ on
smooth functions with compact support in
$M\setminus\Sigma$ is not essentially
self-adjoint, which is bad for spectral
theory. One takes the Friedrichs extension,
the self-adjoint operator associated with the
Dirichlet form
$$
Q(u)=\int_M |\nabla u|^2\,dA.
$$
It has discrete spectrum
$0=\lambda_0<\lambda_1\leq\dots$, and the
counting function satisfies Weyl's law
$N(T)\sim\mathrm{Area}(X)\,T/(4\pi)$ (spoken:
proved in 1997; author not identified in the
transcript; constant from the usual Weyl law,
not checked). Sketch: localize by domain
monotonicity of eigenvalues; away from
$\Sigma$ the metric is flat and the usual
Weyl law applies; near a cone point of angle
$2\pi(m+1)$ write $\Delta$ in polar
coordinates and separate variables: the
eigenfunctions are Bessel functions in the
radius times exponentials in the angle, and
asymptotics of zeros of Bessel functions
conclude.

\medskip\noindent
{\bf Nonlinear Weyl law and widths.} The
question of the talk for differential
geometers (addressed to Lucas in the
audience). Min-max widths $\omega_p(X)$:
sweep the surface out by $p$-parameter
families of curves, take the maximal length
in a family and minimize over families. For
smooth surfaces, the widths are realized by
closed geodesics or geodesic nets. Spoken:
for a smooth convex domain in $\mathbb{R}^2$
it was conjectured that each $\omega_p$ is
realized by a finite union of billiard
trajectories, and this was proved for convex
polygons (authors garbled in the transcript;
not identified). Values computed for an
equilateral triangle $T$ and a square $S$,
both inscribed in the unit circle; the
transcript gives approximately
$\omega_1(T)=\omega_2(T)=3/2$,
$\omega_3(T)=3\sqrt{3}/2$, $\omega_4(T)=3$,
and $\omega_3(S)=2\sqrt{2}$ (garbled, not
checked). These two polygons unfold to the
hexagonal torus and the square torus, two
very special points of moduli space.
Question: can these widths be computed using
Siegel--Veech constants?

\medskip\noindent
{\bf Siegel--Veech transform.} Standard
version of what was said. A \emph{saddle
connection} on $X$ is a straight segment from
a cone point to a cone point with no cone
point in its interior. Its \emph{holonomy} is
$\int_\gamma\omega\in
\mathbb{C}=\mathbb{R}^2$.
Let $V(X)\subset\mathbb{R}^2$ be the set of
holonomies of saddle connections, a discrete
set. For $f$ a reasonable function on
$\mathbb{R}^2$ (continuous with compact
support, or the indicator of a nice compact
set), the \emph{Siegel--Veech transform} is
$$
\widehat f(X)=\sum_{v\in V(X)}f(v),
$$
a finite sum. Let $\mu$ be an
$\mathrm{SL}_2(\mathbb{R})$-invariant
probability measure on a stratum. Then
$f\mapsto\int\widehat f\,d\mu$ is an
$\mathrm{SL}_2(\mathbb{R})$-invariant measure
on $\mathbb{R}^2$, and the only such measures
are combinations of the Dirac mass at the
origin and Lebesgue measure. The Dirac mass
is ruled out, so
$$
\int\widehat f\,d\mu
=c(\mu)\int_{\mathbb{R}^2}f\,dx\,dy
$$
for a constant $c(\mu)$, the
\emph{Siegel--Veech constant} (Veech 1998).
Taking $f$ the indicator of the disc of
radius $\varepsilon$ shows that the measure
of the set of surfaces with a saddle
connection shorter than $\varepsilon$ (the
flat analogue of a small systole) is about
$c(\mu)\pi\varepsilon^2$. Spoken: the method
is very general; it only uses an equivariant
assignment of vectors in $\mathbb{R}^2$ to
points of the space.

\medskip\noindent
{\bf Random hyperbolic surfaces.} On the
moduli space $\mathcal{M}_g$ of hyperbolic
surfaces of genus $g$ there is the
Weil--Petersson probability measure: cut the
surface into pairs of pants, use the lengths
and twists of the cuts (Fenchel--Nielsen
coordinates); Wolpert's formula says the
Weil--Petersson symplectic form is $\sum_i
d\ell_i\wedge d\tau_i$. One asks what a
random surface looks like as $g\to\infty$:
results of the form ``for every
$\varepsilon>0$, with probability tending to
$1$'' about the diameter (close to the area
lower bound, about $2\log g$, Mirzakhani),
the separating systole (about $2\log g$,
Mirzakhani, refined by Nie--Wu--Xue), the
Cheeger constant, and the first eigenvalue
$\lambda_1$ (recently shown to be at least
$1/4-\varepsilon$). The precise constants and
attributions in the transcript are garbled;
not checked.

\medskip\noindent
{\bf Random flat spheres.} A sphere has no
nonzero holomorphic $1$-form, so one uses
quadratic differentials, locally
$\phi(z)\,dz^2$: \emph{half-translation
surfaces}, glued by $z\mapsto\pm z+c$.
Consider those with a single zero of high
order and the remaining singularities simple
poles. With the Masur--Veech measure (from
period coordinates), as the number of poles
tends to infinity, results announced by Kasra
Rafi and Anton Zorich: the expected height of
the tallest cylinder is $\sqrt{2}/2$, of the
second tallest $\sqrt{2}/4$, the expected
diameter $3\sqrt{2}/4$, and the systole times
$n$ tends to $\pi/\sqrt{2}$, with the area
normalized (numbers from the transcript, not
checked). A \emph{cylinder} is a maximal
family of parallel closed geodesics avoiding
the cone points; its height is the distance
between its two boundaries.

\medskip\noindent
{\bf Open questions at the end.} What is the
geometry of a typical translation surface in
higher genus? Is there a connection between
Siegel--Veech constants and the nonlinear
Weyl law for billiards? Quantum unique
ergodicity: do the measures $|u_\lambda|^2dA$
of eigenfunctions converge to Lebesgue
measure, or can they concentrate? Partial
results say eigenfunctions cannot concentrate
away from the cone points.

\medskip\noindent
{\bf Questions from the audience.} Other
subgroups: for the horocycle flow (the upper
unipotent subgroup) orbit closures can be
fractal, not submanifolds, but the invariant
measures known so far are nice. Height of a
cylinder: the distance between its boundary
components, with the area of the surface
normalized.

{\bf Daniel's notes, with answers.} The
answers are mine (Claude), not from the talk,
and not checked by anyone else.

``Why are they called \emph{translation}
surfaces? The initial picture looks more like
gluing triangles with dihedral groups to
construct Riemann surfaces.'' The reflections
(the dihedral group) appear only in the
unfolding. One takes all reflected copies of
the table, but two copies that differ by a
translation are glued as the same copy. The
resulting polygon has its sides glued in
pairs by translations, and in the charts
$z=\int\omega$ every change of chart is a
translation $z\mapsto z+c$. That is the name:
an atlas whose changes of chart are
translations. Reflections are not allowed in
the gluing; allowing $z\mapsto -z+c$ gives
half-translation surfaces.

``I don't understand the `deforming some
shapes gives the same surface' argument.'' A
translation surface is the glued object, not
the polygon. If you cut a polygon along a
segment and glue the two pieces back along a
pair of sides that are identified anyway, the
glued surface does not change. Example: the
parallelogram $0,1,2+i,1+i$ is the image of
the unit square under
$\left(\begin{smallmatrix}1&1\\
0&1\end{smallmatrix}\right)$.
Cut off the triangle with vertices $1$,
$2+i$, $1+i$ and translate it by $-1$: it
fills the missing triangle and gives back the
unit square with the same gluings. So that
matrix fixes the square torus: it lies in its
Veech group.

``Suppose $X=(M,\omega)$ is generated by a
regular polygon. But what does that mean?
Where are the translations in this?'' Take
the regular octagon. Its opposite sides are
parallel and have the same length, so each
pair can be glued by the translation taking
one side to the other. This gives
$(M,\omega)$ with $\omega=dz$: genus $2$, one
cone point of angle $6\pi$, i.e. a point of
$\mathcal{H}(2)$. The translations are these
four side gluings.

``The talk starts with billiards [\ldots] But
what happened to the billiards?'' Unfolding
turns the billiard in a rational polygon into
the straight-line flow on a translation
surface. The point of the moduli space and
the $\mathrm{SL}_2(\mathbb{R})$ action is
renormalization: to understand straight lines
on one surface, one moves it by $g_t$ and
studies its orbit. For Veech surfaces this
gives Veech's counting $N(T)\sim c\,T^2$ of
periodic billiard trajectories, and in
general the Siegel--Veech constants and the
wind-tree diffusion exponent. Billiards come
back at the end through the widths $\omega_p$
of convex polygons, realized by billiard
trajectories.

while before the end of the talk.'' The last
part (from about minute 33) changed subject
twice: spectral theory of the flat Laplacian
and min-max widths, then random surfaces. It
is a list of results and questions, mostly
without definitions or proofs, so the notes
above only record the statements.
