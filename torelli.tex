\section{Background already in the Heap}
\label{section-background}

A Torelli problem starts with a geometric
object and asks whether some data attached
to it determine its isomorphism class.
The familiar examples in the Heap use
cohomology: we replace a variety by linear
algebra, then ask how much of its geometry
we can recover.

The first bridge is Hodge theory.
On a compact Riemannian manifold, each
de Rham cohomology class has a unique
harmonic representative. This is the
Hodge theorem in {\it Hodge Theory},
Theorem~\ref{hodge-theory-%
theorem-hodge-de-rham}.
For a compact K\"ahler manifold, cohomology
also splits into pieces of type $(p,q)$.
This decomposition carries information
about the complex structure that the
underlying cohomology group alone forgets.
The section on Hodge structures and
polarization in
{\it Variations of Hodge Structures},
Section~\ref{variations-of-hodge-structures-%
section-hodge-structures},
explains how to package these pieces,
together with a lattice and a compatible
pairing, as Hodge-theoretic data.

Now vary the complex structure in a
smooth proper family. Locally on the
base, we can identify the underlying
smooth manifolds and their cohomology.
Inside this fixed cohomology space,
the subspaces of type $(p,q)$ can change.
The period map records their positions.
A Torelli question then asks whether two
varieties with the same period data must
be isomorphic. The precise comparison
depends on the markings and
polarizations being retained.
The section on period domains and period
maps in
{\it Variations of Hodge Structures},
Section~\ref{variations-of-hodge-structures-%
section-period-maps},
sets up this viewpoint.

K3 surfaces give our main familiar
example. We keep $H^2(X,\mathbb{Z})$,
cohomology with coefficients in the
constant sheaf $\mathbb{Z}$, together with
its intersection pairing. This lattice
alone does not distinguish the surfaces.
We also keep the line
$
H^{2,0}(X)=\mathbb{C}\sigma
\subset H^2(X,\mathbb{C}),
$
where $\sigma$ is the holomorphic
symplectic form. It is the line that
matters: multiplying $\sigma$ by a
nonzero scalar does not change it.
As the complex structure varies, picture
this line moving inside the fixed
complexified cohomology space.
For K3 surfaces, the period map records
exactly the position of this line.
This is the period described
in {\it K3 Surfaces},
Definition~\ref{k3-%
definition-period-k3-surface}.

Global Torelli says that this data
recovers the complex surface, even when
the two surfaces are not nearby in a
family. Two complex K3 surfaces are
biholomorphic if and only if there is an
isomorphism of their integral second
cohomology groups preserving both the
intersection pairing and the holomorphic
two-form line. Such a map is called a
Hodge isometry. There is a finer question:
does a particular Hodge isometry come
from a biholomorphism? It does, uniquely,
if it also sends a K\"ahler class to a
K\"ahler class. Thus recovering the
surface and realizing a chosen map on
cohomology are distinct assertions.
See D.~Huybrechts,
{\it Lectures on K3 Surfaces},
Cambridge University Press (2016),
Chapter~7, Section~5
(Theorem~7.5.3 in the author's draft).
The automorphism version is recalled in
{\it Factoring Cremona transformations},
Theorem~\ref{cremona-transformations-%
theorem-global-torelli-k3}.
Local Torelli, by comparison, describes
the period map near a given surface;
see {\it K3 Surfaces},
Section~\ref{k3-section-local-torelli}.

For curves, the natural next object is
the Jacobian, which parametrizes
degree-zero line bundles.
The Abel--Jacobi map sends a point,
relative to a chosen base point, to
its associated degree-zero line bundle.
These constructions are recalled in
{\it Complex Geometry}, in the
definitions of the Jacobian and
Abel--Jacobi map,
Definitions~\ref{complex-geometry-%
definition-jacobian} and
\ref{complex-geometry-%
definition-abel-jacobi}.
They provide the starting point for
asking whether the curve can be recovered
from its Jacobian with its polarization.
The graph--polytope question follows the
same pattern: construct an invariant,
specify which structure it retains,
and ask whether it remembers the
original graph.
