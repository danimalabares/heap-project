\section{Method and point of departure}
\label{section-history-method}

These notes began while walking through
Salvador on September 26, 2026.  The first
question was simple: when does the history
of colonial Brazil begin?

The answer requires three distinctions:

\begin{itemize}
\item Indigenous history did not begin with
European arrival;
\item arrival in 1500 was not instantaneous
conquest of the present Brazilian
territory;
\item the foundation of a city is not the
same as the moment when that city became a
major economic or political centre.
\end{itemize}

The detailed record of museum panels and
photographs is in
\texttt{salvador-fieldnotes.tex}.

\section{Before and after 1500}
\label{section-before-after-1500}

The Atlantic coast later claimed by
Portugal was already inhabited by diverse
Indigenous peoples with their own
territories, languages, political
relations, and histories.

The conventional date 1500 marks the
arrival of Cabral's expedition on the coast
of the present state of Bahia.  It does not
mark complete occupation or control of the
territory later called Brazil.

During the first decades, Portuguese
presence was concentrated mainly on the
coast and on extraction, trade, alliances,
and conflict.  More permanent colonization
intensified in the 1530s.

\section{A first urban chronology}
\label{section-urban-chronology}

A first chronology discussed during the
visit was:

\begin{itemize}
\item S\~ao Vicente was founded in 1532;
\item Olinda was founded in the 1530s;
\item Salvador was founded in 1549;
\item the Jesuit college that became a
nucleus of S\~ao Paulo dates from 1554;
\item Rio de Janeiro was founded in 1565;
\item Bel\'em was founded in 1616.
\end{itemize}

These dates do not describe one uniform
wave.  They belong to different regional
histories of settlement, warfare,
missionary action, trade, and alliance.

Rio de Janeiro, for example, emerged in a
conflict over Guanabara Bay involving
Portuguese and French forces and different
Indigenous alliances.  Indigenous peoples
were political actors, not background
figures in a purely European war.

\section{Why 1549 matters}
\label{section-why-1549-matters}

Salvador was founded in 1549 by Tom\'e de
Sousa as the seat of the Government-General.
The event therefore marks a new effort by
the Portuguese Crown to coordinate a
colonization that had already begun.

Government, military defence, justice,
finance, missionary activity, and urban
construction were brought together in a
planned colonial capital.

Salvador remained the seat of colonial
government until 1763, when the capital was
moved to Rio de Janeiro.  The later shift
must be connected with mining, Atlantic
trade, and the political importance of the
South and Southeast.

\section{The Jesuit city}
\label{section-jesuit-city}

The Cathedral Basilica was originally the
church of the Jesuit college.  This makes
the building evidence of a larger colonial
institution.

The Jesuits arrived with Tom\'e de Sousa in
1549.  Their work joined education,
missionary activity, and the colonial
reorganization of Indigenous life.

The church, school, and government were
not isolated spheres.  Colonial religion
also participated in the production of
knowledge, authority, discipline, and
social order.

\section{Sugar, the Reconcavo, and the port}
\label{section-sugar-reconcavo-port}

The early importance of Salvador cannot be
understood without the Rec\^oncavo and the
sugar economy.

Plantations and mills around the Bay of All
Saints produced sugar for Atlantic markets.
Salvador served as an administrative,
commercial, and port centre connecting the
Rec\^oncavo with Portugal, Africa, and the
wider Atlantic.

This economy depended on coerced labour.
Indigenous people were enslaved as well as
African people.  Indigenous resistance and
African resistance must both remain inside
the history, even though their scales and
chronologies differ.

A later version of these notes needs a
quantitative chronology of the Atlantic
trade to Bahia, including changes in the
African regions of embarkation.

\section{Africa was not one origin}
\label{section-africa-not-one-origin}

The MUNCAB map replaced the vague phrase
``African influence'' with a more precise
geography.

It marked Yoruba regions around present-day
Nigeria and Benin, Akan regions around
Ghana and C\^ote d'Ivoire, peoples around
Cameroon and Gabon, and several Central
African societies in areas now divided
among Congo and Angola.

These regions do not coincide neatly with
modern state borders.  Nor did the people
transported to Bahia share one language,
one religion, or one political identity.

The categories Nag\^o, Jeje, Congo, and
Angola used in Brazil belong to diasporic
history.  They cannot be read simply as a
list of current African nationalities.

\section{Akan and museum geography}
\label{section-akan-geography}

A MUNCAB label identified a decorative
Akan mask as coming from Central Africa.
A map in the same museum placed the Akan
region in Ghana and C\^ote d'Ivoire, in
West Africa.

The contradiction is valuable evidence
about method.  Museum labels must be read
critically and compared with maps,
catalogues, and scholarship.

The direct lesson is not merely that one
label was wrong.  It is that Africa should
not be divided casually into broad regions
without checking the actual people and
place under discussion.

\section{Black Catholic brotherhoods}
\label{section-black-brotherhoods}

The attempted visit to Ros\'ario dos Pretos
introduced another kind of institution.
The church was closed, but its history
still matters.

The brotherhood associated with the church
was erected in 1685, and construction of
the church began in the early eighteenth
century \cite{ipac-rosario}.

Black brotherhoods could organize worship,
festivals, burial, aid, and collective
identity.  They were institutions inside
Catholic society, but they also created
social spaces for enslaved and free Black
people.

This makes them important for the history
of both religion and Black association in
colonial Brazil.

\section{Candomble as diasporic formation}
\label{section-history-candomble}

Candombl\'e is Brazilian, but its formation
cannot be separated from African histories
and the Atlantic trade.

The religion did not emerge by simply
copying one intact African system.  People
from different societies preserved and
reorganized divine beings, ritual
languages, music, food, plants, kinship,
and forms of authority under new and
violent conditions.

By the nineteenth century, Jeje and Nag\^o
religious formations were established in
Salvador.  The later prominence of Nag\^o
traditions was itself the result of
historical competition, alliances, and
self-definition \cite{pares2010nago}.

\section{Bahia and the Gulf of Benin}
\label{section-bahia-gulf-benin}

The Casa do Benin exists to make the
Bahia--Benin relation visible.  It is not
simply a museum containing objects from a
generic Africa.

The modern Republic of Benin is a narrow
West African country between Togo and
Nigeria.  Its map made K\'etou visible as a
place, not only as a Brazilian ritual
name.

The region historically called the Gulf or
Bight of Benin was central to several
phases of the Atlantic trade and to the
histories of Yoruba- and Fon-speaking
peoples.

\section{Flux and reflux}
\label{section-flux-reflux}

Pierre Verger's phrase ``flux and reflux''
gives a stronger model than one-way
``influence'' \cite{verger2021fluxo}.

The first movement includes forced
transport from Africa to Bahia.  Later
movements include return migration,
commerce, family relations, religious
journeys, scholarship, artistic exchange,
and institutions such as the Casa do
Benin and the corresponding Casa do Brasil
in Ouidah.

The guiding questions are:

\begin{itemize}
\item what travelled from Africa to Bahia;
\item what was transformed in Bahia;
\item what later travelled from Brazil to
Africa;
\item how both shores changed through the
relation.
\end{itemize}

\section{Benin, Nigeria, and modern borders}
\label{section-benin-nigeria-borders}

Modern Benin and Nigeria are colonial and
postcolonial states.  Yoruba, Fon, and
other historical regions cross or predate
their borders.

French is the official language of Benin
because of colonial history, but it is not
the only language of the country.  The
museum map and the Candombl\'e material
show why modern national language cannot
stand in for the region's linguistic
history.

\section{Zumbi and Palmares}
\label{section-zumbi-palmares}

The monument photographed near the
Cathedral represented Zumbi dos Palmares.
Zumbi was a historical leader associated
with the seventeenth-century Quilombo dos
Palmares and became a major symbol of Black
resistance to slavery.

The monument prompted a confusion with
Nzambi or Nzambi a Mpungu, a supreme
creator name in Kongo and other Central
African religious contexts.

Zumbi and Nzambi are different referents.
A historical or etymological relation must
not be inferred only from the similarity
of the words.

\section{The 1983 manifesto as history}
\label{section-history-manifesto}

The 1983 manifesto by five Bahian
ialorix\'as is not only a religious text.
It is evidence for the political and
intellectual history of late twentieth-
century Brazil.

The authors rejected colonial categories
that presented Candombl\'e as primitive,
demanded religious autonomy, and criticized
folklorization and tourist consumption.

Their position shows that traditions do
not merely survive history.  Practitioners
actively interpret slavery, syncretism,
racism, and public culture and argue about
how the past should shape the present.

\section{Mae Stella in modern history}
\label{section-history-mae-stella}

M\~ae Stella de Ox\'ossi linked several
historical fields that should not be kept
separate:

\begin{itemize}
\item the internal history of Op\^o Afonj\'a;
\item public health and professional life;
\item the international orix\'a world;
\item Nigeria and Benin;
\item anti-syncretism and religious
freedom;
\item literature and intellectual life;
\item schools, museums, libraries, and
heritage policy.
\end{itemize}

Her biography therefore belongs not only
to a history of religion but also to the
social and intellectual history of modern
Bahia.

\section{Carnival and the Black movement}
\label{section-carnival-black-movement}

The books encountered at MUNCAB point to a
modern history that continues beyond the
terreiro.

The work of Alberto Pitta and J. Cunha
connects textiles, graphic language,
blocos afro, carnival, and public identity.
Zulu Ara\'ujo's autobiography offers a
first-person route through Black political
and cultural activism, Olodum, public
institutions, and citizenship.

These are not secondary cultural ornaments
to an already completed national history.
They are ways in which modern Black
institutions made Salvador and Brazil.

\section{Bembe and the Reconcavo}
\label{section-history-bembe}

The Bemb\'e do Mercado, celebrated in Santo
Amaro, connects Candombl\'e, public space,
memories of abolition, and the collective
organization of terreiros in the
Rec\^oncavo \cite{ufrb2026bembe}.

It offers a future field trip beyond the
historic centre of Salvador.  It also
prevents the history of Afro-Brazilian
religion from being reduced to a few famous
houses in the capital.

\section{Claims still to verify}
\label{section-history-open-claims}

Several statements encountered during the
visit remain research tasks:

\begin{itemize}
\item the slogan that Bahia is the most
Black state outside Africa requires a
defined population, a statistical measure,
and a comparison set;
\item the chronology and scale of enslaved
arrivals from different African regions
need quantitative sources;
\item the relation among Indigenous
enslavement, African enslavement, and the
sugar economy needs a more precise
chronology;
\item the changing importance of Salvador,
Rio de Janeiro, S\~ao Paulo, Pernambuco,
Amazonia, and the South must be studied
region by region;
\item the distinction between modern
African states and older kingdoms,
languages, and cultural regions needs a
proper historical map.
\end{itemize}
