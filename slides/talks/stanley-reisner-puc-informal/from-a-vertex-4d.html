What this is

A regular 4-polytope sits in 4-space with its vertices on a round 3-sphere. Its boundary is a 3-sphere made of cells. All vertices look the same, so one vertex v is enough.

The other examples are boundaries of convex 4-polytopes that are not regular, all made of tetrahedra. A double pyramid adds two new vertices, one on each side. The cyclic polytopes have their vertices on the curve (cos t, sin t, cos 2t, sin 2t); every two vertices are joined by an edge, so the link of v has all the other vertices. The one with 8 vertices has the same numbers as M (8 vertices, 28 edges, 20 tetrahedra), but M is not the boundary of a convex polytope; neither is Barnette's sphere. The 6-vertex one is the double pyramid over a tetrahedron; the 7-vertex one is the double pyramid over a triangular double pyramid. In these the vertices are of different kinds: use the arrows.

Round picture. Push the boundary out onto the round 3-sphere and project stereographically from v. Then v is at infinity, the neighbours of v (the link) lie on a round 2-sphere, edges are arcs of circles, and the edges through v are straight rays going out. The cells that do not touch v fill the inside; the cells that touch v are the outside.

Straight picture. Look at the polytope from a point just outside it, near v, and draw only the far side. Everything is flat, the link of v is a convex polyhedron, and the cells that do not touch v tile it. This picture exists because the polytope is convex. The edges going to v are drawn as straight rays pointing away from the centre: with them, the cells that touch v are the regions outside, between the rays.

The slider adds the cells inside starting from the ones nearest to v. The cells at v are drawn paler, each as the region outside one triangle of the link, between its three rays. They go on to infinity; they are cut where the rays are cut.
