\section{Electrostatics}
What would a small charge do if we placed it
here? The electric field $\mathbf E$ answers
this question. A stationary positive test
charge $q$ feels the force $q\mathbf E$. An
electron has charge $-e$ and accelerates in
the opposite direction.

Outside a stationary point charge $Q$ in
vacuum the field at distance $r$ is
$$
\mathbf E=
\frac{Q}{4\pi\varepsilon_0 r^2}\hat r,
$$
where $\hat r$ points away from the charge.
Doubling the distance divides the force on
the same test charge by four.

\section{Voltage across two plates}
Give two large parallel conducting plates
opposite charges. Away from their edges the
field in the gap is nearly uniform and points
toward the negative plate. For plate
separation $d$ its magnitude is approximately
$$
E=\frac{|V_1-V_2|}{d}.
$$
One volt means one joule per coulomb. An
electron moving freely from the lower
potential to a potential one volt higher
gains one electronvolt of kinetic energy.

\section{Electrodynamics}
A moving charge also feels a magnetic force.
For velocity $\mathbf v$ the full force is
$$
\mathbf F=q(\mathbf E+
\mathbf v\times\mathbf B).
$$
The magnetic term is perpendicular to the
velocity, so it can bend a trajectory while
preserving speed.

Changing magnetic fields can also produce
electric fields. Hold a wire loop still and
push a bar magnet toward it. The changing
magnetic flux through the loop induces an
electromotive force. Pull the magnet away and
the induced current reverses. This is the
induction studied in electrodynamics.

See \cite[Vol. II, Sections 1-1 to 1-4 and
17-1]{feynman-lectures}.
