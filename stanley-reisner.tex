\section{Stanley-Reisner schemes}
\label{section-sr}

\noindent
Here I follow \cite{luk1} for the basic definitions; these
are consistent with other sources.

Let $[n]:=\{0,\ldots,n\}$ be the set of all
positive integers from 0 to $n$, and let
$\Delta_n$ denote the power set of $[n]$. A
subset $\mathcal{K} \subseteq \Delta_n$ is
called a {\it simplicial complex} if for
every $f \in \mathcal{K}$ all the subsets of
$f$ are also in $\mathcal{K}$. Elements of
$\mathcal{K}$ are called {\it faces}.

Let $S=k[x_0,\ldots,x_n]$ be the polynomial ring in $n+1$ variables
over an algebraically closed field $k$.
If $a=\{i_1,\ldots,i_k\}\in \Delta_n$ we write $x_a \in S$ 
for the square-free monomial $x_{i_1}\cdot\ldots\cdot x_{i_k}$.
The {\it Stanley-Reisner} ideal
of a simplicial complex $\mathcal{K}$ is
$$
I_{\mathcal{K}}=\langle x_p : p \in \Delta_n\setminus \mathcal{K} \rangle
\subseteq S.
$$
The {\it Stanley-Reisner ring} of $\mathcal{K}$ is
$A_{\mathcal{K}}=S/I_{\mathcal{K}}$
and the {\it Stanley-Reisner scheme} is $\text{Proj} A_{\mathcal{K}}$.

\section{The dualizing sheaf of a sphere}
\label{section-sr-dualizing-sheaf}

The goal of this section is to explain
why the Stanley-Reisner scheme of a
triangulated sphere has trivial dualizing
sheaf, and why this passes to a
smoothing. The dualizing sheaf is
recalled in Algebraic Geometry, Section
\ref{algebraic-geometry-%
section-dualizing-sheaf}; the
Gorenstein property is recalled in
Commutative Algebra, Section
\ref{commutative-algebra-%
section-gorenstein-properties}. The
general theorems below are quoted from
\cite{stanley-cca}, \cite{BAYER19911} and
\cite{har}, with the place in each source.
The example of the tetrahedron is worked
out by hand.

\medskip\noindent
{\bf Links.}
Let $\mathcal{K}$ be a simplicial complex
and $\sigma$ a face. The link of $\sigma$
is the set of faces $\tau$ of
$\mathcal{K}$ such that
$$
\tau\cap\sigma=\emptyset,
\qquad
\tau\cup\sigma\in\mathcal{K}.
$$
It is again a simplicial complex,
written $\operatorname{lk}\sigma$. The
link of the empty face is $\mathcal{K}$
itself.

For example, let $\mathcal{K}$ be the
boundary of the tetrahedron: all proper
subsets of $\{0,1,2,3\}$. It is a
triangulation of the $2$-sphere.
\begin{enumerate}
\item The link of the vertex $\{0\}$
consists of the proper subsets of
$\{1,2,3\}$. It is the boundary of a
triangle: a circle.
\item The link of the edge $\{0,1\}$
consists of $\emptyset$, $\{2\}$ and
$\{3\}$. It is two points: a sphere of
dimension $0$.
\item The link of the triangle
$\{0,1,2\}$ consists of $\emptyset$
alone.
\end{enumerate}
The complex whose only face is the empty
set is counted as the sphere of
dimension $-1$. Its reduced homology is
$k$ in degree $-1$ and zero in all other
degrees.

\medskip\noindent
{\bf Links in a sphere.}
Suppose that $\mathcal{K}$ is a
triangulation of a sphere of dimension
$d$. Let $\sigma$ be a face with
$|\sigma|$ vertices. Then the link of
$\sigma$ has the reduced homology of a
sphere of dimension $d-|\sigma|$:
$$
\widetilde{H}_i
(\operatorname{lk}\sigma;k)\cong
\begin{cases}
k,& i=d-|\sigma|,\\
0,& i\neq d-|\sigma|.
\end{cases}
$$
The reason is that the homology of the link
of a face computes the local homology of the
sphere at a point of that face. In the
tetrahedron example it is visible in the list
above, with $d=2$. In general it follows from
two results quoted below: a triangulation of
a sphere is Gorenstein, and the displayed
condition characterizes the Gorenstein
complexes among those in which no vertex
belongs to all maximal faces \cite[Chapter
II, Theorem 5.1 and Corollary
5.2]{stanley-cca}. A triangulated sphere has
no such vertex: a complex with such a vertex
is a cone, hence contractible.

\medskip\noindent
{\bf Two theorems.}
The first theorem is Reisner's criterion
\cite{reisner-cm}, in the form of
\cite[Chapter II, Corollary
4.2]{stanley-cca}.

\begin{theorem}
\label{theorem-reisner-criterion}
The ring $A_{\mathcal{K}}$ is
Cohen--Macaulay if and only if, for
every face $\sigma$ of $\mathcal{K}$,
including the empty face, the reduced
homology of $\operatorname{lk}\sigma$
with coefficients in $k$ vanishes in all
degrees below the dimension of
$\operatorname{lk}\sigma$.
\end{theorem}

The second is due to Stanley and Hochster.

\begin{theorem}
\label{theorem-stanley-gorenstein-sphere}
Let $\mathcal{K}$ be a triangulation of a
sphere. Then $A_{\mathcal{K}}$ is Gorenstein,
and its canonical module satisfies
$$
\omega_{A_{\mathcal{K}}}\cong
A_{\mathcal{K}}
$$
as graded modules, with no shift of the
grading.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
The ring is Gorenstein by \cite[Chapter II,
Corollary 5.2]{stanley-cca}. The canonical
module of a Gorenstein graded ring is the
ring itself up to a shift of the grading
\cite[Chapter I, Theorem 12.5]{stanley-cca};
for face rings of orientable manifolds
without boundary this is restated in
\cite[Chapter II, Section 7]{stanley-cca}. It
remains to see that the shift is zero. This
is done below, in the paragraph on the shift.
\end{proof}

In the language of Commutative Algebra,
Theorem
\ref{commutative-algebra-%
theorem-graded-gorenstein},
the $a$-invariant is $0$.

\begin{corollary}
\label{corollary-sr-sphere-trivial-dualizing}
Let $\mathcal{K}$ be a triangulation of
a sphere of dimension $d\geq 1$, and let
$X$ be its Stanley-Reisner scheme. Then
$X$ is a Gorenstein projective scheme of
dimension $d$, and
$$
\omega_X\cong\mathcal{O}_X.
$$
\end{corollary}

\begin{proof}
By Theorem
\ref{theorem-stanley-gorenstein-sphere},
the ring $A_{\mathcal{K}}$ is Gorenstein
and its canonical module is
$A_{\mathcal{K}}$ with no shift. The
local rings of $X$ are obtained from
localizations of $A_{\mathcal{K}}$, and
localizations of a Gorenstein ring are
Gorenstein. So $X$ is Gorenstein. The
sheaf on $X$ associated with the
canonical module is the dualizing sheaf;
see the comparison with the canonical
module in Algebraic Geometry, Section
\ref{algebraic-geometry-%
section-dualizing-sheaf}. The sheaf
associated with $A_{\mathcal{K}}$ is
$\mathcal{O}_X$.
\end{proof}

The same conclusion holds more generally. If
$\mathcal{K}$ is a triangulation of a compact
manifold, then the canonical bundle of its
Stanley-Reisner scheme is trivial if and only
if the manifold is orientable \cite[Theorem
6.1]{BAYER19911}.

\medskip\noindent
{\bf The tetrahedron by hand.}
Let $\mathcal{K}$ be the boundary of the
tetrahedron. The only subset of
$\{0,1,2,3\}$ that is not a face is the
whole set. So
$$
I_{\mathcal{K}}=(x_0x_1x_2x_3),
\qquad
A_{\mathcal{K}}=
k[x_0,x_1,x_2,x_3]/(x_0x_1x_2x_3).
$$
The scheme $X$ is the union of the four
coordinate planes of $\mathbb{P}^3$. Put
$S=k[x_0,x_1,x_2,x_3]$ and
$g=x_0x_1x_2x_3$. A graded free
resolution of $A_{\mathcal{K}}$ is
$$
0\to S(-4)\xrightarrow{g}S\to
A_{\mathcal{K}}\to 0.
$$
The codimension is $1$, so the canonical
module is
$$
\omega_{A_{\mathcal{K}}}=
\operatorname{Ext}^1_S
(A_{\mathcal{K}},S(-4)).
$$
To compute it, apply
$\operatorname{Hom}_S(-,S(-4))$ to the
two free modules of the resolution. From
$S$ one gets $S(-4)$. From $S(-4)$ one
gets $S$. The resulting complex is
$$
S(-4)\xrightarrow{g}S,
$$
and $\operatorname{Ext}^1$ is the
cokernel of this map:
$$
\omega_{A_{\mathcal{K}}}\cong
S/(g)=A_{\mathcal{K}}.
$$
There is no shift. This agrees with
Theorem
\ref{theorem-stanley-gorenstein-sphere},
and with Algebraic Geometry, Corollary
\ref{algebraic-geometry-%
corollary-dualizing-hypersurface},
which gives
$\omega_X\cong\mathcal{O}_X(4-3-1)$.

\medskip\noindent
{\bf Where the sphere enters, and the shift.}
Here is why the canonical module is generated
in degree $0$. Let $n$ be the number of
vertices, so that $S$ has $n$ variables, and
let $d$ be the dimension of $\mathcal{K}$.
The ring $A_{\mathcal{K}}$ has dimension
$d+1$, so its codimension is $c=n-d-1$, and a
minimal graded free resolution of
$A_{\mathcal{K}}$ over $S$ has length $c$.
Hochster's formula \cite[Chapter II, Theorem
4.8]{stanley-cca} describes the generators of
its $i$-th module: those whose degree is the
product of the variables in a set $W$ of
vertices are counted by the dimension of
$$
\widetilde{H}_{|W|-i-1}
(\mathcal{K}_W;k).
$$
Here $\mathcal{K}_W$ is the complex of faces
of $\mathcal{K}$ contained in $W$. Take $i=c$
and let $W$ be the set of all vertices. Then
$\mathcal{K}_W=\mathcal{K}$ and the homology
group is
$$
\widetilde{H}_{d}(\mathcal{K};k)=k,
$$
spanned by the fundamental class of the
sphere. So the last module of the resolution
has a generator of degree $n$. Since the ring
is Gorenstein, the last module has rank one:
it is $S(-n)$. The canonical module is
computed by applying
$\operatorname{Hom}_S(-,S(-n))$ to the
resolution, as in the example of the
tetrahedron. The last module $S(-n)$ becomes
$S$, and the canonical module is a quotient
of it. So the canonical module is generated
in degree $0$, by the class dual to the
fundamental class, and
$$
\omega_{A_{\mathcal{K}}}\cong A_{\mathcal{K}}
$$
with no shift. This is the argument sketched
in the proof of \cite[Theorem
6.1]{BAYER19911}. In the tetrahedron, $n=4$
and the last module is $S(-4)$.

There is a second way to see that the shift
is zero. For a Gorenstein graded ring the
shift is the degree of the Hilbert series as
a rational function \cite[Chapter I, Theorem
12.6 and the remark after it]{stanley-cca}.
The monomials of $A_{\mathcal{K}}$ whose
variables are exactly the vertices of a face
$\sigma$ contribute
$$
\left(\frac{t}{1-t}\right)^{|\sigma|}
$$
to the Hilbert series, so the series is the
sum of these terms over all faces, the empty
face included. As $t$ goes to infinity each
term tends to $(-1)^{|\sigma|}$. The sum of
these signs over all faces is minus the
reduced Euler characteristic of
$\mathcal{K}$, which for a sphere of
dimension $d$ is $(-1)^{d+1}$. This is not
zero, so the Hilbert series has degree $0$.

\medskip\noindent
{\bf Smoothings.}
Let $\mathcal{K}$ be a triangulation of a
compact manifold, with Stanley-Reisner scheme
$X$. The cohomology of the structure sheaf of
$X$ is the cohomology of $\mathcal{K}$: for
$i>0$,
$$
H^i(X,\mathcal{O}_X)\cong
H^i(\mathcal{K};k).
$$
This is stated in \cite[Section
6]{BAYER19911}, where it is attributed to
Reisner and to Hochster and Roberts. For a
sphere of dimension $d$ the right hand side
is $k$ for $i=d$ and zero for $0<i<d$. Also
$H^0(X,\mathcal{O}_X)=k$, because
$A_{\mathcal{K}}$ is Cohen--Macaulay of
dimension at least $2$ and its part of degree
$0$ is $k$.

The following theorem is \cite[Theorem
0.2.1]{Fausk} for $d=3$. I have not read the
proof there; the proof below follows the two
steps I noted from it: the dualizing sheaf of
$X$ is trivial, and this passes to nearby
fibres.

\begin{theorem}
\label{theorem-fausk-021}
Let $\mathcal{K}$ be a triangulation of a
sphere of dimension $d\geq 2$, with
Stanley-Reisner scheme $X$. Let $C$ be a
smooth curve with a point $0$, and let
$$
f:\mathcal{X}\to C
$$
be a flat projective morphism whose fibre
over $0$ is $X$. Then there is an open
neighbourhood $V$ of $0$ in $C$ such that
every smooth fibre $Y$ over a point of $V$ is
connected and satisfies
$$
\omega_Y\cong\mathcal{O}_Y,
\qquad
H^i(Y,\mathcal{O}_Y)=0
$$
for $0<i<d$. For $d=2$ such a fibre is a K3
surface. For $d=3$ it is a Calabi--Yau
threefold.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
The proof has four steps.

Step 1: the special fibre. By Corollary
\ref{corollary-sr-sphere-trivial-dualizing},
the scheme $X$ is Gorenstein and
$\omega_X\cong\mathcal{O}_X$. This is where
the sphere is used.

Step 2: vanishing on the special fibre. By
the formula above, $H^i(X,\mathcal{O}_X)=0$
for $0<i<d$. In particular
$H^1(X,\mathcal{O}_X)=0$, because $d\geq 2$.

Step 3: the dualizing sheaf of nearby fibres.
By Steps 1 and 2 the hypotheses of Algebraic
Geometry, Proposition
\ref{algebraic-geometry-%
proposition-trivial-dualizing-nearby}
hold. So there is a neighbourhood $V$ of $0$
such that every fibre $Y$ over a point of $V$
has $\omega_Y\cong\mathcal{O}_Y$. The proof
of that proposition extends a nowhere
vanishing section of $\omega_X$ to the nearby
fibres; the extension exists because
$H^1(X,\omega_X)=0$. If $Y$ is smooth,
$\omega_Y$ is its canonical bundle.

Step 4: semicontinuity. The dimension of
$H^i$ of the structure sheaf of the fibre is
an upper semicontinuous function on $C$
\cite[III, Theorem 12.8]{har}. After making
$V$ smaller, for every fibre $Y$ over a point
of $V$ the dimension of
$H^i(Y,\mathcal{O}_Y)$ is at most that of
$H^i(X,\mathcal{O}_X)$. For $0<i<d$ this
gives zero. For $i=0$ it gives that
$H^0(Y,\mathcal{O}_Y)$ has dimension at most
$1$, so $Y$ is connected.
\end{proof}

The proposition used in Step 3 rests on one
fact about the relative dualizing sheaf of a
family that I have not found in a reference
in the form needed; see the discussion before
Algebraic Geometry, Proposition
\ref{algebraic-geometry-%
proposition-trivial-dualizing-nearby}.

For $d=1$ the hypothesis on $H^1$ fails.
The boundary of a triangle gives three
lines in the plane, and
$H^1(X,\mathcal{O}_X)=k$. The conclusion
still holds there, for a different
reason: a plane cubic has trivial
dualizing sheaf because of its degree,
by Algebraic Geometry, Corollary
\ref{algebraic-geometry-%
corollary-dualizing-hypersurface}.

\section{The Grünbaum-Sreedharan sphere}
\label{section-grunbaum-sphere}

\noindent
Let $\mathcal{M}$ be the triangulation of the
sphere of dimension $3$ with $8$ vertices and
$20$ tetrahedra found by Grünbaum and
Sreedharan \cite{gru}. It is not the boundary
of a convex polytope; see Polytopes, Section
\ref{polytopes-section-grunbaum-sreedharan-sphere}.
Its Stanley-Reisner ideal is generated by the
sixteen cubic monomials
$$
\begin{aligned}
x_6x_7x_8,
x_4x_6x_8, x_3x_7x_8, x_3x_5x_7, x_3x_4x_8,
x_2x_7x_8, \\ 
x_2x_5x_7, x_2x_5x_6,
x_2x_4x_7, x_2x_4x_6, x_1x_4x_6,\\ 
x_1x_4x_5,
x_1x_3x_8, x_1x_3x_6, x_1x_3x_5, x_1x_2x_5
\end{aligned}
$$
as computed in gfan and in GAP, and as shown
in \cite{luk1}. So its Stanley-Reisner scheme
is a union of $20$ copies of $\mathbb{P}^3$
in $\mathbb{P}^7$, one for each tetrahedron.
By Theorem \ref{theorem-fausk-021}, a smooth
fibre near it in a flat projective family is
a Calabi--Yau threefold.
