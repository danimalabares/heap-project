\section{Stanley-Reisner schemes}
\label{section-sr}

\noindent
Here I follow \cite{luk1} for the basic definitions; these
are consistent with other sources.

Let $[n]:=\{0,\ldots,n\}$ be the set of all
positive integers from 0 to $n$, and let
$\Delta_n$ denote the power set of $[n]$. A
subset $\mathcal{K} \subseteq \Delta_n$ is
called a {\it simplicial complex} if for
every $f \in \mathcal{K}$ all the subsets of
$f$ are also in $\mathcal{K}$. Elements of
$\mathcal{K}$ are called {\it faces}.

Let $S=k[x_0,\ldots,x_n]$ be the polynomial ring in $n+1$ variables
over an algebraically closed field $k$.
If $a=\{i_1,\ldots,i_k\}\in \Delta_n$ we write $x_a \in S$ 
for the square-free monomial $x_{i_1}\cdot\ldots\cdot x_{i_k}$.
The {\it Stanley-Reisner} ideal
of a simplicial complex $\mathcal{K}$ is
$$
I_{\mathcal{K}}=\langle x_p : p \in \Delta_n\setminus \mathcal{K} \rangle
\subseteq S.
$$
The {\it Stanley-Reisner ring} of $\mathcal{K}$ is
$A_{\mathcal{K}}=S/I_{\mathcal{K}}$
and the {\it Stanley-Reisner scheme} is $\text{Proj} A_{\mathcal{K}}$.

\section{Torus action and AC multidegrees}
\label{section-torus-action-ac-multidegrees}

\noindent
Our goal is to understand the multidegree
pieces of $T^2$ used by
Altmann and Christophersen (AC) in
\emph{Cotangent cohomology of
Stanley--Reisner rings} \cite{jan3}.
They compute $T^1$ and $T^2$ from the
simplicial complex. Before studying their
combinatorial formulas, we explain what
the pieces being computed mean:
they are weight spaces for the torus
that independently rescales the variables.
Thus the multidegree decomposition has a
representation-theoretic interpretation.
We will build this interpretation in
three steps.

\begin{enumerate}
\item The torus acts on the Stanley--Reisner
algebra by independently rescaling
the variables.
\item This action induces a linear
representation on $T^2$.
\item This representation decomposes into
weight spaces, which are precisely the
multidegree pieces.
\end{enumerate}

\noindent
We now explain the first item.
Put
$$
S=\mathbb{C}[x_1,\ldots,x_n],
\qquad T=(\mathbb{C}^{\times})^n.
$$
Independent rescaling means choosing
one nonzero scalar $t_i$ for each variable.
For $t=(t_1,\ldots,t_n)\in T$, define
the algebra automorphism
$$
\rho_t:S\longrightarrow S,
\qquad x_i\longmapsto t_i x_i.
$$
Its inverse is $\rho_{t^{-1}}$.
These automorphisms give a torus action:
$$
\rho_t\circ\rho_u=\rho_{tu},
\qquad \rho_{(1,\ldots,1)}=\operatorname{id}.
$$

\begin{proposition}
\label{proposition-monomial-torus-action}
For every monomial ideal $I\subset S$,
independent rescaling descends to a
torus action on the quotient algebra $S/I$.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
Let $m=x_1^{a_1}\cdots x_n^{a_n}$ be
a monomial generator of $I$. Then
$$
\rho_t(m)=
(t_1^{a_1}\cdots t_n^{a_n})m.
$$
Thus $\rho_t(m)\in I$.
Every $f\in I$ is a finite sum
$$
f=\sum_j h_jm_j,
\qquad h_j\in S,
$$
where the $m_j$ are monomial generators.
Consequently,
$$
\rho_t(f)=
\sum_j\rho_t(h_j)\rho_t(m_j)\in I.
$$
This proves $\rho_t(I)\subseteq I$.
Applying the same argument to $t^{-1}$
gives the reverse inclusion, so
$$
\rho_t(I)=I.
$$

We can therefore define
$$
\overline{\rho}_t:S/I\longrightarrow S/I,
\qquad [f]\longmapsto[\rho_t(f)].
$$
This is well-defined: if $[f]=[g]$, then
$f-g\in I$, hence
$\rho_t(f)-\rho_t(g)\in I$.
It is an algebra automorphism with inverse
$\overline{\rho}_{t^{-1}}$.
The action identities on $S$ pass to
the quotient:
$$
\overline{\rho}_t\circ\overline{\rho}_u
=\overline{\rho}_{tu},
\qquad
\overline{\rho}_{(1,\ldots,1)}
=\operatorname{id}.
$$
\end{proof}

\noindent
A Stanley--Reisner ideal is monomial,
so the proposition applies to our algebra.


\medskip\noindent
For now we accept items 2 and 3:
the action on $R=S/I$ induces an
algebraic torus representation on $T^2$,
and its weight spaces are the AC
multidegree pieces. We postpone the
construction through the cotangent
complex.

The general torus decomposition is stated
in Theorem~\ref
{representation-theory-theorem-torus-weights}
of \emph{Representation Theory}.
That section also explains its relation
to Lie algebra weight spaces.
This is standard representation theory;
AC's contribution is the combinatorial
description of the individual cotangent
cohomology pieces, not the existence of
the weight decomposition.

Write $t\cdot v$ for the induced action
on $T^2$. What does a weight describe?
First keep two different objects apart.
An element
$$
t=(t_1,\ldots,t_n)\in T
$$
chooses the rescaling: multiply $x_i$
by $t_i$. A weight will instead record
how a vector responds to all these
choices.

Start with a familiar example in the
polynomial ring:
$$
\rho_t(x_1^2x_2)=t_1^2t_2x_1^2x_2.
$$
Every rescaling preserves the line
spanned by this monomial.
The scalar changes with $t$, but the
rule is always $t_1^2t_2$.
We label this rule by the exponent
vector $(2,1,0,\ldots,0)$.

Now suppose a nonzero vector $v\in T^2$
also stays on its own line under every
rescaling. Write
$$
t\cdot v=\chi(t)v.
$$
The function $\chi$ records the scalar
for every choice of $t$.
Acting by $u$ and then by $t$ is the
same as acting by $tu$, so
$$
\chi(tu)v
=t\cdot(u\cdot v)
=\chi(u)\chi(t)v.
$$
Since $v\neq0$, we obtain
$$
\chi(tu)=\chi(t)\chi(u).
$$
The scalars are nonzero because the
action is invertible. Such an algebraic
homomorphism
$\chi:T\to\mathbb{C}^{\times}$
is called a \emph{character}.
Here this means a one-dimensional
representation; it agrees with the
trace character in dimension one.

Why do integer vectors appear?
The algebraic characters of $T$ are
precisely the Laurent monomials
$$
\chi_\alpha(t)=t^\alpha
=\prod_{i=1}^n t_i^{\alpha_i},
\qquad \alpha\in\mathbb{Z}^n.
$$
This classification is a standard fact;
see \cite[Section 4g and Chapter 12,
Sections a--d]{milne-ag-2022}.
The exponents are integers, and negative
ones are allowed since each $t_i$ is
nonzero. Thus $t$ specifies a
transformation, while $\alpha$ labels
a scaling rule. They belong to
different sets.

We group together all vectors with
the same scaling rule:
$$
T^2_\alpha=
\{v\in T^2:t\cdot v=t^\alpha v
\ \forall t\in T\}.
$$
This space can have dimension greater
than one: several independent vectors
can respond by the same scalar.
The theorem we are accepting says that
every vector is a unique finite sum of
vectors belonging to these spaces:
\begin{equation}
\label{equation-ac-weight-decomposition}
T^2=\bigoplus_{\alpha\in\mathbb{Z}^n}
T^2_\alpha.
\end{equation}
This is \cite[Theorem 12.12]
{milne-ag-2022}, applied to the
algebraic torus representation on $T^2$.
These are the pieces computed by AC.

The following comparison is only a bridge
to the weight decompositions we already
know from Lie algebras. It is not needed
for the deformation argument and may
be skipped.
To recognize the Lie algebra version,
put $t_i=e^{s h_i}$ and differentiate
at $s=0$. For $v\in T^2_\alpha$,
$$
t(s)\cdot v
=e^{s\sum_i\alpha_i h_i}v
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
\pi(h)v=\left(\sum_i\alpha_i h_i\right)v.
$$
Thus the Lie algebra weight is the
linear functional
$\lambda_\alpha(h)=\sum_i\alpha_i h_i$.
This is the simultaneous-eigenvector
condition from
Definition~\ref
{lie-algebras-definition-weight-space}.

For the graded projective problem we
use the total-degree-zero part:
$$
(T^2)_0=
\bigoplus_{\sum_i\alpha_i=0}T^2_\alpha.
$$
Indeed, setting all $t_i=c$ gives the
scalar $c^{\sum_i\alpha_i}$.
Total degree zero means that this
common rescaling acts trivially.

There is a second compatibility that
we also accept for now. Let $G$ be a
group of vertex permutations preserving
the simplicial complex.
Use the convention $g(x_i)=x_{g(i)}$.
Then
\begin{equation}
\label{equation-ac-weight-permutation}
g(T^2_\alpha)=T^2_{g\alpha},
\qquad
(g\alpha)_i=\alpha_{g^{-1}(i)}.
\end{equation}
This says that relabelling the vertices
also relabels the entries of the weight.
For example, swapping vertices 1 and 2
sends the space of weight
$(a,b,\alpha_3,\ldots,\alpha_n)$
to the space of weight
$(b,a,\alpha_3,\ldots,\alpha_n)$.
It need not preserve each weight space
separately.

The reason is that a permutation
conjugates independent rescaling to
independent rescaling with permuted
scalars. The induced actions on $T^2$
retain this compatibility.
Consequently we can organize the AC
pieces into $G$-orbits and study one
representative at a time.
Permutations fixing a weight can still
act nontrivially inside its weight
space; understanding that action will
be essential for finding invariant
obstruction classes.

\section{The dualizing sheaf of a sphere}
\label{section-sr-dualizing-sheaf}

The goal of this section is to explain
why the Stanley-Reisner scheme of a
triangulated sphere has trivial dualizing
sheaf, and why this passes to a
smoothing. The dualizing sheaf is
recalled in Algebraic Geometry, Section
\ref{algebraic-geometry-%
section-dualizing-sheaf}; the
Gorenstein property is recalled in
Commutative Algebra, Section
\ref{commutative-algebra-%
section-gorenstein-properties}. The
general theorems below are quoted from
\cite{stanley-cca}, \cite{BAYER19911} and
\cite{har}, with the place in each source.
The example of the tetrahedron is worked
out by hand.

\medskip\noindent
{\bf Links.}
Let $\mathcal{K}$ be a simplicial complex
and $\sigma$ a face. The link of $\sigma$
is the set of faces $\tau$ of
$\mathcal{K}$ such that
$$
\tau\cap\sigma=\emptyset,
\qquad
\tau\cup\sigma\in\mathcal{K}.
$$
It is again a simplicial complex,
written $\operatorname{lk}\sigma$. The
link of the empty face is $\mathcal{K}$
itself.

For example, let $\mathcal{K}$ be the
boundary of the tetrahedron: all proper
subsets of $\{0,1,2,3\}$. It is a
triangulation of the $2$-sphere.
\begin{enumerate}
\item The link of the vertex $\{0\}$
consists of the proper subsets of
$\{1,2,3\}$. It is the boundary of a
triangle: a circle.
\item The link of the edge $\{0,1\}$
consists of $\emptyset$, $\{2\}$ and
$\{3\}$. It is two points: a sphere of
dimension $0$.
\item The link of the triangle
$\{0,1,2\}$ consists of $\emptyset$
alone.
\end{enumerate}
The complex whose only face is the empty
set is counted as the sphere of
dimension $-1$. Its reduced homology is
$k$ in degree $-1$ and zero in all other
degrees.

\medskip\noindent
{\bf Links in a sphere.}
Suppose that $\mathcal{K}$ is a
triangulation of a sphere of dimension
$d$. Let $\sigma$ be a face with
$|\sigma|$ vertices. Then the link of
$\sigma$ has the reduced homology of a
sphere of dimension $d-|\sigma|$:
$$
\widetilde{H}_i
(\operatorname{lk}\sigma;k)\cong
\begin{cases}
k,& i=d-|\sigma|,\\
0,& i\neq d-|\sigma|.
\end{cases}
$$
The reason is that the homology of the link
of a face computes the local homology of the
sphere at a point of that face. In the
tetrahedron example it is visible in the list
above, with $d=2$. In general it follows from
two results quoted below: a triangulation of
a sphere is Gorenstein, and the displayed
condition characterizes the Gorenstein
complexes among those in which no vertex
belongs to all maximal faces \cite[Chapter
II, Theorem 5.1 and Corollary
5.2]{stanley-cca}. A triangulated sphere has
no such vertex: a complex with such a vertex
is a cone, hence contractible.

\medskip\noindent
{\bf Two theorems.}
The first theorem is Reisner's criterion
\cite{reisner-cm}, in the form of
\cite[Chapter II, Corollary
4.2]{stanley-cca}.

\begin{theorem}
\label{theorem-reisner-criterion}
The ring $A_{\mathcal{K}}$ is
Cohen--Macaulay if and only if, for
every face $\sigma$ of $\mathcal{K}$,
including the empty face, the reduced
homology of $\operatorname{lk}\sigma$
with coefficients in $k$ vanishes in all
degrees below the dimension of
$\operatorname{lk}\sigma$.
\end{theorem}

The second is due to Stanley and Hochster.

\begin{theorem}
\label{theorem-stanley-gorenstein-sphere}
Let $\mathcal{K}$ be a triangulation of a
sphere. Then $A_{\mathcal{K}}$ is Gorenstein,
and its canonical module satisfies
$$
\omega_{A_{\mathcal{K}}}\cong
A_{\mathcal{K}}
$$
as graded modules, with no shift of the
grading.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
The ring is Gorenstein by \cite[Chapter II,
Corollary 5.2]{stanley-cca}. The canonical
module of a Gorenstein graded ring is the
ring itself up to a shift of the grading
\cite[Chapter I, Theorem 12.5]{stanley-cca};
for face rings of orientable manifolds
without boundary this is restated in
\cite[Chapter II, Section 7]{stanley-cca}. It
remains to see that the shift is zero. This
is done below, in the paragraph on the shift.
\end{proof}

In the language of Commutative Algebra,
Theorem
\ref{commutative-algebra-%
theorem-graded-gorenstein},
the $a$-invariant is $0$.

\begin{corollary}
\label{corollary-sr-sphere-trivial-dualizing}
Let $\mathcal{K}$ be a triangulation of
a sphere of dimension $d\geq 1$, and let
$X$ be its Stanley-Reisner scheme. Then
$X$ is a Gorenstein projective scheme of
dimension $d$, and
$$
\omega_X\cong\mathcal{O}_X.
$$
\end{corollary}

\begin{proof}
By Theorem
\ref{theorem-stanley-gorenstein-sphere},
the ring $A_{\mathcal{K}}$ is Gorenstein
and its canonical module is
$A_{\mathcal{K}}$ with no shift. The
local rings of $X$ are obtained from
localizations of $A_{\mathcal{K}}$, and
localizations of a Gorenstein ring are
Gorenstein. So $X$ is Gorenstein. The
sheaf on $X$ associated with the
canonical module is the dualizing sheaf;
see the comparison with the canonical
module in Algebraic Geometry, Section
\ref{algebraic-geometry-%
section-dualizing-sheaf}. The sheaf
associated with $A_{\mathcal{K}}$ is
$\mathcal{O}_X$.
\end{proof}

The same conclusion holds more generally. If
$\mathcal{K}$ is a triangulation of a compact
manifold, then the canonical bundle of its
Stanley-Reisner scheme is trivial if and only
if the manifold is orientable \cite[Theorem
6.1]{BAYER19911}.

\medskip\noindent
{\bf The tetrahedron by hand.}
Let $\mathcal{K}$ be the boundary of the
tetrahedron. The only subset of
$\{0,1,2,3\}$ that is not a face is the
whole set. So
$$
I_{\mathcal{K}}=(x_0x_1x_2x_3),
\qquad
A_{\mathcal{K}}=
k[x_0,x_1,x_2,x_3]/(x_0x_1x_2x_3).
$$
The scheme $X$ is the union of the four
coordinate planes of $\mathbb{P}^3$. Put
$S=k[x_0,x_1,x_2,x_3]$ and
$g=x_0x_1x_2x_3$. A graded free
resolution of $A_{\mathcal{K}}$ is
$$
0\to S(-4)\xrightarrow{g}S\to
A_{\mathcal{K}}\to 0.
$$
The codimension is $1$, so the canonical
module is
$$
\omega_{A_{\mathcal{K}}}=
\operatorname{Ext}^1_S
(A_{\mathcal{K}},S(-4)).
$$
To compute it, apply
$\operatorname{Hom}_S(-,S(-4))$ to the
two free modules of the resolution. From
$S$ one gets $S(-4)$. From $S(-4)$ one
gets $S$. The resulting complex is
$$
S(-4)\xrightarrow{g}S,
$$
and $\operatorname{Ext}^1$ is the
cokernel of this map:
$$
\omega_{A_{\mathcal{K}}}\cong
S/(g)=A_{\mathcal{K}}.
$$
There is no shift. This agrees with
Theorem
\ref{theorem-stanley-gorenstein-sphere},
and with Algebraic Geometry, Corollary
\ref{algebraic-geometry-%
corollary-dualizing-hypersurface},
which gives
$\omega_X\cong\mathcal{O}_X(4-3-1)$.

\medskip\noindent
{\bf Where the sphere enters, and the shift.}
Here is why the canonical module is generated
in degree $0$. Let $n$ be the number of
vertices, so that $S$ has $n$ variables, and
let $d$ be the dimension of $\mathcal{K}$.
The ring $A_{\mathcal{K}}$ has dimension
$d+1$, so its codimension is $c=n-d-1$, and a
minimal graded free resolution of
$A_{\mathcal{K}}$ over $S$ has length $c$.
Hochster's formula \cite[Chapter II, Theorem
4.8]{stanley-cca} describes the generators of
its $i$-th module: those whose degree is the
product of the variables in a set $W$ of
vertices are counted by the dimension of
$$
\widetilde{H}_{|W|-i-1}
(\mathcal{K}_W;k).
$$
Here $\mathcal{K}_W$ is the complex of faces
of $\mathcal{K}$ contained in $W$. Take $i=c$
and let $W$ be the set of all vertices. Then
$\mathcal{K}_W=\mathcal{K}$ and the homology
group is
$$
\widetilde{H}_{d}(\mathcal{K};k)=k,
$$
spanned by the fundamental class of the
sphere. So the last module of the resolution
has a generator of degree $n$. Since the ring
is Gorenstein, the last module has rank one:
it is $S(-n)$. The canonical module is
computed by applying
$\operatorname{Hom}_S(-,S(-n))$ to the
resolution, as in the example of the
tetrahedron. The last module $S(-n)$ becomes
$S$, and the canonical module is a quotient
of it. So the canonical module is generated
in degree $0$, by the class dual to the
fundamental class, and
$$
\omega_{A_{\mathcal{K}}}\cong A_{\mathcal{K}}
$$
with no shift. This is the argument sketched
in the proof of \cite[Theorem
6.1]{BAYER19911}. In the tetrahedron, $n=4$
and the last module is $S(-4)$.

There is a second way to see that the shift
is zero. For a Gorenstein graded ring the
shift is the degree of the Hilbert series as
a rational function \cite[Chapter I, Theorem
12.6 and the remark after it]{stanley-cca}.
The monomials of $A_{\mathcal{K}}$ whose
variables are exactly the vertices of a face
$\sigma$ contribute
$$
\left(\frac{t}{1-t}\right)^{|\sigma|}
$$
to the Hilbert series, so the series is the
sum of these terms over all faces, the empty
face included. As $t$ goes to infinity each
term tends to $(-1)^{|\sigma|}$. The sum of
these signs over all faces is minus the
reduced Euler characteristic of
$\mathcal{K}$, which for a sphere of
dimension $d$ is $(-1)^{d+1}$. This is not
zero, so the Hilbert series has degree $0$.

\medskip\noindent
{\bf Smoothings.}
Let $\mathcal{K}$ be a triangulation of a
compact manifold, with Stanley-Reisner scheme
$X$. The cohomology of the structure sheaf of
$X$ is the cohomology of $\mathcal{K}$: for
$i>0$,
$$
H^i(X,\mathcal{O}_X)\cong
H^i(\mathcal{K};k).
$$
This is stated in \cite[Section
6]{BAYER19911}, where it is attributed to
Reisner and to Hochster and Roberts. For a
sphere of dimension $d$ the right hand side
is $k$ for $i=d$ and zero for $0<i<d$. Also
$H^0(X,\mathcal{O}_X)=k$, because
$A_{\mathcal{K}}$ is Cohen--Macaulay of
dimension at least $2$ and its part of degree
$0$ is $k$.

The following theorem is \cite[Theorem
0.2.1]{Fausk} for $d=3$. I have not read the
proof there; the proof below follows the two
steps I noted from it: the dualizing sheaf of
$X$ is trivial, and this passes to nearby
fibres.

\begin{theorem}
\label{theorem-fausk-021}
Let $\mathcal{K}$ be a triangulation of a
sphere of dimension $d\geq 2$, with
Stanley-Reisner scheme $X$. Let $C$ be a
smooth curve with a point $0$, and let
$$
f:\mathcal{X}\to C
$$
be a flat projective morphism whose fibre
over $0$ is $X$. Then there is an open
neighbourhood $V$ of $0$ in $C$ such that
every smooth fibre $Y$ over a point of $V$ is
connected and satisfies
$$
\omega_Y\cong\mathcal{O}_Y,
\qquad
H^i(Y,\mathcal{O}_Y)=0
$$
for $0<i<d$. For $d=2$ such a fibre is a K3
surface. For $d=3$ it is a Calabi--Yau
threefold.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
The proof has four steps.

Step 1: the special fibre. By Corollary
\ref{corollary-sr-sphere-trivial-dualizing},
the scheme $X$ is Gorenstein and
$\omega_X\cong\mathcal{O}_X$. This is where
the sphere is used.

Step 2: vanishing on the special fibre. By
the formula above, $H^i(X,\mathcal{O}_X)=0$
for $0<i<d$. In particular
$H^1(X,\mathcal{O}_X)=0$, because $d\geq 2$.

Step 3: the dualizing sheaf of nearby fibres.
By Steps 1 and 2 the hypotheses of Algebraic
Geometry, Proposition
\ref{algebraic-geometry-%
proposition-trivial-dualizing-nearby}
hold. So there is a neighbourhood $V$ of $0$
such that every fibre $Y$ over a point of $V$
has $\omega_Y\cong\mathcal{O}_Y$. The proof
of that proposition extends a nowhere
vanishing section of $\omega_X$ to the nearby
fibres; the extension exists because
$H^1(X,\omega_X)=0$. If $Y$ is smooth,
$\omega_Y$ is its canonical bundle.

Step 4: semicontinuity. The dimension of
$H^i$ of the structure sheaf of the fibre is
an upper semicontinuous function on $C$
\cite[III, Theorem 12.8]{har}. After making
$V$ smaller, for every fibre $Y$ over a point
of $V$ the dimension of
$H^i(Y,\mathcal{O}_Y)$ is at most that of
$H^i(X,\mathcal{O}_X)$. For $0<i<d$ this
gives zero. For $i=0$ it gives that
$H^0(Y,\mathcal{O}_Y)$ has dimension at most
$1$, so $Y$ is connected.
\end{proof}

The proposition used in Step 3 rests on one
fact about the relative dualizing sheaf of a
family that I have not found in a reference
in the form needed; see the discussion before
Algebraic Geometry, Proposition
\ref{algebraic-geometry-%
proposition-trivial-dualizing-nearby}.

For $d=1$ the hypothesis on $H^1$ fails.
The boundary of a triangle gives three
lines in the plane, and
$H^1(X,\mathcal{O}_X)=k$. The conclusion
still holds there, for a different
reason: a plane cubic has trivial
dualizing sheaf because of its degree,
by Algebraic Geometry, Corollary
\ref{algebraic-geometry-%
corollary-dualizing-hypersurface}.

\section{The Grünbaum-Sreedharan sphere}
\label{section-grunbaum-sphere}

\noindent
Let $\mathcal{M}$ be the triangulation of the
sphere of dimension $3$ with $8$ vertices and
$20$ tetrahedra found by Grünbaum and
Sreedharan \cite{gru}. It is not the boundary
of a convex polytope; see Polytopes, Section
\ref{polytopes-section-grunbaum-sreedharan-sphere}.
Its Stanley-Reisner ideal is generated by the
sixteen cubic monomials
$$
\begin{aligned}
x_6x_7x_8,
x_4x_6x_8, x_3x_7x_8, x_3x_5x_7, x_3x_4x_8,
x_2x_7x_8, \\ 
x_2x_5x_7, x_2x_5x_6,
x_2x_4x_7, x_2x_4x_6, x_1x_4x_6,\\ 
x_1x_4x_5,
x_1x_3x_8, x_1x_3x_6, x_1x_3x_5, x_1x_2x_5
\end{aligned}
$$
as computed in gfan and in GAP, and as shown
in \cite{luk1}. So its Stanley-Reisner scheme
is a union of $20$ copies of $\mathbb{P}^3$
in $\mathbb{P}^7$, one for each tetrahedron.
By Theorem \ref{theorem-fausk-021}, a smooth
fibre near it in a flat projective family is
a Calabi--Yau threefold.

\end{itemize}

\medskip\noindent
Now let's go over the details of Calla's construction.
In \cite{KuhnelBanchoff1983} it was shown that there is a 9-vertex
triangulation $\Sigma$ of $\mathbb{C}P^2$.
This triangulation has 36 4-simplices.
Notice that the definition of SR scheme for Calla
might be different from ours: the {\it Stanley-Reisner variety}
of $\Sigma$ is the union of 36 copies of $\mathbb{P}^4$,
one for each 4-simplex of $\Sigma$.
Its ideal is generated by 36 quartics.

Part 1. We denote by $\Sigma$ the
9 vertex triangulation of  $\mathbb{P}^2$,
and $\text{SR}_{36}$ its corresponding
Stanley-Reisner scheme.

\begin{enumerate}

\item Blow up 9 coordinate points in $\mathbb{P}^8$ 
$(1:0:\ldots:0),\ldots,(0:\ldots:0:1)$.
(Recall that by the construction of the
Stanley-Reisner scheme associated to
a simplicial complex, in this case with 9
vertices, the vertices of the SR scheme
are precisely the coordinate points of $\mathbb{P}^8$.)
This introduces 9 copies of $\mathbb{P}^7$.
Proper transform of  $\text{SR}_{36}$
intersects $\mathbb{P}^7$ in a union
of  20 $\mathbb{P}^3$s, denoted $T_{20}$.
This does not depend on which copy of $\mathbb{P}^7$ 
we look at.

As a note, recall that the proper
transform of a subvariety $Y \subset X$ 
is defined as the {\it closure} of
$\pi^{-1}(Y\setminus Z)$ where
the blow up occurs at $Z$. In this case
$Z$ is the 9 vertices and $Y=\text{SR}_{36}$.
Calla claims that
that the closure of the proper
transform of $\text{SR}_{36}$ 
intersects each of the exceptional divisors
(one $\mathbb{P}^7$ for every vertex)
in a union of 20 $\mathbb{P}^3$ 
regardless of which exceptional divisor we
look at. We call that intersection $T_{20}$,
and it is inside $\mathbb{P}^7$.

\item Now we turn to modify $T_{20}$ 
to satisfy Friedman conditions.
Blow up 8 coordinate points in $\mathbb{P}^7$.
The proper transform of $T_{20}$ intersects
$\mathbb{P}^6$ in a union of 10 $\mathbb{P}^2$ s,
which we call $S_{10}$.
This {\bf does} depend on which copy of
$\mathbb{P}^7$ and $\mathbb{P}^6$ 
we are looking at!
$S_{10}$ is a Stanley-Reisner variety
and in fact is a triangulation of a sphere.
(A polyhedron!?)

\item Now we proceed to smooth $S_{10}$.
It has seven vertices, which we blow up,
introducing seven copies of $\mathbb{P}^5$.
Each of the $\mathbb{P}^2$ s becomes
a del Pezzo 6.
Looking at the picture we
see that there are vertices with
different valency. For each vertex
where $d$ $\mathbb{P}^2$s meet,
we glue in a del Pezzo surface of 
degree $d$. 
We get $\tilde{S}_{10}$, which is a 
simple normal crossing surface,
with trivial canonical bundle
and satisfies $d$-stability 
Friedman conditions.

Then there exists a Kulikov model
of type III degenerations of K3s
(of degree $10$ in $\mathbb{P}^7$)
with special fiber $\tilde{S}_{10}$.
(This is local smoothing over a
small disc, does not give a projective
family of K3s.)
\end{enumerate}

{\bf Key move.}
$S_{10}$ is a triangulation of a sphere.
Blow up the singularities and look
how does the proper transform of $S_{10}$ 
intersect the exceptional divisors.
To produce a smoothing,
we glue in del Pezzo surfaces
at the exceptional divisors.

Now we shall do the same for $T_{20}$.
(Which is still not the blow up
of $\text{SR}_{36}$, but the intersection
of the proper transform of $\text{SR}_{36}$ 
with any of the 9 exceptional divisors.)
We will glue in our projecive degeneration of K3s.
Notice that Friedman only gave us existence
of local model, so we must find equations.

For now let's just say that there's a bunch of procedures and
the resulting $\tilde{T}_{20}$ is a normal crossing and 
has trivial canonical bundle.
Then we can run smoothing procedure and find equations of family
of CY3s in $\mathbb{P}^7$ by noting that $T_{20} \subset 10 \mathbb{P}^4$s.
We should find generators of the ideal of
the variety $10\mathbb{P}^4$
among minors of $3\times 5$ matrix, similarly to surface case.

\section{Moduli space of generalized Kummer type fourfolds}
\label{section-moduli-kummer}

\noindent
According to Calla's talk,
the whole point of constructing the smoothing
of $\text{SR}(\mathcal{M})$ is that it
should help us to smooth $\mathbb{C}P^2_9$,
which in turn should lie in the boundary
of the moduli space of generalized Kummer type fourfolds
with polarization degree 2.

Here's a quote from Calla's talk:
``Aim: construct a type III
degeneration of varieties of generalized
Kummer type 4-folds with polarization degree 2,
by starting from a given special fibre
and constructing a smoothing.''

\medskip\noindent
Let's address the following question.
Even if there exists a smoothing of $\text{SR}(\mathbb{C}P^2_9)$,
why would it be a generalized Kummer variety?

First recall Theorem \ref{theorem-fausk-021}:
a smoothing, if it exists, of the
Stanley-Reisner scheme of a triangulation of
the 3-sphere yields a Calabi-Yau 3-fold.

\noindent
Sure, that says that if I smooth $\text{SR}(M)$ 
then I have a CY 3fold. That's only the vertex-figure
of $\mathbb{C}P^2_9$.
Still, this looks far from saying that a smoothing
{\it of $\mathbb{C}P^2_9$} is a generalized Kummer 4fold.
