\section{Adding amplitudes}
Let $a$ and $b$ be the complex probability
amplitudes at one detector for the two
indistinguishable paths. With both paths
available the detection probability is
$$
|a+b|^2=|a|^2+|b|^2
+2\operatorname{Re}(\bar a b).
$$
At a point where $b=-a$ the probability
vanishes, although either path alone could
contribute. If a detector leaves
distinguishable records of which slit the
electron crossed, the interference term
disappears when those records are ignored.
The experiment makes the phase of an
amplitude observable through its relation to
another amplitude.

See \cite[Vol. III, Sections 1-4 to
1-7]{feynman-lectures}.

\section{Schr\"odinger evolution
and the Dirac rule}
\label{section-dirac-rule}

The amplitudes at a fixed time are described
by one unit vector $\psi(t)$ in a complex
Hilbert space $\mathcal H$ as in Definition
\ref{functional-analysis-%
definition-hilbert-space}.
This vector represents a pure state.
Multiplying it by a common phase leaves all
measurement probabilities unchanged.

The Hamiltonian $H_q$ is the self-adjoint
operator that represents energy. In a finite
orthonormal basis it is a Hermitian matrix,
$\psi(t)$ is a column vector, and
$H_q\psi(t)$ is their matrix-vector product.
Given the initial state $\psi(0)$, the
Schr\"odinger equation specifies its
velocity at each time.
$$
i\hbar\dot\psi(t)=H_q\psi(t).
$$
Here $\hbar$ is the reduced Planck constant.
For an unbounded Hamiltonian this derivative
in $\mathcal H$ requires the state to lie in
the operator domain.

How does one solve this equation? Suppose
$H_q$ is independent of time and $u$ is a
unit vector satisfying $H_qu=Eu$. Its energy
$E$ is real. Start the system at $u$ and
write $\psi(t)=c(t)u$. Substitution gives
$$
i\hbar c'(t)=Ec(t),\qquad c(0)=1.
$$
Thus $\psi(t)=e^{-iEt/\hbar}u$. An energy
eigenstate acquires only a common phase.
For an initial state $au+bv$ with energies
$E_u,E_v$, linearity instead gives
$$
\psi(t)=ae^{-iE_ut/\hbar}u
       +be^{-iE_vt/\hbar}v.
$$
If the energies differ, the relative phase
changes. This is how the evolution can
change interference probabilities. See
\cite[Sections 3.7.1--3.7.3]{hallq}.

For an observable $A$ with no explicit time
dependence, its expected value in this state
is the number
$$
\left\langle{} A \right\rangle{}_\psi
=\left\langle{} A\psi,\psi \right\rangle{}.
$$
Take the inner product linear in its first
argument. In finite dimensions, the product
rule and the Schr\"odinger equation give
$$
\begin{aligned}
\frac{d}{dt}
\left\langle{} A \right\rangle{}_\psi
&=\left\langle{} A\dot\psi,\psi
\right\rangle{}
 +\left\langle{} A\psi,\dot\psi
 \right\rangle{}\\
&=\frac{1}{i\hbar}\bigl(
  \left\langle{} AH_q\psi,\psi
  \right\rangle{}
 -\left\langle{} A\psi,H_q\psi
 \right\rangle{}\bigr)\\
&=\frac{1}{i\hbar}
  \left\langle{} [A,H_q]\psi,\psi
\right\rangle{} .
\end{aligned}
$$
The minus sign comes from conjugating
$1/(i\hbar)$ in the second argument.
Self-adjointness moves $H_q$ to the first
argument in the last line. For unbounded
operators the same calculation applies on a
common invariant domain with differentiable
expectations and the product rule.

Along a classical trajectory the rate of
change of $f$ is $\{f,H\}$, by Definition
\ref{symplectic-geometry-%
definition-poisson-bracket}.
The commutator above plays the same role.
This motivates the Dirac rule in Definition
\ref{symplectic-geometry-%
definition-dirac-prequantization}.
